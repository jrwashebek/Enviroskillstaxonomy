## Executive summary: five things leaders need to know

Environmental work is changing for four reasons at once. Federal capacity contracted sharply in 2025–26. Rules and responsibilities are moving between levels of government. Fire and climate workloads are rising. AI and sensing are reaching both desk work and field work. The first audience for this report is federal and state workforce, training and HR leaders; NGOs, employers and educators will find most of it applies to them too.

**1. The largest measured change in federal environmental jobs is a contraction in capacity, not AI.** Between September 2024 and January 2026 the federal civilian workforce fell 12%, from 2,313,216 to 2,035,344 employees. The Forest Service and the National Park Service each lost more than a quarter of their staff, and the number of federal park rangers fell 34.1% [2]. EPA ended January 2026 with 12,849 employees, about 24% fewer than a year earlier [3]. Entry pipelines thinned at the same time. The Forest Service hired no non-fire seasonal staff in 2025 [4], and employees under 30 made up 7.9% of the federal workforce in January 2026 [2]. These changes followed personnel, hiring and grant decisions: deferred resignations and retirements [3], hiring controls [28] and grant terminations [34]. Appropriations fell far less than staffing [32]. AI's employment effect in these occupations has not been measured, and in federal headcount data the contraction would swamp any AI signal [J; 6].

**2. AI exposure varies widely across environmental work, and the evidence on actual use is still thin.** On the most widely used capability measure, the analytical, regulatory and planning occupations EMST tracks fall in the top fifth of all occupations, most field and operations occupations fall in or near the bottom third, and forestry and range occupations sit in between [5, 6]. Usage data come from consumer and enterprise platforms that see little government or field work, and on Microsoft's applicability measure most environmental occupations score below their capability exposure (a comparison of ranks, not of absolute use) [7, 6]. In eleven federal environmental units (nine agencies and bureaus and two USDA mission areas), classical machine learning for science work is the largest type of registered AI: 232 of the 329 use cases that report a type. Of the 347 use cases that report a development stage, 61 are deployed, and none of the five in NEPA review or permitting is deployed [8]. Commercial AI tools are licensed at 10,000 or more users in EPA (drafting, summarizing and search tools), the Department of the Interior (a writing tool) and USDA (a search and knowledge-retrieval assistant): available at workforce scale, which is not the same as used [8]. EMST's labor-market review found no study that measures AI's employment effects in environmental occupations [J; 6].

**3. Policy is moving faster than technology, and AI's effect on roles is still a hypothesis.** In 2025–26, rules and institutions changed review, authorization, hiring and disclosure work more visibly than any technology did [J; 11, 12, 13, 14]. Capability evidence suggests that desk tasks such as first drafts, compiling, comment coding and routine mapping can be done faster with AI, and that specifying, checking, signing and defending work would become more central; field work is expected to move toward servicing instruments and producing ground truth. Where AI touches the corridor roles EMST profiles first, the expected change is a shift in task mix rather than elimination. EMST's draft role profiles are built on that expectation, but EMST found no measurement of it in an environmental workplace [J; 6, 9, 10]. Meanwhile reorganization and attrition are removing positions now (point 1). EMST expects new work to appear in tasks before it appears in job titles or occupational series [J; 6].

**4. The entry path is the risk to watch. It is a hypothesis with a mechanism, not yet a finding.** Formation (the supervised repetition that turns a novice into a practitioner) is squeezed from two directions. In federal land agencies and conservation corps, personnel, hiring and grant decisions are removing entry jobs now. AI and sensors could compress the tasks juniors learned on; their actual use inside agencies is unmeasured. Meanwhile long-tenured staff are leaving: the roughly 2,000 EPA employees who left at the end of 2025, mostly through deferred-resignation retirements, had a median 30.3 years of service, against 10.8 years for those who stayed [3]. If hiring resumes without entry tasks redesigned for learning, a temporary contraction could become a permanent break in the career ladder [S]. Section 5 sets out the mechanism, the evidence for each link and what would settle the question.

**5. Some moves look right in every future EMST considered, and they can start now.** EMST's foresight analysis, a desk exercise drafted by an AI agent and not yet reviewed by a human panel, assessed workforce strategies against four scenarios for 2026–2035. The scenarios are built on two uncertainties: whether public stewardship capacity is rebuilt, and whether AI proves trustworthy in high-stakes environmental decisions [15]. It judged eight moves robust across all four: protect a minimum entry cohort and the tasks it learns on; capture tacit knowledge now; make validating AI output a requirement for anyone who uses it; treat ground truth as infrastructure; build skills-based mobility channels between employers; strengthen agencies' ability to buy technology as informed buyers; invest in the craft of intergovernmental agreements; and set up horizon scanning. Other moves should wait for named triggers. The strategy it judged most fragile is using AI to cut entry roles first. That verdict partly follows from how the scenarios are defined, since one of them assumes exactly that cut followed by AI under-delivering [J; 15].

**What EMST v2 offers.** EMST v2 describes environmental work as tasks, competencies and role profiles, each carrying a blend of three crafts: field craft, data craft and statecraft. Evidence is labeled, uses are tiered, and no personal data is held [16, 17, 18]. The 2.0 release candidate supports describing and developing (workforce analysis, training, development plans and curriculum), not selecting people. Human validation (reliability studies, a pre-registered Delphi panel and job analysis with agencies that agree to take part) is planned from 2027 [19, 20]. Section 7 shows how a federal HR specialist, a state training officer, an NGO program lead and an educator could use it from the first day. EMST asks readers to try the draft profiles on their own roles, tell EMST where they are wrong, and consider the 2027 NGO pilot; section 8.5 says how to take part and gives the contact route.

## 1. What is changing and why now

Five forces act on environmental work at once. Each moves on its own clock, so leaders who watch only one will misread the others.

### 1.1 AI: broad capability, uneven deployment

General-purpose AI is now available to large parts of the federal workforce. Commercial drafting, summarizing, writing and search tools are licensed at 10,000 or more users in each of the three main federal environmental departments (Figure F04) [8]. Capability is broadest in information work. In Microsoft's analysis of 200,000 conversations on its consumer Bing Copilot service, gathering, writing and communicating were the most common and most successful AI-assisted activities [7]. Consumer conversations say little about government work. Section 2 shows that deployment inside agencies, and evidence on outcomes, lag well behind capability.

### 1.2 The 2025–26 contraction in federal capacity

**Headcount.** The federal civilian workforce fell from 2,313,216 in September 2024 to 2,035,344 in January 2026, a net drop of about 278,000 or 12%, according to the Partnership for Public Service's (PPS) analysis of OPM data; the PPS count excludes the Postal Service, the Foreign Service and much of the intelligence community [2]. The OPM Director's own tally put 2025 departures at about 317,000; that figure counts departures rather than the net change, over a different period and with different definitions, so the two should not be combined [25]. Environmental agencies were hit harder than average (Figure F01):

- The Forest Service and the National Park Service each lost more than a quarter of their employees between September 2024 and January 2026, and federal park rangers fell 34.1% [2].
- EPA had 12,849 employees at the end of January 2026, more than 4,000 (24%) fewer than a year earlier [3].
- The Natural Resources Conservation Service was down about 23.8% from September 2024 to November 2025 [27].
- Separately, USDA Inspector General data, as reported in the press, put the Forest Service's workforce loss in 2025 at 16%. That figure covers a different period and base from the PPS figure and should not be compared with it; EMST has not read the Inspector General document itself [26].

**Hiring rules.** Under Executive Order 14356 (15 October 2025), each agency's Strategic Hiring Committee approves the creation or filling of every vacancy, and agencies file annual staffing plans with quarterly updates [28]. Federal posting volume therefore reflects policy decisions more than labor demand, and EMST treats it as a policy signal [24].

**Reorganization.** Interior set up the U.S. Wildland Fire Service on 12 January 2026, unifying the fire programs of four Interior bureaus with the Office of Wildland Fire and the Office of Aviation Services; Interior states that it employs 5,780 federal wildland fire personnel a year. Forest Service fire was not moved [29]. In March and April 2026 the Forest Service announced that it will move its headquarters to Salt Lake City, close all nine regional offices in favor of a state-based model with 15 state directors, and close 57 of its 77 research facilities, with relocations running into 2027 [30]. Reporting on the reorganization describes it as leaning heavily on states, and foresters quoted in that reporting call Good Neighbor Authority a critical tool [31].

**Appropriations and staffing moved differently.** Congress enacted FY2026 Interior–Environment appropriations of $42.56 billion on 23 January 2026, 1.9% below FY2025 and far above the President's request of $26.85 billion [32]. Most of the staffing decline described above came before that bill, during 2025, and the sources tie it to personnel and hiring actions: deferred resignations and retirements [3], hiring controls, including Strategic Hiring Committee approval of every vacancy from October 2025 [28], and grant terminations [34]. The operative uncertainty is therefore public stewardship *capacity* (people, authority, coordination and entry pipelines) rather than appropriated dollars [J; 15].

**Entry pipelines (Figure F08).**

- The Forest Service hired no non-fire seasonal staff in 2025; a congressional letter of 18 March 2026, relaying agency statements, gives budget constraints as the reason for that decision. The same letter reports that on 12 February 2026 the agency committed to hiring 2,000 seasonals for 2026 [4].
- On the Conservation Job Board, postings fell 29.4% year over year for March–September 2025; paid internships fell 43.8% and AmeriCorps positions 42%. These figures cover one board, not the whole conservation labor market [33].
- In April 2025 AmeriCorps terminated nearly $400 million in grants, hitting conservation corps. A federal court enjoined the terminations in the 24 plaintiff states and DC on 5 June 2025, and the programs there were reinstated [34]. In July 2025 AmeriCorps also told grantees whose grants had been terminated on 25 April 2025, including programs in states that had not joined the litigation, that they were eligible for reinstatement; how many took it up is not documented [111]. An 11 September 2026 settlement with 23 state attorneys general and two governors covers FY2026 in those jurisdictions only; it does not cover programs elsewhere. The litigation is stayed until 1 February 2027 [34]. Corps leads should check whether their state is in the coalition.
- The share of federal employees under 30 fell from 8.9% to 7.9% [2].
- Forest Service trail miles maintained fell 22% in 2025. A congressional letter citing the agency's own status report lists the skills lost: equipment operation, traditional masonry, volunteer coordination and project management [35].

**Knowledge leaving at the top.** The roughly 2,000 EPA employees who left at the end of 2025, mostly through deferred-resignation retirements, had a median 30.3 years of service; the staff who remained had a median of 10.8 years [3].

**People moving to states.** Several states recruited displaced federal staff. In February 2025 New York began recruiting laid-off federal workers into state jobs [36]. Pennsylvania's Office of Administration has identified federal job titles whose experience may count as equivalent for certain Commonwealth titles, and reports 1,350 hires with federal work experience since Executive Order 2025-01 (March 2025) [37]. EMST judges that work is moving between employers faster than the people who know how to do it [J; 38].

### 1.3 Policy oscillation and devolution

Rules that shape environmental review, authorization and selection changed substantially in 2025–26 [J; 39]. EMST records these changes as dated, sourced instruments, so that what each role requires can change when the rules do (Figure F09) [40, 24]. Several of the items below are moving: each is stated as of 23 October 2026, with the date after which it must be re-checked.

**Environmental review.**

- CEQ's government-wide NEPA regulations were removed by an interim final rule effective 11 April 2025, adopted as final effective 8 January 2026; each department now follows its own procedures [11].
- Interior's final rule of 24 February 2026 keeps a slim 43 CFR part 46 and moves most procedures into the DOI NEPA Handbook [11]. USDA consolidated its procedures in 7 CFR part 1b, effective 3 April 2026, and rescinded the Forest Service's 36 CFR part 220 [11].
- In *Seven County Infrastructure Coalition v. Eagle County* (29 May 2025), the Supreme Court's judgment was 8–0, with a five-justice majority opinion holding that courts owe agencies substantial deference on the scope of NEPA review [12].
- Since July 2025 a project sponsor can pay a fee (125% of the anticipated cost, per counsel's reading of the statute) for deadlines of 180 days for an environmental assessment and one year for an environmental impact statement. No public count of projects using it was found [41].
- The SPEED Act (H.R. 4776) passed the House 221–196 on 18 December 2025; it is pending in the Senate Environment and Public Works Committee and is not law [42, 112]. A separate bipartisan Senate permitting bill, S. 5653, was unveiled on 30 September 2026, and it is a different bill from the SPEED Act. As of 23 October 2026 no committee markup or floor vote had been reported, and counsel expected floor consideration in early November (to be re-checked after 15 November 2026) [43].
- The Forest Service's revised project-level objection rule was published as final on 13 October 2026 and is scheduled to take effect on 12 November 2026 (as of 23 October 2026; to be re-checked after 12 November 2026) [44].

For the workforce, review work is shifting from following one government-wide set of rules to exercising judgment that must be justified in the record, under procedures that differ by agency [J; 39].

**Scope of federal regulation.**

- The rescission of the Endangered Species Act's regulatory definition of "harm" was finalized on 14 July 2026 and took effect on 14 September 2026; suits were filed in mid-July 2026 [45].
- EPA's rescission of the 2009 greenhouse gas endangerment finding and federal vehicle standards took effect on 20 April 2026; petitions for review are pending in the D.C. Circuit [46].
- There is no final "waters of the United States" rule: a proposal of 20 November 2025 was followed by a supplemental proposal published on 9 September 2026 [47].
- The Advisory Council on Historic Preservation voted on 24 July 2026 to advance a rewrite of its Section 106 regulations. The draft is under review at OMB's Office of Information and Regulatory Affairs (OIRA) and had not been published as of 9 October 2026 (to be re-checked after the Council's 10 November 2026 meeting) [48].

**Legal basis now varies by place.** Three federal executive orders directed analysis of the cumulative burdens and distributional effects of environmental decisions: EO 12898 (Federal Actions to Address Environmental Justice in Minority Populations and Low-Income Populations), EO 14096 (Revitalizing Our Nation's Commitment to Environmental Justice for All) and the relevant parts of EO 14008 (Tackling the Climate Crisis at Home and Abroad). All three were revoked in January 2025, and the Congressional Research Service notes that revocation does not change EPA's statutory responsibilities [49]. New Jersey and New York require such analysis in permitting. The rules implementing New Jersey's Environmental Justice Law were affirmed by the Appellate Division on 5 January 2026 [50]; the New Jersey Supreme Court granted certification in July 2026, and the rules remain in effect while that appeal is pending [113]. New York's Environmental Justice Siting Law applies disproportionate-burden review to applicable permits, with related environmental review amendments in effect from 12 June 2026 [51]. EMST names every competency by the work performed, keeps it wherever the evidence supports it, and computes where it currently has a legal basis from the status of the instruments. The same item can therefore show "no current basis" in a federal view and "required basis" in a state view [40].

**Selection and hiring law.**

- OPM's interim final rule of 31 July 2026 removed references to the Uniform Guidelines on Employee Selection Procedures (UGESP) from OPM's hiring regulations. 5 CFR 300.103 still requires each employment practice to rest on a job analysis and a rational relationship to performance [13]. UGESP itself remains codified at 29 CFR part 1607 [52].
- The Office of Federal Contract Compliance Programs' rescission of its Executive Order 11246 regulations, including 41 CFR part 60-3 to the extent it was codified for that order, was published on 21 August 2026 and is scheduled to take effect on 26 October 2026 (as of 23 October 2026; to be re-checked after 26 October 2026) [53].
- Colorado's 2024 AI statute never took effect as written. SB 26-189, signed on 14 May 2026, replaces it from 1 January 2027 with a notice-and-disclosure regime for automated decision tools used in consequential decisions, including employment [54]. The Attorney General released interim draft rules on 6 October 2026, which also implement the state's chatbot safety act, with comments and a hearing scheduled for 26 October (as of 23 October 2026; to be re-checked after 26 October 2026) [55].

EMST's selection-adjacent materials therefore rest on job analysis and job-relatedness, and EMST never describes them as meeting UGESP [19].

**Devolution through agreements.** Delivery is moving to states, counties and partners through agreements rather than plans. The Congressional Research Service, citing Forest Service data, reports 540 Forest Service Good Neighbor agreements in FY2025, most of them with states; the Bureau of Land Management does not report comparable counts [56]. EMST judges that statecraft demand is growing in state, local and corporate venues and in agreement-making, even as some federal regulatory scope narrows [J; 39].

### 1.4 Fire and climate: a rising workload that every scenario expects

As of 11 October 2026 the 2026 fire season had reached 63,884 fires and 9.0 million acres burned, 133% and 145% of the 2016–2025 average [57]. Fire is the one workload that every EMST scenario treats as rising [15]. The fire workforce is being restructured at the same time. Interior's new fire service is in place [29], and aligned federal qualification standards take effect in January 2027; the alignment is between U.S. Wildland Fire Service and Forest Service standards [58]. NWCG separately updated its position-qualification standard (PMS 310-1) on 23 January 2026 [58]. The Forest Service had 10,496 wildland firefighters on board on 11 May 2026, against a goal of 11,300 by mid-July [26].

Technology is already reshaping fire operations. In FY2024 Forest Service drones flew 4,536 hours, 78% more than in FY2023, and aerial ignition from drones treated 188,986 acres [59]. Flying is mostly a secondary duty: the agency's national UAS program manager has said that 99% of its trained pilots are collateral-duty pilots, whose primary job is something else [60]. BLS projects 4% growth for firefighters from 2025 to 2035, but does not count wildland firefighters separately [61].

### 1.5 Markets and disclosure: compliance-grade data work

Disclosure rules are reshaping private environmental demand:

- California's Office of Administrative Law approved CARB's SB 253 regulation on 12 October 2026. First Scope 1 and 2 reports are due on 10 November 2026 from U.S. entities with more than $1 billion in annual revenue doing business in California, and Scope 3 reporting begins in 2027. SB 261 remains enjoined (as of 23 October 2026; to be re-checked after 10 November 2026) [14].
- The EU's Omnibus I directive entered into force on 18 March 2026. It narrows CSRD to undertakings with more than 1,000 employees and more than €450 million in turnover, with transposition due by 19 March 2027 [14].

Among 500+ sustainability professionals at firms with $1 billion or more in revenue, 46% said their company had increased sustainability headcount and budgets over two years, against 25% reporting cuts, and 58% gave compliance a higher priority [62]. Green skills keep spreading into ordinary jobs: in LinkedIn's December 2025 stocktake, green hiring grew about 8% against about 4% growth in green skills over two years, and about half of green-skilled hires went into job titles not labeled green (the report gives both 51% and 53%) [63]. Clean energy, by contrast, recorded a net loss of 36,949 U.S. jobs in 2025 in E2's tally, as reported by the trade press [64].

Moving from public to private work carries a pay friction that mobility tools must show honestly. In BLS wage data for May 2025, the median environmental scientist earned:

| Employer | Median annual pay |
|---|---|
| Federal government | $116,110 |
| Engineering services | $83,500 |
| Local government | $81,580 |
| State government | $80,500 |
| Consulting | $79,320 |

Source: [65]. The comparison is compositional, because federal staff are older and more senior on average. Still, a move from federal service to consulting is often a pay cut at the median unless the mover takes on sign-off or revenue responsibility [J; 66].

## 2. What the evidence says about AI and environmental work

### 2.1 Five kinds of evidence, five different questions

Public debate often treats "AI exposure" as one number. It is at least five different measurements, and each fails in a different way for environmental work [J; 6].

| Question | Best current evidence | How well it fits environmental work |
|---|---|---|
| Could AI do this task? | Capability indices built on O*NET tasks (Eloundou et al.; the "theoretical" half of the BLS categories) | Good task coverage. Blind to robotics and to sensing (drones, acoustic recorders, camera traps, eDNA), which matter most in the field |
| Is AI used for it? | Usage indices (Anthropic Economic Index; Microsoft "Working with AI") | Platforms see little government or field work; "not observed" does not mean "not used" |
| Has the employer deployed it? | OMB's Federal Agency AI Use Case Inventory | Direct evidence for federal agencies; nothing comparable for most states |
| Did employment change? | Payroll and administrative-data studies | EMST found none with an environmental cut; the main payroll dataset under-covers government |
| What will employers ask for? | Postings, position descriptions, land management plans | Usable, but EMST has not yet taken a baseline of public postings |

### 2.2 Exposure: a gradient, not one number

The clearest pattern in the capability data is that environmental work is not one thing. In EMST's analysis of the Eloundou et al. task labels, environmental scientists, climate change policy analysts, sustainability specialists, planners, hydrologists and compliance officers fall in the top fifth of all occupations on exposure to language models. Hazardous materials removal workers, forest and conservation workers, firefighters, water and wastewater operators and fish and game wardens fall in or near the bottom third. Foresters, forest technicians, range managers and environmental technicians sit in between [5, 6]. The pattern is a gradient, not two clusters. Compliance officers is a broad occupation, most of it outside environmental work, so its rank overstates the signal for environmental compliance staff. The common claim that environmental work is "protected by its field character" holds mainly for the field and operations occupations at the low end [J; 6]. EMST holds the occupation-level values and will publish them once the dataset terms are recorded in its third-party license register [67]. BLS's new AI exposure categories (section 2.5) are U.S. government data, so they offer a way to publish an exposure profile without that license question once EMST ingests them [J; 76, 24].

Three cautions apply. The indices agree broadly on how occupations rank because they share an O*NET base, but they answer different questions [6]. Low exposure to language models is not low exposure to technology. Anthropic's September 2026 study estimates that robots could technically perform about 74% of U.S. physical tasks (about 34% of working hours) but are cost-competitive for only about 0.3% of tasks; these are Anthropic estimates, and they rely partly on ratings made by Anthropic's own model [68]. Sensing is measured by no general index, although species detection and remote sensing are the two largest clusters of AI in federal environmental bureaus (Figure F03) [8]. And labels for single tasks are unstable, so EMST never attaches a single external label to a task; it aggregates across matched tasks and shows the spread [23].

### 2.3 Usage: still thin, and hard to see

Environmental tasks appear in the Anthropic Economic Index's first release (February 2025 data), but each accounts for a very small share of sampled conversations [69]. EMST has not yet examined the later releases or the March 2026 observed-exposure data that BLS uses, so this statement describes early-2025 usage only. In Microsoft's usage-based measure, most environmental occupations rank lower, relative to other occupations, than they do on capability exposure. The gap in ranks is widest for roles such as foresters and forest technicians, whose analytical tasks sit inside agency systems that consumer tools do not see [J; 6]. Part of the gap is sampling, since government tenants are not sampled; part is probably real adoption lag. The data cannot yet separate the two [J; 6].

Two usage figures are often misread:

- On Claude.ai, the share of directive (automation-style) conversations was 27% in the first report, 39% in August 2025 and 32% in November 2025 [70].
- In API traffic, 77% of transcripts showed automation patterns (directive and feedback-loop modes, both of which Anthropic classes as automation) in the September 2025 report, which used August 2025 data [70]. That figure describes how developers call the model. It does not describe job automation or task automation.

For context, 19.8% of U.S. businesses reported having used AI in any business function in the previous two weeks in the Census Bureau's survey of 3 May 2026, as did 37% of firms with 250 or more employees [71].

### 2.4 What is actually deployed in federal environmental agencies

OMB's 2025 inventory is the best direct evidence of deployment. It is self-reported, and registering a use case is not the same as using it (Figures F02–F04).

- **Scale.** Government-wide there are 3,611 individually reported use cases, 445 of them high-impact. Interior reports 247 use cases (129 deployed or piloted, none high-impact), USDA 162 (86 and 4) and EPA 29 (14 and 2) [8].
- **Environmental units.** EMST counted 504 use cases in eleven environmental units (nine agencies and bureaus and two USDA mission areas): the U.S. Geological Survey (187), NOAA (157), USDA Natural Resources and Environment (76), EPA (29), USDA Farm Production and Conservation (20), the Fish and Wildlife Service (11), the Bureau of Reclamation (8), the Bureau of Land Management (7), the Bureau of Safety and Environmental Enforcement (6), the National Park Service (2) and the Bureau of Indian Affairs (1). Of the 329 that report an AI type, 232 are classical machine learning, 34 computer vision and 32 generative AI; 175 report no type, including all of NOAA's. Of the 347 that report a stage, 61 are deployed. EMST counts only "deployed" here, while OMB's summary pools deployed and piloted; NOAA reported no stages [8].
- **By function.** Species and wildlife detection leads with 89 use cases (11 deployed), followed by remote sensing 63 (11), generative-AI drafting and summarizing 41 (4), water and hydrology 36 (10), forecasting 33 (2) and fire 31 (8). NEPA, environmental review and permitting has 5, none deployed. These families come from EMST's keyword classification and overlap [8].
- **Agentic AI.** Four agentic use cases appear in the environmental units: three pilots and one pre-deployment, none deployed (Figure F02) [8].
- **Drafting tools.** Drafting, summarizing, writing and search tools are licensed at 10,000 or more users in EPA. Interior and USDA license some tools department-wide at 50,000 or more users: at Interior a writing tool, at USDA a search and knowledge-retrieval assistant (Figure F04) [8].

So generic document tools are available to much of the federal environmental workforce now, while specialist substitution in environmental review is not deployed [J; 6]. The environmental AI that agencies class as high-impact is machine learning that drives payments or enforcement, and the classification turns on whether human review is "the principal basis" of the decision. Substantive human review is therefore the hinge of federal AI compliance, and it is a data-craft skill [10].

**Permitting technology.** CEQ's NEPA and Permitting Data and Technology Standard is at version 1.2 (18 August 2025) [72]. A national laboratory's PermitAI project offers a search tool designed for federal NEPA reviewers, and a drafting benchmark built with OpenAI has 102 test cases drawn from environmental impact statements of 19 agencies [114]. These are availability and benchmarks, not registered deployment; EMST does not publish claims about time saved per document [1].

**Governance.** OMB's April 2025 memos M-25-21 and M-25-22 set federal AI use and acquisition rules, and M-26-04 (11 December 2025) adds contract terms for procuring large language models [73]. OPM's memo of 27 August 2026 treats AI drafting of position descriptions as generally not high-impact when the responsible official independently reviews, revises and adopts the draft; adopting a draft without substantive, documented review is to be treated as high-impact. The memo presumes rating and ranking of applicants to be high-impact [74]. NIST's AI Risk Management Framework 1.0 remains the released version; a revision is in progress with no date [75].

### 2.5 BLS projections and AI exposure categories

BLS released its 2025–35 Employment Projections on 27 August 2026. Total employment is projected to grow 3.5%, from 170.3 million to 176.2 million; data scientists (shown for reference, not an environmental occupation) 34.6%; office and administrative support −4.0%, partly from AI-enabled automation [61]. For environmental occupations (Figure F07), grouped by EMST's convention of treating anything within one percentage point of the 3.5% average as "about average" (these groups are EMST's, not BLS categories):

- Faster than average: natural sciences managers +8.2%, environmental science and protection technicians +7%, environmental engineers +6.3%, environmental scientists +6%, conservation scientists +5.3%.
- About average: firefighters +4%, compliance officers +3.8% (a broad occupation, mostly outside environmental work), zoologists and wildlife biologists +3.6%.
- Slower or declining: foresters +2.2%, forest and conservation workers −2%, water and wastewater operators −6%.

Source: [61]. These are context, not evidence of skill change, and BLS does not attribute them to AI [24].

With this release BLS began publishing AI exposure categories: four relative levels (Low, Moderate, High, Very high) for each projected occupation. "Theoretical" exposure combines Felten–Raj–Seamans, Eloundou et al. and Eisfeldt et al.; "observed" exposure combines Anthropic and Microsoft usage data. BLS says the categories are not forecasts and do not separate automation from augmentation [76]. They are a convenient official summary, not independent evidence, because they are built from the same capability and usage sources discussed above. EMST has not yet ingested the per-occupation values [24].

### 2.6 Outcome evidence: what has, and has not, been measured

- **Young workers in exposed occupations.** In ADP payroll data, employment of 22–25-year-olds in the most AI-exposed occupations was about 19% below the trend for less-exposed occupations as of June 2026, in the authors' August 2026 update. The original 2025 paper reported 13%; the update revises that vintage to 15%. The adjustment came through reduced hiring, with no comparable gap for experienced workers. The authors call the pattern descriptive. They also note that the gaps shrink when education is controlled for, that some of the differing trends began before generative AI, and that the gaps are larger in ADP data than in national benchmarks; ADP also under-covers government [77].
- **Unemployment.** Anthropic's March 2026 "observed exposure" study found no systematic rise in unemployment for highly exposed workers since late 2022. Job-finding by 22–25-year-olds into the most exposed occupations fell about 14% compared with 2022, a barely significant result [78].
- **Earnings and hours.** In Denmark, chatbot users reported saving on average 2.8% of work hours in a 2024 survey, and the study found precisely estimated zero effects on earnings and recorded hours: its confidence intervals rule out effects larger than 1% [79]. The Budget Lab at Yale's May 2026 analysis found "no clear effects yet" on the labor market [80].
- **Gaps.** EMST found no outcome study with an environmental cut; environmental occupations are too small for reliable survey cells [J; 6]. GDPval, a widely cited benchmark of AI performance on real occupational tasks, covers 44 occupations; none is in environmental science, forestry or environmental engineering, though it includes compliance officers, an occupation EMST tracks [81].
- **Expectations.** In WEF's Future of Jobs 2025, more than 1,000 large employers expect 39% of core skills to change by 2030 and list environmental stewardship among the top ten rising skills [82]. PwC reports an average wage premium of 62% for AI skills in job ads, but only 16% in government and the public sector [83].

## 3. How roles are changing

### 3.1 What newer plans require

Land management plans are one of the few demand signals that state what work must be done. EMST analyzed the open OpenLMP corpus (154 non-empty plan documents). About 95% of its components come from Forest Service plans, all in western states, so this section describes western national forests only. The rule comparison uses 120 Forest Service documents, 84 written under the 1982 Planning Rule and 36 under the 2012 rule. It compares different documents written under different rules; it is not a time series (Figures F05–F06).

- **Monitoring.** Components typed as monitoring average 30.7% of components per 2012-rule document, against 2.7% per 1982-rule plan, with each document weighted equally; weighted by components, the figures are 22.2% and 1.8% [86]. That gap does not survive a closer look. Of the 36 2012-rule documents, 17 are monitoring evaluation reports and 5 are stand-alone monitoring plans, and one 2022 monitoring evaluation report supplies 29% of all monitoring components in the corpus. Among plan documents alone, the shares are about 10% (14 plans) and 3% (81 plans), and the 95% interval of their difference includes zero, so the two cannot be told apart (Figure F05). The 1982 rule also required plans to contain monitoring and evaluation requirements, yet only 24 of the 84 older plans have any component typed as monitoring, so the older baseline probably reflects how monitoring was documented and extracted rather than an absence of monitoring [J; 86]. And because the 2012 rule requires a monitoring program, a larger monitoring share would partly restate the rule in any case.
- **Content.** Newer documents mention climate change about 20 times per 1,000 components against about 2 under the older rule, data or models about 81 times against 14, and carbon about 1.4 times against 0.1. For each of the three, the 95% interval of the difference excludes zero, both across all documents and among plan documents alone (Figure F06) [86].
- **Coordination.** Coordination grew less than component counts suggest. Partnership touchpoints roughly doubled (11.9 to 24.9 per 1,000 components), but the 95% interval of that difference includes zero; cross-boundary touchpoints were flat (4.7 to 5.0); and state-agency touchpoints were lower (6.2 to 4.3), a difference whose interval also includes zero. Among plan documents alone, the rise in partnership touchpoints is distinguishable from zero (Figure F06) [86].
- **Methods.** Plans name outcomes and reporting far more than field methods; remote sensing and eDNA are rarely named in monitoring questions [6].

A plan requirement is not a funded workload, and whether agencies staff it is the open question [J]. In western national-forest plans the stated requirement is for indicator design, evaluation and reporting more than for particular field methods, and EMST expects technology change to show up in protocols, position descriptions and postings rather than in plan text [J; 6]. The corpus is narrow: water, air, waste and environmental-health work, which employ some of the largest public environmental workforces, need other requirement sources [J; 87].

### 3.2 The three corridors and the 13 priority roles

EMST v2 starts with function-defined role profiles, not job titles. Each has variants by employer type (federal, state, local, NGO, private, academic) that add or drop tasks, authority, credential gates and frictions, and Federal Series Profiles and State Class Views are generated from them [88]. The first 13 profiles form three corridors that are designed to cross all three crafts and to end in state and federal role variants, so the NGO pilot can test whether pathways carry across employers (Figure F10) [88]. The online explorer also holds earlier, lower-depth drafts of 18 more roles (among them forester, hydrologist and air-quality, drinking-water, wastewater, environmental-health and solid-waste roles) and six watch roles (hypothesized work, not yet observed in postings or position descriptions); this report discusses only the 13 corridor roles [88, 119].

*Watch* marks a role whose work is hypothesized: EMST has not yet observed it in postings or position descriptions, and no signal behind it met the evidence test for a stronger state [119]. The other corridor roles are *established*, which means long-standing work in the sector, not that EMST has validated their content.

What EMST can and cannot say about these roles today:

- **Each role has a draft task bundle and a draft change view.** The reshaping claims in those views are agent drafts; every task shows "assessment pending" until a multi-rater assessment exists, and no role has an active change indicator [85, 89]. This report therefore states no role-level change as a finding.
- **Field and data corridor.** Monitoring plans assume a technician layer: the monitoring programs of two recent forest plans list GS-5 and GS-7 aquatics and range technicians alongside GS-11 specialists [9]. Environmental science and protection technicians are projected to grow 7%, while forest and conservation workers are projected to fall 2% [61]. EMST expects sensors, drones and AI triage to move technician work from collecting data toward siting, servicing and checking instruments and outputs, and expects Earth-observation foundation models to change the methods of geospatial work rather than the core of the role [J; 9, 10]. The ground-truth and validation steward is a watch role: EMST drafted it because every scaled remote or AI product needs labels, reference plots and field checks, but has not yet observed it as a post. The named counter-signal is validation absorbed into vendor services with no agency field calibration [88, 9, 119].
- **Fire corridor.** Position qualifications act as credential gates, not proficiency levels, and EMST links to those qualification systems rather than competing with them. The fire intelligence analyst is also a watch role: EMST's draft expects the work to shift from incident-only positions to year-round, continuous intelligence, but that shift has not yet been observed in postings or position descriptions; the named counter-signal is that the function stays inside incident positions with no year-round staffing [J; 88, 119].
- **Stewardship and regulation corridor.** Most state environmental work sits on the regulator's side of the counter: permit writing, inspection, enforcement and delegated programs. EMST v1 was written mostly from the land manager's and applicant's side [J; 90]. Review specialists now work to agency-specific procedures under substantial judicial deference [11, 12], and AI drafting tools are available to them, but no NEPA or permitting AI is registered as deployed [8]. Program managers carry the budgeting, workforce-planning and formation responsibilities that contraction makes harder [88].

## 4. What stays human: field craft, statecraft and the formation problem

This section describes where human performance remains the binding constraint today. It is descriptive, not a claim that certain work must stay human; where accountability keeps a task human, that is a governance choice that can change [J; 6, 89].

### 4.1 The three crafts

EMST v2 records a rating for each task on all three crafts, so blends are visible rather than forced into one "primary craft"; every current rating is an agent draft [16, 19].

- **Field craft** is the capacity to perceive, measure, act and stay safe in a specific place: reading land, water, air, living systems and the engineered systems that hold, treat or move them, and producing the ground truth and physical interventions that other environmental work depends on. It is formed through repeated, supervised exposure to real conditions.
- **Data craft** is the disciplined capacity to turn observations into *warranted* evidence, models and decision support, including specifying, validating, supervising and answering for AI-generated analysis.
- **Statecraft** is the learnable capacity to turn knowledge and public purpose into decisions that are authorized, legitimate, resourced and durable. Its defining deliverable is a commitment that holds.

Role blends are computed from task ratings and are used to describe roles and design learning, not to match people to jobs [16]. Every v2 craft rating is still an agent draft awaiting the craft-rating pilot, so none is charted here [19].

### 4.2 Field craft in federal land agencies: the field moves, it does not vanish

The evidence in this section comes from the Forest Service and Interior. The strongest comes from drones, which so far are added to existing jobs more than they create new ones: 99% of Forest Service trained pilots fly as a collateral duty alongside another primary job [60]. When Interior restricted drones, flights fell sharply (BLM's from 3,643 in FY2019 to 528 in FY2023), and habitat monitoring for one endangered butterfly shifted to two staff on foot; GAO notes that drones would have covered nine times the area. GAO also cautions that these changes do not necessarily reflect the policies' precise effect [91]. Adoption is gated by policy, procurement and cost more than by capability. Since 22 December 2025, section 1825 of the FY2024 National Defense Authorization Act (part of the American Security Drone Act of 2023) has barred using federal funds to buy or operate covered drones from foreign-adversary-linked manufacturers [116], while the FCC's Covered List action bars only new equipment authorizations [92]. The FAA's rule for flights beyond visual line of sight (Part 108) has been under review at OIRA since 10 July 2026 and was still pending there as of 23 October 2026 (to be re-checked before release) [115, 92].

Every automated monitoring system carries a calibration cost: lidar inventories need calibration plots, classifiers need verified labels, burn-severity maps need field checks. Continuous sensors cut routine visits, so the visits that remain are for problems, calibration and judgment. That raises the skill needed per visit and removes the easy repetitions novices used to learn on [J; 9].

### 4.3 Statecraft: authority and accountability

Statecraft's deliverable is a review, permit, rule, agreement, budget or coalition that survives challenge, turnover and implementation [39]. AI touches its written medium most: drafting, comparison and summary. Accountability is where the human role concentrates; the OPM memo's test for AI-drafted position descriptions turns on whether the responsible official independently reviews, revises and adopts the draft [74]. Statecraft can be taught and assessed, but the field has codified it more than it has assessed it: EMST found no validation studies of how environmental statecraft is assessed [39].

### 4.4 Data craft: warrant, not pattern-making

Pattern-making and projection are what machines now do cheaply. The human contribution in data craft is **warrant**: a claim about the world whose provenance, method, uncertainty and fitness for a decision the practitioner can show and answer for [10, 16]. Specific models, platforms and policy memos have half-lives of one to three years, so EMST keeps durable competencies separate from fluency in particular tools and records tools in their own layer with review and retirement dates [10].

### 4.5 The formation problem

Field craft and statecraft are reproduced through graduated exposure: many seasons of varied, supervised work with feedback. Seasonal, corps, trainee and junior analyst jobs have been the sector's main supply of those repetitions [J; 9]. Contraction now removes the jobs and the supervisors together; automation could then remove the easiest repetitions (grab samples, call-back surveys, plot measurements, image sorting, first drafts, comment coding). If both continue, the sector could have more data and fewer people who can tell whether it is right [S; 9]. EMST's foresight analysis names a quieter version of the same risk, "validation without formation": juniors who only review AI output, without producing and defending analyses, could become seniors who cannot tell when the AI is wrong [S; 15].

EMST's response is built into the data model; the rules are provisional [93, 19]:

- **Formation tasks are flagged** in role profiles.
- **Craft balance.** No pixel without plot: every data- or AI-heavy pathway includes field ground-truth work, with 20–30% field time for remote-sensing reskilling. No model without a decision owner.
- **A by-hand quota.** Formation tasks that AI compresses require a quota of unaided products before review-and-certify credit, and every pathway with formation tasks needs a supervisor formation plan and a mentor.
- **Assessment** uses seeded-error work samples, and both AI-off and AI-on assessment where formation is at risk.
- **AI literacy** maps to the five content areas of the Department of Labor's framework: Understand AI Principles, Explore AI Uses, Direct AI Effectively, Evaluate AI Outputs and Use AI Responsibly [94].

## 5. The entry-path problem: a hypothesis, stated so it can be tested

> **Hypothesis (EMST v2; status: hypothesis, not finding).** In environmental work, the rungs on which people form field craft and statecraft are being removed by two mechanisms acting in sequence: personnel, hiring and grant decisions that cut entry jobs now (so far evidenced in federal land agencies and conservation corps), and AI and sensing compressing the formation tasks that remain. A third mechanism, the loss of long-tenured staff, weakens the review-and-certify function that keeps AI-assisted work trustworthy. If hiring resumes without redesigned entry work, a temporary contraction may become a lasting change to the career ladder.
>
> **Publication rule.** EMST materials show this hypothesis with its mechanism and evidence status. It will be published as a finding only after named human reviewers, a qualified industrial-organizational psychologist and a labor economist, have validated it, in addition to the reviews by EMST's industrial-organizational psychology and labor-market roles, which are AI agents; it will then be co-published with a nonpartisan partner [89]. EMST's Chief Taxonomy Architect (an AI agent role) adopted this rule on 23 October 2026, and the EMST project lead confirmed it on 24 October 2026 [107].

| Link | Claim | Evidence | Status |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Entry jobs are being removed now, by personnel, hiring and grant decisions | No Forest Service non-fire seasonal hires in 2025 [4]; internship postings −43.8% on one board [33]; corps grants terminated in April 2025, then reinstated in the plaintiff states and offered for reinstatement elsewhere in July 2025 [34, 111]; under-30 share 7.9% [2] | **Evidenced** for federal land agencies and corps; state, local, NGO and private entry hiring **unmeasured** |
| 2 | AI and sensing compress formation tasks | Desk entry tasks are highly exposed on capability measures [6]; drafting tools are licensed at workforce scale [8]; NEPA and permitting AI is not deployed [8] | **Partly evidenced** (capability and availability); use inside agencies and outcomes **unknown** |
| 3 | Long-tenured staff are leaving (top-of-ladder loss) | EPA year-end 2025 departures, mostly deferred-resignation retirements: median 30.3 years of service, against 10.8 for those remaining [3] | **Evidenced** for one agency |
| 4 | When hiring resumes, managers do not refill entry tasks that AI now compresses | Private analogue only: young workers in exposed occupations about 19% below trend, through reduced hiring; descriptive; the gap shrinks with education controls and partly predates generative AI; government under-covered [77]. Job-finding into exposed occupations down about 14%, barely significant [78] | **No environmental evidence yet** [S] |
| 5 | By the early 2030s, fewer mid-career staff can judge AI-assisted work | Scenario reasoning [15] | **Speculation** [S] |

**Counter-mechanisms that could break the chain** [J; 6]. Saved time may go to backlog reduction under new review deadlines rather than to fewer hires, and demand may rise with fire, disclosure and devolution. In Danish data, chatbot time savings have so far shown no measurable effect on earnings or recorded hours [79]. States, NGOs and contractors may absorb entry roles, as some partners hired seasonals when the Forest Service did not, and entry work can be redesigned around supervised ground-truth and sensor-service roles and multi-employer apprenticeships [9].

**What would settle it.**

1. Entry-grade (GS-5/7) accessions and trainee shares in the 0400-series and related environmental occupations in 2027–28, compared with 2023–24 and conditioned on budget. OPM's Federal Workforce Data site has offered monthly data since January 2026 [95].
2. Quarterly comparisons of the duties text in entry-level postings; EMST has not yet taken the October 2026 baseline [1].
3. Time-use and AI-use data from the NGO pilot cohort.
4. Entry-cohort volume (seasonal, corps, apprentice and trainee starts) against 2024; a fall of more than 25% triggers a field-pathway warning [96].

**How EMST will compute it.** Career-ladder risk is a computed, pathway-level indicator: the share of an entry role's formation tasks assessed as compressed, supervised or substituted, adjusted when a pathway records a credible alternative formation route, with every reading tagged by its driver (AI, sensing, automation, fiscal or policy) [23, 93]. It cannot be computed until multi-rater task assessments exist, so no value is reported here.

## 6. Four futures and what to do now

### 6.1 The scenarios

EMST's foresight agent (the AI agent in EMST's Strategic Foresight Lead role) drafted four scenarios for 2026–2035 on the two driver clusters that it judged both highest-impact and most uncertain. No human foresight panel has reviewed them [15]:

- **Public stewardship capacity**: rebuilt and coordinated, or thin and fragmented. This is about people, authority, coordination and entry pipelines across all levels of government, not about dollars.
- **AI reliability and legitimacy in environmental decisions**: trusted and assured, or brittle and contested.

| | **AI trusted and assured** | **AI brittle and contested** |
|---|---|---|
| **Capacity rebuilt and coordinated** | **Assured Augmentation.** Headcount near 2024 levels, different mix: fewer people producing documents, more specifying and reviewing AI-assisted analysis, collecting ground truth and running continuous intelligence. Field craft becomes the bottleneck. Quiet risk: validation without formation. | **Human Premium.** After failures or court remands, AI is confined to search, summary and quality checks. Experienced reviewers, field ecologists and fire leaders command a premium; throughput is slow. |
| **Capacity thin and fragmented** | **Platform Stewardship.** A small, senior public core procures, oversees and signs; AI-leveraged consultancies and platforms deliver. Risk: a hollow state that cannot judge vendor output. | **Patchwork Retreat.** Junior roles are cut on the assumption that AI will carry the load; AI under-delivers; fire absorbs what capacity remains. Highest risk of irreversible loss: fire leadership, place-based judgment, taxonomic skills, long-term monitoring records. |

The foresight analysis treats some elements as given in all four: more fire and climate-hazard workload, expert retirements, regulatory swings between administrations, and continued private demand for greenhouse gas accounting under state and EU rules [J; 15]. EMST's foresight review found no court ruling on an AI-assisted environmental record as of October 2026 (a not-found result, not a systematic docket search), so the Human Premium scenario's trigger events remain speculative [S; 15].

## 7. What EMST v2 is and how to use it

### 7.1 What it is

EMST v2 is an open, versioned taxonomy and a set of tools built on it. Its design responds directly to the evidence above.

- **Tasks are the spine.** Competencies earn their place by linking to tasks. Role profiles bundle tasks with formation flags, and with fields for time share, criticality, needed-at-entry status and competency targets that public views leave empty until a job analysis or a multi-rater assessment supplies them. In the data download, the agents' drafts of those values sit only in a labeled `agentDraft` sub-object, and the CSV and HR import files leave them out [88, 19, 89, 120].
- **Crafts are recorded as blends per task** (all current craft ratings are agent drafts), plus two attributes that weights alone would lose: *field dependency* (must the work happen on site?) and *formation dependency* (must the expertise have been formed in the field, even if the task is now done on a screen?) [16].
- **Change is evidence, not opinion.** Capability, deployment and staffing outcome are separate constructs; change signals are dated and sourced; indicators move only under the two-family evidence rule [23, 84].
- **Lifecycle and use tiers.** A record's lifecycle state describes the work in the sector, not the record: *established* means long-standing work, not validated content, and *watch* means hypothesized work, not yet observed in postings or position descriptions. Validation of the record is tracked separately. The vocabulary also has *proposed*, *emerging* and *declining*, but in the 2.0.0-rc.1 release no record met the evidence test for *proposed* or *emerging*, so those records are *watch*. Uses are *exploratory*, *describe*, *develop* or *select*. A record's effective tier is capped by its validation status and evidence and by the display gate; lifecycle no longer caps it. Nothing in v2.0 is at *select* [17, 19, 119].
- **Display honesty.** Every on-screen number traces to a stored, attributed record; without a v2 multi-rater assessment a view shows "assessment pending"; v1 values appear only in a task-level evidence drawer marked "v1 single-analyst prior, LLM-assisted, unvalidated — not for decisions" [89].
- **No personal data, ever.** The public explorer is static and local-first: a gap check runs in the browser and nothing a user enters leaves the device. Kits for HR and learning systems run inside the adopter's own authorized systems. EMST never ranks, scores, screens or selects people [18].
- **The product.** A Role Change and Development Planner with four views ("How is this role changing?", "Where are the gaps?", "Where can this role lead?" (exploratory until validated) and "What's coming"), plus generated Federal Series Profiles, State Class Views and kits [89].
- **Licenses.** Data and schema CC0; publications CC BY 4.0; code Apache-2.0. Third-party material keeps its own terms [67].

**The theory behind it.** The charter puts it in one sentence: "If those blends are visible and portable, mobility follows" [21]. The pilot is designed to test that sentence link by link. The published evidence suggests that announcing skills-based policy does not by itself change who is hired, although neither study below tests visibility directly. In a Burning Glass Institute and Harvard Business School study of private firms that removed degree requirements, about 37% of those firms accounted for nearly all of the change in actual hiring, about 45% changed their postings but not their hiring, and the change reached fewer than 1 in 700 hires in 2023 [97]. Colorado officials say about 80% of state job classifications now emphasize skills over degrees, yet about 25% of 2024 hires in those classifications were people without degrees, roughly the same share as in 2023; because 2023 is the first year of data, after the reforms began, this cannot show whether reclassification had an effect [98]. EMST's product strategy therefore puts its identifiers into the documents that gate movement (position descriptions, class specifications, postings, assessments, development plans and learning records) ahead of browse-only views [96].

### 7.2 How four users could use it from the first day

**A federal HR specialist.**

- **Modernizing a PD (for example 0460 Forestry).** Open the Federal Series Profile, which assembles draft major duties, task lists and Factor 1 knowledge statements from each federal role variant that an AI agent crosswalked to the series as a close match or stronger. Weaker agent matches are left out of the profile and counted, and the match confidence shown is the agent's own, not calibrated against any review. The 2.0.0-rc.1 release has 13 such profiles, so many series, including 0401 and 0404, have none yet [120]. Percentages of time, criticality and needed-at-entry flags are left to the supervisor and the job analysis. Treat the profile as a draft for normal classification review. EMST's regenerated Forester PD (GS-0460, FES format) shows the pattern and is labeled "subject to classification review" [99, 88]. OPM's August 2026 memo treats AI-assisted PD drafting as generally not high-impact when the responsible official independently reviews, revises and adopts the draft [74]. Check with your labor-relations office before changing PDs on the basis of EMST drafts.
- **Assessments.** Use EMST task lists as the starting point for the job analysis that 5 CFR 300.103 requires [13], and toward the technical assessments the Chance to Compete Act calls for. Since 30 September 2025, under OPM's Merit Hiring Plan, self-assessment questionnaires may be used only to screen for minimum qualifications, not to rate and rank, except for seasonal positions, positions at GS-4 and below, and other OPM-authorized cases. In FY2025, 91.23% of delegated-examining announcements relied solely on self-report questionnaires; in FY2026 Q2, 34.3% of delegated-examining announcements used technical or off-the-shelf assessments [100].
- **Timing.** OPM's plan places Natural Resources Management in Tier 2, to be addressed after the Tier 1 occupations, and gives no Tier 2 completion date; the plan's governmentwide target for full implementation is the end of FY2028 [100]. OPM has so far issued competency-based classification and qualification standards only for series 2210 [101]. Its April 2026 list of series *proposed* for realignment (for example 0455 into 0404) had no final decision as of 23 October 2026 (to be re-checked before release). EMST records the proposal as proposed and keeps task-level links, so a PD can be written either way [102].
- **Staffing plans.** Under EO 14356, use the formation flags and the tasks EMST's draft profiles mark for protection to justify critical entry and knowledge-transfer positions [28, 17].
- **Reductions in force.** OPM issued a memo titled "Final Regulations on Reduction in Force" on 2 September 2026; EMST has read only its title and date [117]. Do not use EMST profiles to define competitive levels or to make retention decisions (section 7.3).

**A state training officer.**

- **Credit federal experience.** Use the State Class View to see how a federal role variant maps to your class plan, to decide which federal experience to credit, and to design onboarding for transferred staff. Like the series profiles, class views are built only from agent matches at close match or stronger, with the agent's uncalibrated confidence; the 2.0.0-rc.1 release has 20, for classes in the jurisdictions whose public class plans EMST read [120]. Pennsylvania's Office of Administration, for example, has identified federal job titles whose experience may count as equivalent for certain Commonwealth titles [37, 88].
- **Build curricula for regulator-side roles.** Permit writers and inspectors are corridor roles with draft task bundles at full depth [88]. For air, water, wastewater, drinking-water, waste and environmental-health staff, who make up much of a state environmental agency, the explorer holds only earlier, lower-depth drafts, and EMST has not yet written briefs for them. Check the current status of any EPA-provided training your staff rely on, since EPA's own staffing has fallen [J; 3].
- **Short programs.** Workforce Pell can fund programs of 150–599 clock hours over 8 to fewer than 15 weeks from 1 July 2026; governors, working with state workforce boards, identify the in-demand occupations [103]. WIOA has not been reauthorized [103].
- **Gap checks.** Run the local-first gap check with your team; ratings stay in a file on your own machine [18].

**An NGO program lead (the intended first pilot user).**

- Use "How is this role changing?" for the corps member and field technician roles to plan a season's training and a crew member's development plan.
- Write job descriptions, grant reports and members' exit portfolios that cite EMST role and competency identifiers, so a state or federal employer can read them.
- Design ground-truth and sensor-service work as supervised learning tasks, so the formation they provide shows in a portable record [93].
- The pilot is planned to run from March 2027 to February 2028 with about 12 organizations across the three corridors. It is not yet funded: the design budgets tiered stipends for participating organizations, which depend on pilot funding still being sought. Use is limited to describing and developing; nothing ranks, screens or selects anyone [104]. To ask about taking part, use the contact line in section 8.5.

**An educator.**

- Map courses to EMST tasks and competencies through generic course learning outcomes, keeping field ground-truth components and an accountable decision owner in programs that lead into data- and AI-heavy work [93]. The field-practicum rules in section 4.5 (field time in every data-heavy pathway; unaided work before AI-assisted credit) are written to be usable as course design.
- EMST's proficiency levels are defined by responsibility, not years: supervised, independent, senior, lead or expert, and strategic [118]. They describe development targets; EMST shows no target level for any role as a finding.
- Align AI literacy to the Department of Labor's five content areas [94].
- Plan for updates: O*NET 31.0 (August 2026) is current, and the 2028 SOC is planned for finalization in 2027, although as of 23 October 2026 the revision page still listed its second Federal Register notice as "to be determined" [105, 106]. EMST claims no alignment with O*NET, ESCO, OPM, state or accreditation frameworks until a reviewed mapping set exists [89].

### 7.3 What EMST will not do

- **No decisions about people.** EMST will not rank, score, screen or select people, or support reduction-in-force (including competitive levels and retention), adverse action or performance ratings [18].
- **No partisan content.** EMST provides no content for the Merit Hiring Plan's essay questions and gives no help with designating positions into Schedule Policy/Career [40].
- **No unvalidated pathway advice.** No pathway is labeled "recommended" until it predicts real career moves clearly better than simple guesses, against a threshold set in advance [96].
- **No Tribal content** without approval from a Tribal Advisory Council (section 8.4) [22].

## 8. Limits, validation and what comes next

### 8.1 Agent drafts versus validated content

Everything EMST itself has rated so far was drafted by AI agents: task bundles, time shares, craft ratings, change claims, signal directions and driver tags. Each is recorded with its rater type, a frozen model and prompt version, a "draft" validation status and evidence label S [19]. Where one model rated the same item in several passes, the passes count as one rater: their agreement measures run-to-run consistency, not reliability. The tags carried over from EMST v1 are labeled single-analyst, LLM-assisted, evidence S [119, 120]. No human has reviewed these values, and no change indicator has moved. This report uses them only to describe structure, never as findings [1].

The crosswalks to outside frameworks are agent drafts as well, and no person has reviewed any of their rows. In the crosswalk from EMST tasks to O*NET tasks, 202 of 316 rows name an O*NET task, and the two AI coders agreed on a close match or better for 16 of them. Their agreement on match type (κ 0.465) is below the 0.6 that EMST requires, so that crosswalk fails its gate [120]. Change signals are counted by observation: the release's 107 signal records hold 68 distinct observations, because one reading of a dataset can bear on several tasks or roles. Seven signals are marked indeterminate: five plan-document comparisons from section 3.1, whose difference interval or plans-only check includes zero, and two others whose stored values do not establish a direction. The 43 projection readings that reach EMST work only through a broad occupational match are rated low confidence [119, 120].

### 8.2 Evidence gaps

- **No posting baseline yet.** The October 2026 snapshot of federal and state postings was not taken, so EMST makes no claim about posting volume or content [1].
- **Data not yet ingested.** The OPM Federal Workforce Data series pull is pending, so the headcount figures in section 1.2 still rest on secondary analyses of OPM data. BLS per-occupation AI exposure categories and Anthropic Economic Index occupation files after the first release are not ingested [24].
- **Outcomes and coverage.** EMST found no outcome study with an environmental cut [J; 6]. State, local, NGO and private workforce data are thin, and the plan corpus is mostly western Forest Service plans [24, 87].
- **License terms.** Values from two external indices are withheld until their terms are recorded [67].
- **Tribal workforce.** National data and EMST do not measure it [22].

### 8.3 Validation with people, from 2027

- **Reliability studies** of 120–200 units, each rated by six people. Agreement is measured with Krippendorff's alpha and judged on the low end of its confidence interval: values that raters agree on strongly (at least .80) can be used in production, moderate agreement (.667 to .80) is shown as tentative, and values that raters do not agree on are never shown [19].
- **A pre-registered modified Delphi panel** with 15–25 members per panel, exact Lawshe critical values and a hard stop after three rounds. Panels correct for the thinning of the federal workforce by over-recruiting public field and statecraft practitioners, including recently separated staff, and by capping any one employer type at 30% [19].
- **Job analysis** with agencies that agree to take part, using matrix-sampled surveys, for five first clusters: field monitoring and inventory; compliance, permitting and review; geospatial and data analysis; water quality and water operations; and a natural-resources series starter pack [19].
- **Cognitive interviews** (about 10–15), and **AI-assisted tagging only behind a gold-set gate**: an LLM is never the sole coder of a value relevant to selection [19].

### 8.4 The Tribal Advisory Council and the content hold

EMST states on its face that Indigenous knowledge is a distinct body of environmental expertise held and governed by Indigenous nations, and that it is out of scope by design [22]. EMST does not define, describe, rank or credential Indigenous knowledge; holds no confidential Tribal information; publishes no characterization of a named Tribe without consent; does not ingest Tribal employers' documents by default; and creates no Tribal profession family [22].

Until a Tribal Advisory Council constituted by Tribal governments and inter-Tribal organizations exists and approves, nothing Tribal-related is published. The project lead has accepted binding consent authority for that Council over the inclusion, wording and release of Tribal-related content; a charter carve-out so that neither the Chief Taxonomy Architect nor the Editorial Board can override the Council in that category, with "withhold" as the default; and compensation for Tribal reviewers budgeted before first contact [107, 22]. Forming the Council is not EMST's to do, and no outreach has been made [22]. This report contains no Tribal content.

## Methods and sources

**How this report was built.** AI agents drafted it from EMST's accepted decisions, research syntheses and change-signal and instrument records. AI agents checked each external fact against EMST's verification key facts, its corrections register and its claims register as of 23 October 2026, and applied the register's "do not say" wording throughout [1]. Two further reviews on 23 October 2026, neither by an independent human expert (a red-team evidence review of 99 claims and a simulated practitioner read of the whole package), then found errors and overstatements; this draft applies their corrections. The claims list published with this report gives each claim's register row, or flags that it needs one. EMST-derived figures can be reproduced from repository data.

**Moving facts.** Re-read before any external release, each on or after the date shown: the OFCCP effective date and the Colorado rules hearing (26 October 2026); the SB 253 reporting deadline (10 November 2026); Section 106, after the Advisory Council's 10 November 2026 meeting; the Forest Service objection rule (12 November 2026); S. 5653 floor action (expected early November 2026) and the SPEED Act in the Senate; Part 108; OPM's decision on the occupational-series proposal; the 2028 SOC schedule; and all other register rows by 15 December 2026 [1].

**Figures.** F01–F10 are CSV datasets built by the figure script published with this report. The script checks key values against their verification records and fails if one of those records changes, is withdrawn or is marked superseded; it does not check every cell. The plan-document figures (F05 and F06) use bootstrap confidence intervals and include the sensitivity checks described in section 3.1, computed from a plan-level extract of the OpenLMP corpus. Their rows are labeled by the 95% interval of the 2012-rule minus 1982-rule difference: a difference whose interval includes zero is "not distinguishable", whether or not the two rules' own intervals overlap [86, 119]. The figures index gives each figure's headline, suggested chart form, source note, use tier and evidence label. Charts carry a data table and a text message, with no hover-only information or color-only encoding [89]. Two figures were deliberately not built: the exposure-profile chart, until the index terms are recorded, and a craft-blend chart by role, because every craft value is an agent draft or a v1 value [67, 19].

**Technical note on identifiers.** The claims list and figure files published with this report use EMST's internal identifiers: claim IDs (WP14-Cnnn), claims-register rows (for example WF-06 or RPT-14), verification record IDs (V1–V4 and role source IDs) and role keys. They are there so that each statement can be traced; readers do not need them to use the report. References 111–118 were added in the review-fix pass of 23 October 2026, and references 119–120 when this draft was aligned with the 2.0.0-rc.1 re-cut on 25 October 2026, so they are numbered out of order.

> **Methodology history: EMST v1 (2025–26).** EMST v1 was built by one analyst with LLM assistance. Its database holds 222 competencies in 7 domains and 45 skill areas, and it classified 666 tasks on five dimensions (automation type, tacit density, career-ladder position, technology vector and horizon). v1 documents cite other totals too; the database is treated as authoritative [109, 6]. v2 keeps v1's central concepts, especially career-ladder risk and the three crafts, but retires v1's statistics as findings. No tag had reliability evidence, and several dimensions were partly inferred from one another [110, 6]. An internal re-check found 11 of 222 career-ladder flags contradicting v1's own rule [110]. A claimed pattern that "human-essential" calls arise only from field-craft or statecraft competencies is contradicted by the v1 database [6]. Earlier public claims of validation against OPM and O*NET classification systems, of accredited competency standards and of headline shares of "human-essential" competencies are withdrawn [89]. A v2 pilot calibrating 60 v1 tasks against O*NET found no O*NET analogue for 30% of them, many of them new kinds of work, and, where two O*NET tasks plausibly matched, human labels that disagreed in 13 of 21 cases; it was a single-analyst pilot pending a second coder [6]. All v1 values migrate into v2 as labeled prior evidence, shown only in a task-level evidence drawer [23, 89]. Under the project lead's decision, no other v1 statistic appears in this report [107].

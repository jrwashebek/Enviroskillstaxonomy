## Executive summary: five things leaders need to know

Environmental work is changing for four reasons at once. Federal capacity contracted sharply in 2025–26. Rules and responsibilities are moving between levels of government. Fire and climate workloads are rising. AI and sensing are reaching both desk work and field work. The first audience for this report is federal and state workforce, training and HR leaders; NGOs, employers and educators will find most of it applies to them too.

**1. The biggest force on public environmental jobs right now is a capacity shock, not AI.** Between September 2024 and January 2026 the federal civilian workforce fell 12%, from 2,313,216 to 2,035,344 employees. The Forest Service and the National Park Service each lost more than a quarter of their staff, and the number of federal park rangers fell 34.1% [2]. EPA ended January 2026 with 12,849 employees, about 24% fewer than a year earlier [3]. Entry pipelines thinned at the same time. The Forest Service hired no non-fire seasonal staff in 2025 [4], and employees under 30 now make up 7.9% of the federal workforce [2]. These changes have fiscal and policy drivers. No source attributes them to AI, and they swamp any AI signal in federal headcount data [J].

**2. AI splits environmental work in two, and the evidence on actual use is still thin.** On the most widely used capability measure, the analytical, regulatory and planning occupations rank among the most AI-exposed in the economy, while the field and operations occupations rank among the least exposed [5, 6]. Usage data come from consumer and enterprise platforms that see little government or field work, and they show environmental occupations using AI less than their exposure implies [7, 6]. In federal environmental bureaus, most registered AI is classical machine learning for science work. Of the 347 use cases that report a development stage, 61 are deployed, and none of the five in NEPA review or permitting is deployed [8]. Drafting and summarizing assistants are licensed at 10,000 or more users in EPA, the Department of the Interior and USDA: available at workforce scale, which is not the same as used [8]. No study yet measures AI's employment effects in environmental occupations [J; 6].

**3. Roles are being re-bundled, not deleted, and policy is moving faster than technology.** In the corridor roles EMST profiles first, change shows up as a shift in task mix. Producing first drafts, compiling, coding and routine mapping are getting quicker. Specifying, checking, signing and defending work is becoming more central. Field work is moving toward servicing instruments and producing ground truth [J; 9, 10]. In 2025–26, though, rules and institutions changed review, authorization, hiring and disclosure work faster than any technology did [11, 12, 13, 14]. New work appears in tasks before it appears in job titles or occupational series [J; 6].

**4. The entry path is the risk to watch. It is a hypothesis with a mechanism, not yet a finding.** Formation (the supervised repetition that turns a novice into a practitioner) is squeezed from two directions. Policy and budgets are removing entry jobs now. AI and sensors are starting to compress the tasks juniors learned on. Meanwhile senior reviewers are leaving: the roughly 2,000 EPA employees who left at the end of 2025 had a median 30.3 years of service, against 10.8 years for those who stayed [3]. If hiring resumes without entry tasks redesigned for learning, a temporary shock could become a permanent break in the career ladder [S]. Section 5 sets out the mechanism, the evidence for each link and what would settle the question.

**5. Some moves are right in every plausible future, and they can start now.** EMST tested workforce strategies against four scenarios for 2026–2035, built on two uncertainties: whether public stewardship capacity is rebuilt, and whether AI proves trustworthy in high-stakes environmental decisions [15]. Eight moves hold up in all four: protect a minimum entry cohort and the tasks it learns on; capture tacit knowledge now; make validating AI output a requirement for anyone who uses it; treat ground truth as infrastructure; build skills-based mobility channels between employers; strengthen agencies' ability to buy technology as informed customers; invest in the craft of intergovernmental agreements; and set up horizon scanning. Other moves should wait for named triggers. The strategy that fails most often across the scenarios is using AI to cut entry roles first [J; 15].

**What EMST v2 offers.** EMST v2 describes environmental work as tasks, competencies and role profiles, each carrying a blend of three crafts: field craft, data craft and statecraft. Evidence is labelled, uses are tiered, and no personal data is held [16, 17, 18]. The 2.0 release candidate supports describing and developing (workforce analysis, training, development plans and curriculum), not selecting people. Human validation (reliability studies, a pre-registered Delphi and job analysis with partner agencies) starts in 2027 [19, 20]. Section 7 shows how a federal HR specialist, a state training officer, an NGO programme lead and an educator could use it from the first day.

## 1. What is changing and why now

Five forces act on environmental work at once. Each moves on its own clock, so leaders who watch only one will misread the others.

### 1.1 AI: broad capability, uneven deployment

General-purpose AI is now available to large parts of the public workforce. Commercial drafting, summarizing, writing and search tools are licensed at 10,000 or more users in each of the three main federal environmental departments (Figure F04) [8]. Capability is broadest in information work. In Microsoft's analysis of 200,000 Copilot conversations, gathering, writing and communicating were the most common and most successful AI-assisted activities [7]. Section 2 shows that deployment inside agencies, and evidence on outcomes, lag well behind capability.

### 1.2 The 2025–26 public-capacity shock

**Headcount.** The federal civilian workforce fell from 2,313,216 in September 2024 to 2,035,344 in January 2026, a net drop of about 278,000 or 12%, according to the Partnership for Public Service's analysis of OPM data [2]. The OPM Director's own tally put 2025 departures at about 317,000; that figure covers a different period and definitions, so the two should not be combined [25]. Environmental agencies were hit harder than average (Figure F01):

- The Forest Service and the National Park Service each lost more than a quarter of their employees between September 2024 and January 2026, and federal park rangers fell 34.1% [2]. USDA Inspector General data cited in the press put the Forest Service's 2025 loss at 16%, on a different base that should not be compared with the PPS figure [26].
- EPA had 12,849 employees at the end of January 2026, more than 4,000 (24%) fewer than a year earlier [3].
- The Natural Resources Conservation Service was down about 23.8% from September 2024 to November 2025 [27].

**Hiring rules.** Under Executive Order 14356 (15 October 2025), each agency's Strategic Hiring Committee approves the creation or filling of every vacancy, and agencies file annual staffing plans with quarterly updates [28]. Federal posting volume therefore reflects policy decisions more than labour demand, and EMST treats it as a policy signal [24].

**Reorganization.** Interior set up the U.S. Wildland Fire Service on 12 January 2026, unifying the fire programmes of four Interior bureaus with the Office of Wildland Fire and the Office of Aviation Services; Interior states that it employs 5,780 federal wildland fire personnel a year. Forest Service fire was not moved [29]. In March and April 2026 the Forest Service announced that it will move its headquarters to Salt Lake City, close all nine regional offices in favour of a state-based model with 15 state directors, and close 57 of its 77 research facilities, with relocations running into 2027 [30]. Reporting on the reorganization describes it as leaning heavily on states, with Good Neighbor Authority as a critical tool [31].

**Money and capacity have come apart.** Congress enacted FY2026 Interior–Environment appropriations of $42.56 billion, 1.9% below FY2025 and far above the President's request of $26.85 billion [32]. Staffing fell steeply anyway. The operative uncertainty is therefore public stewardship *capacity* (people, authority, coordination and entry pipelines) rather than dollars [J; 15].

**Entry pipelines (Figure F08).**

- The Forest Service hired no non-fire seasonal staff in 2025. On 12 February 2026 it committed to hiring 2,000 seasonals for 2026 [4].
- On the Conservation Job Board, postings fell 29.4% year over year for March–September 2025; paid internships fell 43.8% and AmeriCorps positions 42%. These figures cover one board, not the whole conservation labour market [33].
- In April 2025 AmeriCorps terminated nearly $400 million in grants, hitting conservation corps. A federal court enjoined the terminations in the 24 plaintiff states and DC on 5 June 2025, and the programmes there were reinstated; the terminations persisted elsewhere. An 11 September 2026 settlement covers FY2026 only, and the litigation is stayed until 1 February 2027 [34].
- The share of federal employees under 30 fell from 8.9% to 7.9% [2].
- Forest Service trail miles maintained fell 22% in 2025. A congressional letter citing the agency's own status report lists the skills lost: equipment operation, traditional masonry, volunteer coordination and project management [35].

**Knowledge leaving at the top.** The roughly 2,000 EPA employees who left at the end of 2025 had a median 30.3 years of service; the staff who remained had a median of 10.8 years [3].

**People moving to states.** Several states recruited displaced federal staff. New York opened a portal for laid-off federal workers in February 2025 [36]. Pennsylvania counts federal experience as equivalent in its minimum qualifications and reported 1,350 hires with federal experience by August 2026 [37]. Work is moving between sector actors faster than the people who know how to do it [J; 38].

### 1.3 Policy oscillation and devolution

Rules that shape environmental review, authorization and selection changed more in 2025–26 than in any comparable period since NEPA's original regulations [J; 39]. EMST records these changes as dated, sourced instruments, so that what each role requires can change when the rules do (Figure F09) [40, 24].

**Environmental review.**

- CEQ's government-wide NEPA regulations were removed by an interim final rule effective 11 April 2025, adopted as final effective 8 January 2026; each department now follows its own procedures [11].
- Interior's final rule of 24 February 2026 keeps a slim 43 CFR part 46 and moves most procedures into the DOI NEPA Handbook [11]. USDA consolidated its procedures in 7 CFR part 1b, effective 3 April 2026, and rescinded the Forest Service's 36 CFR part 220 [11].
- In *Seven County Infrastructure Coalition v. Eagle County* (29 May 2025), the Supreme Court's judgment was 8–0, with a five-justice majority opinion holding that courts owe agencies substantial deference on the scope of NEPA review [12].
- Since July 2025 a project sponsor can pay a fee (125% of the anticipated cost, per counsel's reading of the statute) for deadlines of 180 days for an environmental assessment and one year for an environmental impact statement. No public count of projects using it was found [41].
- The SPEED Act (H.R. 4776) passed the House 221–196 on 18 December 2025; it is pending in the Senate Environment and Public Works Committee and is not law [42]. A separate bipartisan Senate permitting bill, S. 5653, was unveiled on 30 September 2026; no committee markup or floor vote has been reported, and it is a different bill from the SPEED Act [43].
- The Forest Service's revised project-level objection rule was published as final on 13 October 2026 and takes effect on 12 November 2026 [44].

For the workforce, review work is shifting from following one government-wide set of rules to exercising judgment that must be justified in the record, under procedures that differ by agency [J; 39].

**Scope of federal regulation.**

- The rescission of the Endangered Species Act's regulatory definition of "harm" was finalized on 14 July 2026 and took effect on 14 September 2026; suits were filed in mid-July 2026 [45].
- EPA's rescission of the 2009 greenhouse gas endangerment finding and federal vehicle standards took effect on 20 April 2026; petitions for review are pending in the D.C. Circuit [46].
- There is no final "waters of the United States" rule: a proposal of 20 November 2025 was followed by a supplemental proposal published on 9 September 2026 [47].
- The Advisory Council on Historic Preservation voted on 24 July 2026 to advance a rewrite of its Section 106 regulations. The draft is under review at OMB's Office of Information and Regulatory Affairs (OIRA) and had not been published as of 9 October 2026 [48].

**Legal basis now varies by place.** Three federal executive orders directed analysis of the cumulative burdens and distributional effects of environmental decisions: EO 12898, EO 14096 and the relevant parts of EO 14008. All three were revoked in January 2025, and the Congressional Research Service notes that revocation does not change EPA's statutory responsibilities [49]. New Jersey and New York require such analysis in permitting: New Jersey's rules were affirmed by the Appellate Division on 5 January 2026 [50], and New York's siting law applies disproportionate-burden review to applicable permits, with related environmental review amendments in effect from 12 June 2026 [51]. EMST names every competency by the work performed, keeps it wherever the evidence supports it, and computes where it currently has a legal basis from the status of the instruments. The same item can therefore show "no current basis" in a federal view and "required basis" in a state view [40].

**Selection and hiring law.**

- OPM's interim final rule of 31 July 2026 removed references to the Uniform Guidelines on Employee Selection Procedures (UGESP) from OPM's hiring regulations. 5 CFR 300.103 still requires each employment practice to rest on a job analysis and a rational relationship to performance [13]. UGESP itself remains codified at 29 CFR part 1607 [52].
- The Office of Federal Contract Compliance Programs' rescission of its Executive Order 11246 regulations, including 41 CFR part 60-3, was published on 21 August 2026 and is scheduled to take effect on 26 October 2026 [53].
- Colorado's 2024 AI statute never took effect as written. SB 26-189, signed on 14 May 2026, replaces it from 1 January 2027 with a notice-and-disclosure regime for automated decision tools used in consequential decisions, including employment [54]. The Attorney General released interim draft rules on 6 October 2026, with comments and a hearing on 26 October [55].

EMST's selection-adjacent materials therefore rest on job analysis and job-relatedness, and EMST never describes them as meeting UGESP [19].

**Devolution through agreements.** Delivery is moving to states, counties and partners through agreements rather than plans; the Congressional Research Service counts 540 Good Neighbor agreements in FY2025 [56]. Statecraft demand is growing in state, local and corporate venues and in agreement-making, even as some federal regulatory scope narrows [J; 39].

### 1.4 Fire and climate: a rising, predetermined workload

As of 11 October 2026 the 2026 fire season had reached 63,884 fires and 9.0 million acres burned, 133% and 145% of the 2016–2025 average [57]. Fire is the one workload that every EMST scenario treats as rising [15]. The fire workforce is being restructured at the same time. Interior's new fire service is in place [29], and aligned federal qualification standards take effect in January 2027; the alignment is between U.S. Wildland Fire Service and Forest Service standards [58]. NWCG separately updated its position-qualification standard (PMS 310-1) on 23 January 2026 [58]. The Forest Service had 10,496 wildland firefighters on board on 11 May 2026, against a goal of 11,300 by mid-July [26].

Technology is already reshaping fire operations. In FY2024 Forest Service drones flew 4,536 hours, 78% more than in FY2023, and aerial ignition from drones treated 188,986 acres [59]. Most of those pilots are field staff who also fly: 99% of the agency's trained pilots fly as a collateral duty [60]. BLS projects 4% growth for firefighters from 2025 to 2035, but does not count wildland firefighters separately [61].

### 1.5 Markets and disclosure: compliance-grade data work

Disclosure rules are reshaping private environmental demand:

- California's Office of Administrative Law approved CARB's SB 253 regulation on 12 October 2026. First Scope 1 and 2 reports are due on 10 November 2026 from U.S. entities with more than $1 billion in annual revenue doing business in California, and Scope 3 reporting begins in 2027. SB 261 remains enjoined [14].
- The EU's Omnibus I directive entered into force on 18 March 2026. It narrows CSRD to undertakings with more than 1,000 employees and more than €450 million in turnover, with transposition due by 19 March 2027 [14].

Among 500+ sustainability professionals at firms with $1 billion or more in revenue, 46% reported headcount or budget growth over two years, against 25% reporting cuts, and 58% gave compliance a higher priority [62]. Green skills keep spreading into ordinary jobs: in LinkedIn's December 2025 stocktake, green hiring grew 7.7% against 4.3% growth in green skills, and 53% of green-skilled hires went into job titles not labelled green [63]. Clean energy, by contrast, recorded a net loss of 36,949 U.S. jobs in 2025 in E2's tally [64].

Moving from public to private work carries a pay friction that mobility tools must show honestly. In BLS wage data for May 2025, the median environmental scientist earned:

| Employer | Median annual pay |
|---|---|
| Federal government | $116,110 |
| Engineering services | $83,500 |
| Local government | $81,580 |
| State government | $80,500 |
| Consulting | $79,320 |

Source: [65]. The comparison is compositional, because federal staff are older and more senior on average. Still, a move from federal service to consulting is often a pay cut at the median unless the mover takes on sign-off or revenue responsibility [J; 66].

## 2. What the evidence says about AI and environmental work

### 2.1 Five kinds of evidence, five different questions

Public debate often treats "AI exposure" as one number. It is at least five different measurements, and each fails in a different way for environmental work [J; 6].

| Question | Best current evidence | How well it fits environmental work |
|---|---|---|
| Could AI do this task? | Capability indices built on O*NET tasks (Eloundou et al.; the "theoretical" half of the BLS categories) | Good task coverage. Blind to robotics and to sensing (drones, acoustic recorders, camera traps, eDNA), which matter most in the field |
| Is AI used for it? | Usage indices (Anthropic Economic Index; Microsoft "Working with AI") | Platforms see little government or field work; "not observed" does not mean "not used" |
| Has the employer deployed it? | OMB's Federal Agency AI Use Case Inventory | Direct evidence for federal agencies; nothing comparable for most states |
| Did employment change? | Payroll and administrative-data studies | None has an environmental cut; the main payroll dataset under-covers government |
| What will employers ask for? | Postings, position descriptions, land management plans | Usable, but EMST has not yet taken a baseline of public postings |

### 2.2 Two exposure profiles

The clearest pattern in the capability data is that environmental work is not one thing. In EMST's analysis of the Eloundou et al. task labels, environmental scientists, climate change policy analysts, sustainability specialists, planners, hydrologists and compliance officers rank among the most exposed occupations in the economy. Hazardous materials removal workers, forest and conservation workers, firefighters, water and wastewater operators and fish and game wardens rank among the least exposed [5, 6]. The common claim that environmental work is "protected by its field character" holds only for the field half [J; 6]. EMST holds the occupation-level values and will publish them once the dataset terms are recorded in its third-party licence register [67].

Three cautions apply. The indices agree broadly on how occupations rank because they share an O*NET base, but they answer different questions [6]. Low exposure to language models is not low exposure to technology: Anthropic's September 2026 study estimates that robots could technically perform about 74% of U.S. physical tasks (about 34% of working hours) but are cost-competitive for only about 0.3% of tasks [68], and sensing is measured by no general index, although species detection and remote sensing are the two largest clusters of AI in federal environmental bureaus (Figure F03) [8]. And labels for single tasks are unstable, so EMST never attaches a single external label to a task; it aggregates across matched tasks and shows the spread [23].

### 2.3 Usage: still thin, and hard to see

Environmental tasks appear in the Anthropic Economic Index's first release, but each accounts for a very small share of sampled conversations [69]. In Microsoft's usage-based measure, most environmental occupations rank well below their capability exposure. The gap is widest for roles such as foresters and forest technicians, whose analytical tasks sit inside agency systems that consumer tools do not see [J; 6]. Part of the gap is sampling, since government tenants are not sampled; part is probably real adoption lag. The data cannot yet separate the two [J; 6].

Two usage figures are often misread:

- On Claude.ai, the share of directive (automation-style) conversations was 27% in the first report, 39% in August 2025 and 32% in November 2025 [70].
- In API traffic, 77% of transcripts showed automation patterns (directive plus feedback-loop collaboration) in September 2025 [70]. That figure describes how developers call the model. It does not describe job automation or task automation.

For context, 19.8% of U.S. businesses reported using AI in the Census Bureau's survey of 3 May 2026, as did 37% of firms with 250 or more employees [71].

### 2.4 What is actually deployed in federal environmental agencies

OMB's 2025 inventory is the best direct evidence of deployment. It is self-reported, and registering a use case is not the same as using it (Figures F02–F04).

- **Scale.** Government-wide there are 3,611 individually reported use cases, 445 of them high-impact. Interior reports 247 use cases (129 deployed or piloted, none high-impact), USDA 162 (86 and 4) and EPA 29 (14 and 2) [8].
- **Environmental bureaus.** EMST counted 504 use cases in nine environmental bureaus: 232 classical machine learning, 34 computer vision and 32 generative AI. Of the 347 that report a stage, 61 are deployed. EMST counts only "deployed" here, while OMB's summary pools deployed and piloted; NOAA reported no stages [8].
- **By function.** Species and wildlife detection leads with 89 use cases (11 deployed), followed by remote sensing 63 (11), generative-AI drafting and summarizing 41 (4), water and hydrology 36 (10), forecasting 33 (2) and fire 31 (8). NEPA, environmental review and permitting has 5, none deployed [8].
- **Agentic AI.** Four agentic use cases appear in the environmental bureaus: three pilots and one pre-deployment, none deployed (Figure F02) [8].
- **Drafting tools.** Drafting, summarizing, writing and search tools are licensed at 10,000 or more users in EPA, and at 50,000 or more for some tools at Interior and USDA (Figure F04) [8].

So generic document compression is available to most of the federal environmental workforce now, while specialist substitution in environmental review is not deployed [J; 6]. The environmental AI that agencies class as high-impact is machine learning that drives payments or enforcement, and the classification turns on whether human review is "the principal basis" of the decision. Substantive human review is therefore the hinge of federal AI compliance, and it is a data-craft skill [10].

**Permitting technology.** CEQ's NEPA and Permitting Data and Technology Standard is at version 1.2 (18 August 2025). A national laboratory's PermitAI project makes a search service available to federal NEPA reviewers, and a drafting benchmark built with an AI developer has 102 test cases drawn from 19 agencies [72]. These are availability and benchmarks, not registered deployment; EMST does not publish claims about time saved per document [1].

**Governance.** OMB's April 2025 memos M-25-21 and M-25-22 set federal AI use and acquisition rules, and M-26-04 (11 December 2025) adds contract terms for procuring large language models [73]. OPM's memo of 27 August 2026 treats AI drafting of position descriptions as generally not high-impact when the responsible official independently reviews, revises and adopts the draft, and presumes rating and ranking of applicants to be high-impact [74]. NIST's AI Risk Management Framework 1.0 remains the released version; a revision is in progress with no date [75].

### 2.5 BLS projections and AI exposure categories

BLS released its 2025–35 Employment Projections on 27 August 2026. Total employment is projected to grow 3.5%, from 170.3 million to 176.2 million; data scientists 34.6%; office and administrative support −4.0%, partly from AI-enabled automation [61]. For environmental occupations (Figure F07):

- Faster than average: natural sciences managers +8.2%, environmental science and protection technicians +7%, environmental engineers +6.3%, environmental scientists +6%, conservation scientists +5.3%.
- About average: firefighters +4%, compliance officers +3.8%, zoologists and wildlife biologists +3.6%.
- Slower or declining: foresters +2.2%, forest and conservation workers −2%, water and wastewater operators −6%.

Source: [61]. These are context, not evidence of skill change, and BLS does not attribute them to AI [24].

With this release BLS began publishing AI exposure categories: four relative levels (Low, Moderate, High, Very high) for each projected occupation. "Theoretical" exposure combines Felten–Raj–Seamans, Eloundou et al. and Eisfeldt et al.; "observed" exposure combines Anthropic and Microsoft usage data. BLS says the categories are not forecasts and do not separate automation from augmentation [76]. They are a convenient official summary, not independent evidence, because they are built from the same capability and usage sources discussed above. EMST has not yet ingested the per-occupation values [24].

### 2.6 Outcome evidence: what has, and has not, been measured

- **Young workers in exposed occupations.** In ADP payroll data, employment of 22–25-year-olds in the most AI-exposed occupations was about 19% below the trend for less-exposed occupations as of June 2026 (15% at the July 2025 vintage). The adjustment came through reduced hiring, with no comparable gap for experienced workers. The authors call it descriptive, and ADP under-covers government [77].
- **Unemployment.** Anthropic's March 2026 "observed exposure" study found no systematic rise in unemployment for highly exposed workers since late 2022. Job-finding by 22–25-year-olds into the most exposed occupations fell about 14% compared with 2022, a barely significant result [78].
- **Earnings and hours.** In Denmark, chatbot users saved on average 2.8% of work hours, with precisely estimated zero effects on earnings or hours [79]. The Budget Lab at Yale reports no clear labour-market effects yet [80].
- **Gaps.** No outcome study has an environmental cut; environmental occupations are too small for reliable survey cells [J; 6]. GDPval, the best-known benchmark of AI performance on real occupational tasks, includes no environmental occupations [81].
- **Expectations.** In WEF's Future of Jobs 2025, more than 1,000 large employers expect 39% of key skills to change by 2030 and list environmental stewardship among the top ten rising skills [82]. PwC reports an average wage premium of 62% for AI skills in job ads, but only 16% in government and the public sector [83].

### 2.7 Known, partly known and unknown

| Claim | Status |
|---|---|

| Field occupations have low exposure to language models | **Known**; robotics and sensing exposure is **unmeasured** |

EMST applies a strict rule before any role's change indicator moves: two independent families of evidence must agree over two periods, a fiscal shock cannot be paired with an AI capability signal, and a named human must review the result. As of 23 October 2026 no EMST change signal has passed that rule, and every corridor role shows "no change indicator" (Figure F10) [84, 24, 85].

## 3. How roles are changing

### 3.1 Where demand is growing: what plans require

Land management plans are one of the few demand signals that state what work must be done. EMST analysed 154 non-empty plans in the open OpenLMP corpus. About 95% of the components come from Forest Service plans, all in western states, and the comparison is across plans written under different rules, not a time series (Figures F05–F06).

- **Monitoring.** Monitoring requirements average 30.7% of components in each 2012-rule plan, against 2.7% under the 1982 rule, with each plan weighted equally; weighted by components, the figures are 22.2% and 1.8% [86].
- **Content.** Newer plans mention climate change about 20 times per 1,000 components against about 2 under the older rule, data or models about 81 times against 14, and carbon about 1.4 times against 0.1; the confidence intervals do not overlap [86].
- **Coordination.** Coordination grew less than component counts suggest. Partnership touchpoints roughly doubled (11.9 to 24.9 per 1,000 components, with overlapping confidence intervals), cross-boundary touchpoints were flat (4.7 to 5.0) and state-agency touchpoints fell (6.2 to 4.3) [86].
- **Methods.** Plans name outcomes and reporting far more than field methods; remote sensing and eDNA are rarely named in monitoring questions [6].

The durable demand is therefore for indicator design, evaluation and reporting, and technology change will show up in protocols, position descriptions and postings rather than plan text [J; 6]. The corpus is narrow: water, air, waste and environmental-health work, which employ some of the largest public environmental workforces, need other requirement sources [J; 87].

### 3.2 The three corridors and the 13 priority roles

EMST v2 starts with function-defined role profiles, not job titles. Each has sector-actor variants (federal, state, local, NGO, private, academic) that add or drop tasks, authority, credential gates and frictions, and Federal Series Profiles and State Class Views are generated from them [88]. The first 13 profiles form three corridors that each cross all three crafts and end in state and federal role variants, so the NGO pilot can test whether pathways carry across employers (Figure F10) [88].

| Corridor | Roles in order | Why it matters now |
|---|---|---|
| **COR-1** Corps and field entry → technician → monitoring and data → geospatial | Conservation corps member; field resource technician; monitoring and data technician; geospatial and remote sensing analyst; ground-truth and validation steward (*emerging*) | The largest entry pathway, and the one hit hardest by the 2025 hiring halts and the uncertainty over corps grants [4, 34] |
| **COR-2** Fire operations → fuels and prescribed fire → fire intelligence | Wildland fire operations (crew to supervisor); fuels and prescribed fire specialist; fire intelligence analyst for continuous operations (*emerging*) | Rising workload, a new federal fire service and aligned qualification standards from January 2027 [57, 29, 58] |
| **COR-3** Restoration and land stewardship → review, permitting and compliance → programme management | Restoration and land stewardship project lead; environmental review specialist; environmental permit writer; environmental compliance inspector; natural resource and environmental programme manager | Review procedures changed and delivery moved toward states and agreements [11, 56] |

What EMST can and cannot say about these roles today:

- **Each role has a draft task bundle and a draft change view.** The reshaping claims in those views are agent drafts; every task shows "assessment pending" until a multi-rater assessment exists, and no role has an active change indicator [85, 89]. This report therefore states no role-level change as a finding.
- **COR-1.** Monitoring plans assume a technician layer: the monitoring programmes of two recent forest plans list GS-5 and GS-7 aquatics and range technicians alongside GS-11 specialists [9]. Environmental science and protection technicians are projected to grow 7%, while forest and conservation workers are projected to fall 2% [61]. Sensors, drones and AI triage move technician work from collecting data toward siting, servicing and checking instruments and outputs; in geospatial work, Earth-observation foundation models change the methods, not the core of the role [J; 9, 10]. The ground-truth and validation steward is listed as emerging because every scaled remote or AI product needs labels, reference plots and field checks. The named counter-signal is validation absorbed into vendor services with no agency field calibration [88, 9].
- **COR-2.** Position qualifications act as credential gates, not proficiency levels, and EMST links to those qualification systems rather than competing with them. The fire intelligence analyst is listed as emerging because the work is shifting from incident-only positions to year-round, continuous intelligence; the named counter-signal is that the function stays inside incident positions with no year-round staffing [88].
- **COR-3.** Most state environmental work sits on the regulator's side of the counter: permit writing, inspection, enforcement and delegated programmes. v1 was written mostly from the land manager's and applicant's side [J; 90]. Review specialists now work to agency-specific procedures under substantial judicial deference [11, 12], and AI drafting tools are available to them, but no NEPA or permitting AI is registered as deployed [8]. Programme managers carry the budgeting, workforce-planning and formation responsibilities that contraction makes harder [88].

This table summarizes the research syntheses. It is judgment based on the evidence above, not a measurement of any role; EMST's own task-level assessments are pending [J].

| Getting quicker (task-level time compression) | Becoming more central | New or forming (watch or proposed) |
|---|---|---|
| First drafts of analysis sections; literature and legal comparison; comment coding and theme analysis; data entry; report tables; routine map production; ESG data collection [39, 66, 15] | Specifying an analysis; validating AI output; defending a record; accountable sign-off; scoping judgment; instrument siting, service and calibration; monitoring design and evaluation; compliance-grade data, controls and assurance; agreement negotiation and administration [10, 9, 39, 66] | AI-assisted review lead; environmental AI assurance officer; permitting-technology product owner; intergovernmental stewardship agreement manager; knowledge continuity lead; GHG and nature data controller [88]. ## 4. What stays human: field craft, statecraft and the formation problem

This section describes where human performance remains the binding constraint today. It is descriptive, not a claim that certain work must stay human; where accountability keeps a task human, that is a governance choice that can change [J; 6, 89].

### 4.1 The three crafts

EMST v2 rates each task on all three crafts, so blends are visible rather than forced into one "primary craft" [16].

- **Field craft** is the capacity to perceive, measure, act and stay safe in a specific place: reading land, water, air, living systems and the engineered systems that hold, treat or move them, and producing the ground truth and physical interventions that other environmental work depends on. It is formed through repeated, supervised exposure to real conditions.
- **Data craft** is the disciplined capacity to turn observations into *warranted* evidence, models and decision support, including specifying, validating, supervising and answering for AI-generated analysis.
- **Statecraft** is the learnable capacity to turn knowledge and public purpose into decisions that are authorized, legitimate, resourced and durable. Its defining deliverable is a commitment that holds.

Role blends are computed from task ratings and are used to describe roles and design learning, not to match people to jobs [16]. Every v2 craft rating is still an agent draft awaiting the craft-rating pilot, so none is charted here [19].

### 4.2 Field craft: the field moves, it does not vanish

The strongest evidence comes from drones, which are absorbed into field roles more than they create new ones: 99% of Forest Service trained pilots fly as a collateral duty [60]. When Interior restricted drones, flights fell sharply (BLM's from 3,643 to 528 between 2019 and 2023), and monitoring of one endangered butterfly shifted to on-foot staff covering a much smaller area [91]. Adoption is gated by policy, procurement and cost more than by capability. Since 22 December 2025 the American Security Drone Act has barred using federal funds to buy, operate or maintain covered foreign-adversary-linked drones, while the FCC's Covered List action bars only new equipment authorizations [92]. The FAA's rule for flights beyond visual line of sight (Part 108) has been at OIRA since 10 July 2026 and was unpublished as of 21 October 2026 [92].

Every automated monitoring system carries a calibration cost: lidar inventories need calibration plots, classifiers need verified labels, burn-severity maps need field checks. Continuous sensors cut routine visits, so the visits that remain are for problems, calibration and judgment. That raises the skill needed per visit and removes the easy repetitions novices used to learn on [J; 9].

### 4.3 Statecraft: authority and accountability

Statecraft's deliverable is a review, permit, rule, agreement, budget or coalition that survives challenge, turnover and implementation [39]. AI touches its written medium most: drafting, comparison and summary. Accountability is where the human role concentrates; the OPM memo's test for AI-drafted position descriptions turns on whether the responsible official independently reviews, revises and adopts the draft [74]. Statecraft can be taught and assessed, but the field has codified it more than it has assessed it: EMST found no validation studies of how environmental statecraft is assessed [39].

### 4.4 Data craft: warrant, not pattern-making

Pattern-making and projection are what machines now do cheaply. The human contribution in data craft is **warrant**: a claim about the world whose provenance, method, uncertainty and fitness for a decision the practitioner can show and answer for [10, 16]. Specific models, platforms and policy memos have half-lives of one to three years, so EMST keeps durable competencies separate from fluency in particular tools and records tools in their own layer with review and retirement dates [10].

### 4.5 The formation problem

Field craft and statecraft are reproduced through graduated exposure: many seasons of varied, supervised work with feedback. Seasonal, corps, trainee and junior analyst jobs have been the sector's main supply of those repetitions [J; 9]. Contraction now removes the jobs and the supervisors together; automation then removes the easiest repetitions (grab samples, call-back surveys, plot measurements, image sorting, first drafts, comment coding). If both continue, the sector will have more data and fewer people who can tell whether it is right [J; 9]. Foresight work names a quieter version of the same risk, "validation without formation": juniors who only review AI output, without producing and defending analyses, could become seniors who cannot tell when the AI is wrong [S; 15].

EMST's response is built into the data model; the rules are provisional [93, 19]:

- **Formation tasks are flagged** in role profiles.
- **Craft balance.** No pixel without plot: every data- or AI-heavy pathway includes field ground-truth work, with 20–30% field time for remote-sensing reskilling. No model without a decision owner.
- **A by-hand quota.** Formation tasks that AI compresses require a quota of unaided products before review-and-certify credit, and every pathway with formation tasks needs a supervisor formation plan and a mentor.
- **Assessment** uses seeded-error work samples, and both AI-off and AI-on assessment where formation is at risk.
- **AI literacy** maps to the five content areas of the Department of Labor's framework: Understand AI Principles, Explore AI Uses, Direct AI Effectively, Evaluate AI Outputs and Use AI Responsibly [94].

## 5. The entry-path problem: a hypothesis, stated so it can be tested

> **Hypothesis (EMST v2; status: hypothesis, not finding).** In environmental work, the rungs on which people form field craft and statecraft are being removed by two mechanisms acting in sequence: a fiscal and policy shock that cuts entry jobs now, and AI and sensing compressing the formation tasks that remain. A third mechanism, the loss of senior reviewers, weakens the review-and-certify function that keeps AI-assisted work trustworthy. If hiring resumes without redesigned entry work, a temporary shock may become a lasting change to the career ladder.
>
> **Publication rule.** EMST materials show this hypothesis with its mechanism and evidence status. It will be published as a finding only after EMST's industrial-organizational and labour-market leads validate it, and then co-published with a nonpartisan partner [89].

| Link | Claim | Evidence | Status |
|---|---|---|---|
| L1 | Entry jobs are being removed now, by fiscal and policy drivers | No Forest Service non-fire seasonal hires in 2025 [4]; internship postings −43.8% on one board [33]; corps grants terminated, then reinstated only in plaintiff states [34]; under-30 share 7.9% [2] | **Evidenced** for federal land agencies and corps; state, local, NGO and private entry hiring **unmeasured** |
| L2 | AI and sensing compress formation tasks | Desk entry tasks are highly exposed on capability measures [6]; drafting tools are licensed at workforce scale [8]; NEPA and permitting AI is not deployed [8] | **Partly evidenced** (capability and availability); use inside agencies and outcomes **unknown** |
| L3 | Senior reviewers are leaving (top-of-ladder loss) | EPA year-end 2025 departures: median 30.3 years of service, against 10.8 for those remaining [3] | **Evidenced** for one agency |
| L4 | When hiring resumes, managers do not refill entry tasks that AI now compresses | Private analogue only: young workers in exposed occupations about 19% below trend, through reduced hiring; descriptive; government under-covered [77]. Job-finding into exposed occupations down about 14%, barely significant [78] | **No environmental evidence yet** [S] |
| L5 | By the early 2030s, fewer mid-career staff can judge AI-assisted work | Scenario reasoning [15] | **Speculation** [S] |

**Counter-mechanisms that could break the chain** [J; 6]. Saved time may go to backlog reduction under new review deadlines rather than to fewer hires, and demand may rise with fire, disclosure and devolution. Time savings so far show no measurable effect on earnings or hours [79]. States, NGOs and contractors may absorb entry roles, as some partners hired seasonals when the Forest Service did not, and entry work can be redesigned around supervised ground-truth and sensor-service roles and multi-employer apprenticeships [9].

**What would settle it.**

1. Entry-grade (GS-5/7) accessions and trainee shares in the 0400-series and related environmental occupations in 2027–28, compared with 2023–24 and conditioned on budget. OPM's Federal Workforce Data site has offered monthly data since January 2026 [95].
2. Quarterly comparisons of the duties text in entry-level postings; EMST has not yet taken the October 2026 baseline [1].
3. Time-use and AI-use data from the NGO pilot cohort.
4. Entry-cohort volume (seasonal, corps, apprentice and trainee starts) against 2024; a fall of more than 25% triggers a field-pathway warning [96].

**How EMST will compute it.** Career-ladder risk is a computed, pathway-level indicator: the share of an entry role's formation tasks assessed as compressed, supervised or substituted, adjusted when a pathway records a credible alternative formation route, with every reading tagged by its driver (AI, sensing, automation, fiscal or policy) [23, 93]. It cannot be computed until multi-rater task assessments exist, so no value is reported here.

## 6. Four futures and what to do now

### 6.1 The scenarios

EMST's foresight lead built four scenarios for 2026–2035 on the two driver clusters that are both highest-impact and most uncertain [15]:

- **Public stewardship capacity**: rebuilt and coordinated, or thin and fragmented. This is about people, authority, coordination and entry pipelines across all levels of government, not about dollars.
- **AI reliability and legitimacy in environmental decisions**: trusted and assured, or brittle and contested.

| | **AI trusted and assured** | **AI brittle and contested** |
|---|---|---|
| **Capacity rebuilt and coordinated** | **SC1 Assured Augmentation.** Headcount near 2024 levels, different mix: fewer people producing documents, more specifying and reviewing AI-assisted analysis, collecting ground truth and running continuous intelligence. Field craft becomes the bottleneck. Quiet risk: validation without formation. | **SC2 Human Premium.** After failures or court remands, AI is confined to search, summary and quality checks. ## 7. What EMST v2 is and how to use it

### 7.1 What it is

EMST v2 is an open, versioned taxonomy and a set of tools built on it. Its design responds directly to the evidence above.

- **Tasks are the spine.** Competencies earn their place by linking to critical tasks. Role profiles bundle tasks with time shares, criticality, needed-at-entry and formation flags, and competency targets [88, 19].
- **Crafts are blends rated per task**, plus two attributes that weights alone would lose: *field dependency* (must the work happen on site?) and *formation dependency* (must the expertise have been formed in the field, even if the task is now done on a screen?) [16].
- **Change is evidence, not opinion.** Capability, deployment and staffing outcome are separate constructs; change signals are dated and sourced; indicators move only under the two-family evidence rule [23, 84].
- **Lifecycle and use tiers.** Records are *watch*, *proposed*, *emerging*, *established* or *declining*. Uses are *exploratory*, *describe*, *develop* or *select*, and a record's effective tier is the lowest of its lifecycle cap, validation evidence and reliability gate. Nothing in v2.0 is at *select* [17, 19].
- **Display honesty.** Every on-screen number traces to a stored, attributed record; without a v2 multi-rater assessment a view shows "assessment pending"; v1 values appear only in a task-level evidence drawer marked "v1 single-analyst prior, LLM-assisted, unvalidated — not for decisions" [89].
- **No personal data, ever.** The public explorer is static and local-first: a gap check runs in the browser and nothing a user enters leaves the device. Kits for HR and learning systems run inside the adopter's own authorized systems. EMST never ranks, scores, screens or selects people [18].
- **The product.** A Role Change and Development Planner with four views ("How is this role changing?", "Where are the gaps?", "Where can this role lead?" (exploratory until validated) and "What's coming"), plus generated Federal Series Profiles, State Class Views and kits [89].
- **Licences.** Data and schema CC0; publications CC BY 4.0; code Apache-2.0. Third-party material keeps its own terms [67].

**The theory behind it.** The charter puts it in one sentence: "If those blends are visible and portable, mobility follows" [21]. The pilot is designed to test that sentence link by link, and the published evidence is cautious about visibility alone. In one study only 37% of firms that announced skills-based hiring followed through, and the net change affected fewer than 1 in 700 hires [97]. Colorado rewrote about 80% of its job classifications, yet its share of hires without degrees stayed near 25% [98]. EMST's product strategy therefore puts its identifiers into the documents that gate movement (position descriptions, class specifications, postings, assessments, development plans and learning records) ahead of browse-only views [96].

### 7.2 How four users could use it from the first day

**A federal HR specialist.**

- **Modernizing a PD (for example 0401 or 0460).** Open the Federal Series Profile, which assembles major duties with indicative time shares, Factor 1 knowledge statements and needed-at-entry flags from every federal role variant crosswalked to the series. Treat it as a draft for normal classification review. EMST's regenerated Forester PD (GS-0460, FES format) shows the pattern and is labelled "subject to classification review" [99, 88]. OPM's August 2026 memo treats AI-assisted PD drafting as generally not high-impact when the responsible official independently reviews, revises and adopts the draft [74].
- **Assessments.** Use EMST task lists as the starting point for the job analysis that 5 CFR 300.103 requires [13], and toward the technical assessments the Chance to Compete Act calls for. Since 30 September 2025, self-assessment questionnaires may be used only for minimum qualifications, not rating and ranking. In FY2025, 91.23% of delegated-examining announcements relied solely on self-report questionnaires; in FY2026 Q2, 34.3% used technical or off-the-shelf assessments [100].
- **Timing.** OPM's plan places Natural Resources Management in Tier 2 with no completion date [100], and OPM has so far issued competency-based classification and qualification standards only for series 2210 [101]. Its April 2026 list of series *proposed* for realignment (for example 0455 into 0404) had no final decision as of 23 October 2026. EMST records the proposal as proposed and keeps task-level links, so a PD can be written either way [102].
- **Staffing plans.** Under EO 14356, use formation flags and protect suggestions to justify critical entry and knowledge-transfer positions [28, 17].

**A state training officer.**

- **Credit federal experience.** Use the State Class View to see how a federal role variant maps to your class plan, to credit federal experience as Pennsylvania does through equivalency, and to design onboarding for transferred staff [37, 88].
- **Build curricula for regulator-side roles.** Permit writers and inspectors are corridor roles with draft task bundles at full depth [88].
- **Short programmes.** Workforce Pell can fund programmes of 150–599 clock hours over 8 to fewer than 15 weeks from 1 July 2026; governors, working with state workforce boards, identify the in-demand occupations [103]. WIOA has not been reauthorized [103].
- **Gap checks.** Run the local-first gap check with your team; ratings stay in a file on your own machine [18].

**An NGO programme lead (the first pilot user).**

- Use "How is this role changing?" for the corps member and field technician roles to plan a season's training and a crew member's development plan.
- Write job descriptions and grant reports that cite EMST role and competency identifiers, so a state or federal employer can read them.
- Design ground-truth and sensor-service work as supervised learning tasks, so the formation they provide shows in a portable record [93].
- The pilot runs from March 2027 to February 2028 with about 12 organizations across the three corridors. Use is limited to describing and developing; nothing ranks, screens or selects anyone [104].

**An educator.**

- Map courses to EMST tasks and competencies through generic course learning outcomes, keeping field ground-truth components and an accountable decision owner in programmes that lead into data- and AI-heavy work [93].
- Align AI literacy to the Department of Labor's five content areas [94].
- Plan for updates: O*NET 31.0 (August 2026) is current, and the 2028 SOC is planned for finalization in 2027 [105, 106]. EMST claims no alignment with O*NET, ESCO, OPM, state or accreditation frameworks until a reviewed mapping set exists [89].

### 7.3 What EMST will not do

- **No decisions about people.** EMST will not rank, score, screen or select people, or support reduction-in-force, adverse action or performance ratings [18].
- **No partisan content.** EMST supplies no essay content and no help with political position designations [40].
- **No unvalidated pathway advice.** No pathway is labelled "recommended" until it predicts observed moves (AUC of at least 0.70 and at least 0.05 better than naive baselines) [96].
- **No Tribal content** without approval from a Tribal Advisory Council (section 8.4) [22].

## 8. Limits, validation and what comes next

### 8.1 Agent drafts versus validated content

Everything EMST itself has rated so far was drafted by AI agents: task bundles, time shares, craft ratings, change claims, signal directions and driver tags. Each is recorded with its rater type, a frozen model and prompt version, `validationStatus = draft` and evidence label S [19]. No human has reviewed these values, and no change indicator has moved. This report uses them only to describe structure, never as findings [1].

### 8.2 Evidence gaps

- **No posting baseline yet.** The October 2026 snapshot of federal and state postings was not taken, so EMST makes no claim about posting volume or content [1].
- **Data not yet ingested.** The OPM Federal Workforce Data series pull is pending; BLS per-occupation AI exposure categories and Anthropic Economic Index occupation files are not ingested [24].
- **Outcomes and coverage.** No outcome study has an environmental cut [J; 6]. State, local, NGO and private workforce data are thin, and the plan corpus is mostly western Forest Service plans [24, 87].
- **Licence terms.** Values from two external indices are withheld until their terms are recorded [67].
- **Tribal workforce.** National data and EMST do not measure it [22].

### 8.3 Validation with people, from 2027

- **Reliability studies** of 120–200 units rated by six raters. Krippendorff's α is decided on the lower bound of its confidence interval: at least .80 for production use, .667–.80 shown as tentative, below .667 not shown [19].
- **A pre-registered modified Delphi** with 15–25 members per panel, exact Lawshe critical values and a hard stop after three rounds. Panels correct for contraction bias by over-recruiting thinned public field and statecraft strata, including recently separated incumbents and capping any sector actor at 30% [19].
- **Job analysis** with partner agencies, using matrix-sampled surveys, for five first clusters: field monitoring and inventory; compliance, permitting and review; geospatial and data analysis; water quality and water operations; and a natural-resources series starter pack [19].
- **Cognitive interviews** (about 10–15), and **AI-assisted tagging only behind a gold-set gate**: an LLM is never the sole coder of a value relevant to selection [19].

### 8.4 The Tribal Advisory Council and the content hold

EMST states on its face that Indigenous knowledge is a distinct body of environmental expertise held and governed by Indigenous nations, and that it is out of scope by design [22]. EMST does not define, describe, rank or credential Indigenous knowledge; holds no confidential Tribal information; publishes no characterization of a named Tribe without consent; does not ingest Tribal employers' documents by default; and creates no Tribal profession family [22].

Until a Tribal Advisory Council constituted by Tribal governments and inter-Tribal organizations exists and approves, nothing Tribal-related is published. The project lead has accepted binding consent authority for that Council over the inclusion, wording and release of Tribal-related content; a charter carve-out so that neither the Chief Taxonomy Architect nor the Editorial Board can override the Council in that category, with "withhold" as the default; and compensation for Tribal reviewers budgeted before first contact [107, 22]. Forming the Council is not EMST's to do, and no outreach has been made [22]. This report contains no Tribal content.

## Methods and sources

**How this report was built.** It was drafted under work package WP-14 from EMST's accepted decisions, research syntheses and change-signal and instrument records. Every external fact was checked against the V1–V4 verification key facts, the corrections register and the WP-15 claims register of 23 October 2026, whose `do_not_say` wording was applied throughout [1]. Facts from sources that a role lead read directly but no verification pass re-checked are marked "read by role lead" in the references and flagged for WP-15 in `claims_used.csv`. EMST-derived figures can be reproduced from repository data.

**Moving facts.** Re-read before any external release: the Colorado rules hearing and the OFCCP effective date (26 October 2026), Section 106 after ACHP's November meeting, the Forest Service objection rule (12 November 2026), the SPEED Act and S. 5653 in the Senate, Part 108, and all other register rows by 15 December 2026 [1].

**Figures.** F01–F10 are CSV datasets in `figures/`, built by `figures/build_figures.py`, which checks every hard-coded value against its verification record and fails if a record changes. `figures/figures_index.csv` gives each figure's headline, suggested chart form, source note, use tier and evidence label. Charts carry a data table and a text message, with no hover-only information or colour-only encoding [89]. Two figures were deliberately not built: the exposure-profile chart, until the index terms are recorded, and a craft-blend chart by role, because every craft value is an agent draft or a v1 value [67, 19].

> **Methodology history: EMST v1 (2025–26).** EMST v1 was built by one analyst with LLM assistance. Its database holds 222 competencies in 7 domains and 45 skill areas, and it classified 666 tasks on five dimensions (automation type, tacit density, career-ladder position, technology vector and horizon). v1 documents cite other totals too; the database is treated as authoritative [109, 6]. v2 keeps v1's central concepts, especially career-ladder risk and the three crafts, but retires v1's statistics as findings. No tag had reliability evidence, and several dimensions were partly inferred from one another [110, 6]. An internal re-check found 11 of 222 career-ladder flags contradicting v1's own rule [110]. A claimed pattern that "human-essential" calls arise only from field-craft or statecraft competencies is contradicted by the v1 database [6]. Earlier public claims of validation against OPM and O*NET classification systems, of accredited competency standards and of headline shares of "human-essential" competencies are withdrawn [89]. A v2 pilot calibrating 60 v1 tasks against O*NET found no O*NET analogue for 30% of them, many of them new kinds of work, and, where two O*NET tasks plausibly matched, human labels that disagreed in 13 of 21 cases; it was a single-analyst pilot pending a second coder [6]. All v1 values migrate into v2 as labelled prior evidence, shown only in a task-level evidence drawer [23, 89]. Under the project lead's decision, no other v1 statistic appears in this report [107].

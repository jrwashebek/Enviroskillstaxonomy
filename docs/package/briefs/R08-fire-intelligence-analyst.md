## Who does this work

EMST expects this to become a **year-round role**. Fire intelligence analysts fuse satellite, camera, sensor, weather and
resource data into operational fire intelligence. They adjudicate automated detections, predict spread with stated
confidence, track forecast skill, and brief duty officers and incident leaders so that resources are ordered on
evidence. Much of this work is done today inside incident positions, such as fire behavior and long-term analysts, and
in predictive services.

**Counter-signal recorded with the role:** the function may stay inside incident positions with no year-round staffing.
EMST's foresight analysis (a desk exercise by an AI agent, not yet reviewed by a human panel) lists funding
fire-intelligence cohorts as a contingent move: hold until agencies ingest satellite fire-detection data on known access
terms, because acting earlier risks lock-in to a data product whose licensing is unknown [J].

## What stays with people, and why

This describes where the work sits today. The role exists to put a person between an automated detection and a resource
order. The draft's core judgments are whether a detection is real, whether a forecast system's skill is still good
enough for the decision it serves, when to restrict or retire it, and how confident a briefing should sound. Each is a
judgment about consequences and accountability. The bundle also keeps a field link: escalating AI output that conflicts
with field evidence, and field-checking automated perimeters and fuels products. The role joins data craft with field
craft.

## Entry paths and formation

People arrive from fire operations or geospatial analysis. The formation provisions in EMST's draft reskilling pathway
protect the judgment the role depends on. Learners make **five hand-worked spread and intensity predictions** checked
against observed behavior, and must explain their errors **before modeled outputs are credited**. They then work a
seeded-error detection set in which both false alarms and misses must be caught, and field-check automated products on
three incidents or burns. The formation threat is scarce detail and duty-officer slots after the 2025–26 staffing
losses: the Forest Service and the National Park Service each lost more than a quarter of their employees between
September 2024 and January 2026 [4].

## Pathways in and out (exploratory, not recommended)

- **In, wildland fire operations → this role (reskill):** five steps, covering behavior prediction compared with
  observation, fusing and adjudicating detections, incident maps and forecast systems (tool modules expire), field
  checks ("no pixel without plot"), and briefing confidence to incident leaders and the public ("no model without a
  decision owner"). EMST's curriculum draft estimates about 300 hours over 18 months, including one to two fire seasons
  [J]. Replayed historical incidents in simulation cover rare large-fire events.
- **In (role link, no pathway drafted yet):** from geospatial and remote sensing analyst.
- **Out:** none recorded; the role is too new.

None of these has been tested against observed career moves.

## What you can do now

- **Supervisor or duty officer.** Write down who adjudicates automated detections before resources are ordered, and what
  they check. Track detection and spread-prediction accuracy over a season and report systematic errors. Keep
  hand-worked predictions as a periodic check on models.
- **Training officer.** Use the operations-to-intelligence sequence to build the function from inside operations staff.
  Combine duty-officer shadowing in season with replayed incidents in simulation out of season. Give forecast-system
  modules an expiry date.
- **HR specialist.** No series fits, so describe the duties within existing series positions, labeled subject to
  classification review, and record the gap. EMST's foresight analysis suggests waiting for the data-access trigger
  above before creating standing cohorts [J].
- **Educator.** Teach spread prediction by hand before models (five hand-worked predictions checked against observed
  behavior, with errors explained), and use seeded-error detection sets in which both false alarms and misses must be
  caught.

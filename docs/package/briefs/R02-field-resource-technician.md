## Who does this work

Field resource technicians collect vegetation, wildlife, water and soil data to protocol. They identify organisms, keep
sample custody and records intact, and check remote and AI-generated products against the ground. It is the largest
field entry role and the main place where people learn identification and measurement judgment.

| | |
|---|---|
| **Employer types** | **Federal:** biological science, forestry, range and soil conservation technicians on federal land (resource manager). **State:** fish, wildlife and natural resource technician ladders. **Local:** special districts. **NGO:** land trusts. **Private:** consulting field technicians, who also write site safety plans and bill against agreements (service provider). |
| **Typical titles** | Biological science technician; natural resource technician; field technician |
| **Federal series** | 0404 Biological Science Technician, 0462 Forestry Technician and 0458 Soil Conservation Technician (each a more specific series; active). 0455 Range Technician is active under its current code and **proposed** for realignment to 0404; the proposal is not final [1]. Indicative band: GS-3 to GS-5 at entry, GS-6 to GS-7 at journey. This is not a classification criterion. |
| **State class examples** | Texas Fish and Wildlife Technician I–III (close fit); Washington Natural Resource Specialist 1 (broader class). EMST read these jurisdictions' public class plans; no agency has been involved. |

## What stays with people, and why

This describes where the work sits today. Almost all of it happens in the field. What the role adds is a defensible
observation: the right plot, the right sample, a documented identification and an intact chain of custody. The
agent-drafted notes put the new AI-related work on top of field skill rather than in place of it. Only someone who can
identify a species can judge an AI's suggestion, and only someone at the site can say why a map and the ground
disagree (timing, mixed pixels, hidden understory, location error). The role draws mainly on field craft, with a
growing share of data craft.

## Entry paths and formation

People learn this work through seasons of supervised fieldwork: plots, surveys, identification, water sampling and
navigation. The formation provisions in EMST's draft pathways protect that learning by holding AI aids back until
unaided skill is shown. In the corps-to-technician pathway, **50 unaided identifications** are checked by an experienced
observer and **10 plots** are established under supervision before AI identification aids are credited. The risk is
that seasonal and internship openings are scarce at the same time as automated detection could compress the repetitions
people learn from [2, 3]. EMST's foresight analysis (a desk exercise by an AI agent, not yet reviewed by a human panel)
describes the same risk. It judges that protecting a minimum entry cohort, and redesigning entry work so juniors still
produce and field-check their own work, holds up in all four of its scenarios [J].

## Pathways in and out (exploratory, not recommended)

- **In:** from conservation corps member (five steps; EMST's curriculum draft estimates about 240 hours over 12 months
  [J]).
- **Out, upskill → monitoring and data technician:** observed sensor installations; manual meter readings at every
  service visit as the reference for sensors; one month of automated flags reviewed by hand before automated flagging is
  credited. About 160 hours over 6 months [J].
- **Out, reskill → geospatial and remote sensing analyst:** teaches only the gap between the two profiles and keeps a
  field calibration practicum at 20–30% of pathway time. Two reference tiles are interpreted by hand before automated
  classification is credited. About 320 hours over 9 months. A community-college certificate may fit Workforce Pell
  where a governor-identified in-demand occupation applies [5; J]. The pathway notes that it "must not be used to stop
  hiring field entrants".
- **Out, upskill → ground-truth and validation steward (a watch role: hypothesized work, not yet observed in postings
  or position descriptions):** reference-data design, observer calibration,
  fitness-for-use decisions. About 200 hours over 12 months [J].

None of these has been tested against observed career moves.

## What you can do now

- **Supervisor.** Keep unaided identification and plot work in every new technician's season before you introduce
  classifiers. When a crew accepts or rejects an AI identification, have them record the diagnostic reason. Use blind
  re-measurement of a crew sample as a routine check.
- **Training officer.** Build seeded-error drills (an identification set or a field product with planted errors) into
  the season. Give modules on specific tools an expiry date. Use the corps-to-technician and technician-to-monitoring
  sequences as drafts to calibrate.
- **NGO program lead.** Write season plans, grant reports and members' exit portfolios that cite EMST role and
  competency identifiers, so a state or federal employer can read the identification and plot work a technician has
  done.
- **HR specialist.** For state positions, compare your class specification with the explorer's State Class Views for
  Texas Fish and Wildlife Technician I–III, the close-fit classes for this role, as a starting point subject to
  classification review. The explorer has no Federal Series Profile for this role in this release: its federal series
  rows are more specific series, which the profiles leave out, so the series pages list the role only among unreviewed
  agent candidates (not an alignment). Write federal duties from the role's draft task list in the explorer. For 0455
  positions, plan
  around the series as it stands: realignment to 0404 is proposed, not decided [1]. Any selection use needs your own
  job analysis under 5 CFR 300.103 [6].
- **Educator.** Build the by-hand quotas into field courses: unaided identifications and supervised plots before
  students use AI identification aids, and a field calibration practicum in any course that leads into remote sensing.

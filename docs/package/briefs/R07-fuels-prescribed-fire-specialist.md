## Who does this work

Fuels and prescribed fire specialists assess fuels, design and supervise fuels treatments, write and execute burn plans,
and make go/no-go decisions. They also plan smoke management with air regulators, evaluate fire effects and treatment
effectiveness, and work with landowners and partners across ownerships.

## What stays with people, and why

This describes where the work sits today. The decisive tasks happen on the ground and are tied to internal
qualifications: the go/no-go decision from a test fire, holding checks before ignition, halting an aerial ignition when
the firing pattern departs from plan, and recommending a stop when a smoke forecast fails against monitors and observers.
The agent-drafted notes keep **prescription judgment** and **receptor judgment** with the specialist even where drafting
and forecasting improve. Accountability for a prescribed fire sits with a qualified person on the ground. The role joins
field craft with the statecraft of working across ownerships and with air regulators.

## Entry paths and formation

People learn burn planning and go/no-go judgment as trainees on qualifying prescribed fires under a qualified burn
boss. Burn windows control the calendar, which is why the exploratory pathway runs 24 months [J]. Tabletop go/no-go
exercises and burn-plan reviews can cover burn-window shortfalls, but they "do not replace observed burns". The
formation risk is fewer qualifying burns and fewer qualified evaluators at the same time. Template-drafted burn plans
add a second risk: a trainee who never writes a prescription from first principles. EMST's foresight analysis (a desk
exercise by an AI agent, not yet reviewed by a human panel) suggested treating prescribed fire as a competency to
protect [J].

## Pathways in and out (exploratory, not recommended)

- **In, wildland fire operations → this role (upskill):** six steps, covering burn plans with two fuels maps verified in
  the field, smoke management and public messaging, go/no-go as a trainee on qualifying burns, monitoring treatment
  effectiveness, funding and delivering projects with partners, and evaluating burn crew trainees. EMST's curriculum
  draft estimates about 240 hours over 24 months [J]. Prior learning credited: wildland fire position qualifications; a
  state prescribed burn manager certification where one exists.
- **In (role link, no pathway drafted yet):** from restoration project lead.
- **Out:** a pathway to county wildfire planning is registered in EMST's role set but not yet drafted.

None of these has been tested against observed career moves.

## What you can do now

- **Supervisor or burn boss.** When a burn plan section is template- or AI-drafted, have the trainee rewrite the
  prescription parameters and contingency triggers from the unit's own fuels and objectives, and record the
  differences. Make field verification of fuels maps part of every unit's preparation.
- **Training officer.** Count qualifying burns per trainee per season and plan tabletop go/no-go exercises to cover
  shortfalls, without crediting them as burns. Add smoke-forecast-versus-monitor comparisons to after-action reviews.
- **HR specialist.** No series fits closely, so draft duties from the explorer's 0401 and 0456 Federal Series Profiles,
  subject to classification review. Do not present qualification or licensure gates from EMST as fact until they are
  checked against current standards. Aligned U.S. Wildland Fire Service and Forest Service qualification standards take
  effect in January 2027 [6].
- **Educator.** Have students write a prescription and contingency triggers from a unit's own fuels data before they
  see a template, and verify a fuels map in the field.

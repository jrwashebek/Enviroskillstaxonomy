## 1. Summary of findings

1. **v1 equates "private sector" with corporate sustainability and finance (F14/F15). Most private environmental jobs are technical field and data work.** In BLS data, consulting plus architectural/engineering services employ about 34% of environmental scientists and specialists (27,410 of 80,730). That is more than state government (21,760) [E: PS-014]. CM's analysis places F14/F15 in the statecraft-heavy "policy" community [E: PS-030]. So a v1-based mobility map would wrongly suggest that moving into the private sector means moving into statecraft. Private context has to attach to every function, not to two families. [J]

2. **For technical roles, private pay is not higher at the median; it is more spread out.** Environmental scientists' May 2025 medians:

   | Employer | Median |
   |---|---|
   | Federal government | $116,110 |
   | Engineering services | $83,500 |
   | Local government | $81,580 |
   | State government | $80,500 |
   | Consulting | $79,320 |

   [E: PS-015] Consulting's *mean* ($90,290) is above the state mean ($75,990) [E: PS-014; reference period not shown on that page]. A higher mean with a lower median points to a right-skewed private distribution. My reading is that private employers pay a premium for **advancing into revenue, client and sign-off responsibility**, and for working in particular industries: petroleum manufacturing ($143,450 mean), natural gas distribution ($131,650) and pipelines ($128,130) [E: PS-014]. They do not pay a premium for the technical craft itself [J]. NAEM's 2023 survey found that in-house EHS professionals average $13,617 more than consulting/service respondents [E: PS-016]. Senior corporate sustainability pay is in a different market: Trellis 2024 reports average total compensation of $244,649 for directors and $471,933 for VPs at firms with more than $1B in revenue [E: PS-011]. By 2026 that market had cooled, with slower growth and slightly lower senior pay [E: PS-010].

3. **Displaced federal staff moving into consulting would often take a pay cut, not a raise.** The median comparison above is compositional. Federal staff are older, higher-graded and more often in high-locality areas, so the gap is not a like-for-like premium [J]. Still, it contradicts v1's framing that private "market-premium niches" draw people out of agencies [E: PS-028 vs PS-015]. I could not find evidence on the scale of 2025–26 federal-to-private movement (§11). The mobility map should show **translation costs**: pay direction, licensure gaps, utilization pressure, and the switch from regulator posture to applicant posture.

4. **Private employers hire on four things the taxonomy barely represents:**
   - **(a) Sign-off authority** (licensure or legal signatory status): PE, PG, CIH, the federal "Environmental Professional" definition for due diligence, accredited verifier, certifying official.
   - **(b) Entry-gate credentials**, such as HAZWOPER, ISA and CCA. 73% of NAEM respondents hold at least one certification [E: PS-016].
   - **(c) Regulatory-program experience**, such as Section 404, Title V, CERCLA and FERC licensing.
   - **(d) At senior levels, business practice**: clients, proposals, billable delivery and profit-and-loss responsibility.

   v1's 222 Private Sector notes name a role 4 times and a credential 0 times. Of the 222 Consulting notes, 38 mention clients, billing or proposals [E: PS-026].

5. **Corporate sustainability has shifted from strategy and narrative to compliance-grade data, controls and assurance.** Teams reporting to legal doubled, and ESG Controller roles appeared at more than half of the Fortune 100 [E: PS-012]. 58% of large companies raised compliance priority while 63% scaled back external communications [E: PS-010]. The 2026 regulatory picture reinforces this [E]:
   - **California SB 253** is in force. First Scope 1–2 reports are due 10 Nov 2026. Limited assurance applies from 2026 and reasonable assurance from 2030. Scope 3 reporting starts in 2027 [PS-001, PS-003].
   - **California SB 261** is enjoined, and the Ninth Circuit has not ruled [PS-001].
   - **The SEC's climate rules** never took effect, and rescission was proposed on 29 May 2026 [PS-009].
   - **The EU CSRD** now covers only companies with more than 1,000 employees *and* more than €450M turnover, roughly a 90% cut in scope, and reasonable assurance was dropped [PS-004, PS-005]. The revised ESRS are mandatory from 2027 [PS-006].
   - **ISSB:** over 45 jurisdictions are on the adoption path [PS-007; S&P counts 35 adopted plus 11 planned, PS-008].

   v1 3.6 is built on TCFD, the SEC rule, blockchain and IoT. It has no controls or assurance content.

6. **AI's private entry-level effect: [J], partly corroborated.** The junior work private employers have used as apprenticeship is exactly what AI compresses: drafting report sections, compiling tables, records review, map production and ESG data collection. Vendors already pitch ESG software as a replacement for interns and consultants [E: PS-023, vendor view]. v1's own exposure instrument flagged ESG data collection as a career-ladder-destruction point [E: PS-029]. The likely result is fewer staff scientists and analysts per project manager, field technician work becoming the main on-ramp, a reviewer shortage in 5–8 years, and pressure on time-and-materials billing [J/S]. Liability-bearing steps (stamp, signature, certification, site visit, independence judgment) are where human expertise stays essential [J].

## 2. Method and source choices

**Plan.** The work had five parts:
1. Verify the 2026 regulatory status that drives private ESG hiring.
2. Get the best available pay data (official first, then professional-body surveys, then vendor or crowd data).
3. Map job structures by employer type.
4. Collect AI evidence.
5. Build the translation table against v1 codes and assess v1.

Parts 3 and 4 were cut short by the search budget.

**Why these sources.**
- *Regulatory status:* recent law-firm alerts and Big Four technical publications (PS-001 to PS-006, PS-009), plus the standard-setter's own tracker (PS-007). I read more than one source per regime because the dates moved during 2026. For example, CARB's first SB 253 deadline moved from 10 Aug to 10 Nov 2026, so PS-002 is superseded [E: PS-002, PS-003].
- *Pay:* BLS OEWS/OOH is the only probability-sample source, and the only one that separates employers by industry (PS-014, PS-015). Trellis is the longest-running US corporate sustainability survey (since 2010), but its sample is recruited from its own readers and clients at firms with more than $1B in revenue, and it reports no response rate (PS-010, PS-011). NAEM is the main in-house EHS benchmark, but it is paywalled and only headline figures are public (PS-016). OneStop ESG (PS-013) and PayScale (PS-020) are low quality and used only as cross-checks.
- *Method appraisal, in brief:*
  - BLS medians by industry mix grade, age and location, so they are not like-for-like premiums.
  - Trellis is biased toward engaged professionals at large firms. Its 2024 VP average ($471,933, PS-011) cannot be reconciled with the trade-press figure for California VPs ($365,667, PS-012), so measures probably differ.
  - OneStop fills cells with fewer than 30 responses from scraped sites, and its manager and director ranges overlap heavily. Its "12–18% carbon accounting premium" has no published method.
  - NAEM's 2026 AI benchmark is sponsored by five EHS software vendors (PS-018), so expect adoption optimism.

**Outdated or poor-fit sources flagged.**
- IEMA's State of the Profession figures that I could reach are from 2017 (PS-021). IEMA has since rebranded as ISEP, and the current pages were blocked.
- NAEM's latest salary edition is from 2023 [E: PS-017].
- TCFD (folded into ISSB) and the SEC climate rule are outdated anchors in v1 [E: PS-009, PS-007].

## 3. Job structures by employer type

`private_job_frameworks.csv` holds 88 rows across 14 employer types. Each row gives level, credentials, a craft-weight vector, a pay signal and an AI signal. Pay signals are labelled [E] in 13 rows and [J] in the rest. Below are the structural features that matter for the taxonomy, rather than a repeat of the file.

### 3.1 Environmental consulting and engineering (large and mid-size)

**Ladder.** The typical progression is Field Technician → Staff Scientist/Engineer I–II → Project Scientist → Senior Scientist (technical track) or Project Manager (management track) → Principal / Practice Leader / VP. Many firms keep a parallel technical-fellow track [J]. What decides advancement is not years but **what you are accountable for**: tasks, then a task budget, then a project and its client, then a practice and its revenue. That maps more naturally to CA's level-of-responsibility hypothesis (DP-4) than to v1's tenure bands [J].

**Distinctive features with no public analog** [J]:
- **Utilization.** Billable hours as a share of total hours is the core performance metric for juniors.
- **The "seller-doer" model.** Senior technical staff are expected to win the work they deliver.
- **Project financials.** Estimate-at-completion, change orders and margin.
- **Sign-off.** PE/PG stamps, the "Environmental Professional" opinion in due diligence, and permitted-biologist status.
- **Subcontractor management.**

**Credentials that act as hiring screens.** HAZWOPER 40-hr (field entry), EIT/GIT then PE/PG, PWS, CWB, CEP, CPESC/QSD, the Secretary of the Interior's qualification standards for archaeologists, and permits held personally for listed-species surveys. Federal contractors also value FAR fluency and, for federal work, the FAC-COR credential [J].

**Posture.** Consultants do the applicant-side mirror of agency work. They write the biological assessment the agency reviews, the 404 application the Corps decides, the remedial design EPA oversees, and EIS chapters under agency direction (§6) [J].

### 3.2 Electric and water utilities

**Electric utilities** employ field-craft-heavy workforces, often through contractors:
- Vegetation pre-inspectors and work planners (ISA arborist, line-clearance safety).
- Utility foresters, accountable to reliability and vegetation standards.
- **Wildfire mitigation analysts and program managers.** This is a distinct function that grew out of California's wildfire-mitigation planning and is spreading to other western utilities.
- Environmental compliance, transmission siting, avian protection and hydro-relicensing specialists.

**Water utilities.** Operator certification is **identical across public and private ownership**. This makes it the cleanest credential bridge in the sector [J]. v1 has no treatment-operations competency [E: CA audit §6 confirms the gap].

**Ownership is not sector.** Utilities can be municipal, special-district or investor-owned. Ownership is an attribute; "utility" is an industry (see §9).

### 3.3 Energy developers

**Renewables and transmission.** Roles include permitting managers, siting/GIS analysts, development managers, land agents, community relations managers, and wildlife leads (eagle and bat issues for wind; desert species for solar).

- **Volatility.** Hiring is volatile after 2025 changes to federal tax credits and federal-land review [S: verify with SC/LM; not evidenced here].
- **Transferable public experience.** NEPA, ESA and BLM/USFS right-of-way experience carries over directly [J].

**Oil & gas, midstream and CCS.** These offer the clearest private pay premium for environmental scientists [E: PS-014]. Roles include EHS coordinators, methane and GHG-reporting specialists, pipeline compliance managers, reclamation specialists, and Class VI carbon-storage permitting and MRV specialists.

### 3.4 Forest products, timberland and certification

**Roles.** Forest technicians and cruisers, area foresters, procurement foresters (stumpage negotiation and logger relations), biometricians, timberland investment analysts (REIT and TIMO valuation), certification managers (SFI/FSC), independent consulting foresters, and certification auditors.

**Credentials.** SAF Certified Forester and state licensing carry across public and private employers [J; v1 Structural Conditions notes SAF and state licensing, PS-028].

**v1 gaps.** No valuation, procurement or certification-conformance competency.

### 3.5 Agriculture and food

**Roles.**
- Field agronomists (Certified Crop Adviser; often sales-linked pay).
- Sustainability-sourcing and regenerative-program managers (Scope 3 FLAG reduction, grower programs).
- Agricultural carbon and MRV specialists.
- Water-stewardship managers.
- **NRCS-certified Technical Service Providers.** This is an explicit federal certification of private conservation planners, and a ready-made public–private bridge [J].

### 3.6 Carbon and nature markets

Roles are splitting into four segments [J]:
- **Origination:** landowner contracts.
- **Methodology and integrity science:** baselines, additionality, leakage, permanence.
- **dMRV engineering:** remote sensing, ML and uncertainty.
- **Assurance:** validation and verification bodies.

Raters, registries and buyers add rating-analyst and program-officer roles.

**Mitigation and conservation banking** under CWA 404 and the ESA is the mature, large US market. v1 6.6.2 underweights it in favor of emerging "biodiversity credits" [J].

**Verification demand.** California's assurance timeline raises demand for GHG verifiers [E: PS-001, PS-003]. CARB has not yet set provider qualifications; it lists independence and no self-review as attributes [E: PS-003].

### 3.7 Corporate sustainability / ESG (in-house)

**Ladder.** Analyst → Manager → Director/VP → CSO.

**New and growing roles:**
- **ESG Controller** [E: PS-012].
- **Carbon accounting and Scope 3 manager.** Reported premium of 12–18% [E, low quality: PS-013].
- **Climate risk analyst** (IFRS S2 scenario analysis).
- **LCA/product sustainability specialist.**
- **EPR and product-stewardship compliance manager** [J; demand driven by state law].
- **Nature/biodiversity lead.** The ISSB nature-related draft is expected by October 2026 [E: PS-008].

**Pay:**
- Trellis 2024 average total compensation: manager $159,430, director $244,649, VP $471,933 [E: PS-011].
- OneStop North America base ranges: analyst $55k–$130k, CSO $165k–$420k+ [E, low quality: PS-013].
- PayScale specialist median: about $70k (n=100) [E, low quality: PS-020].

**Credentials.** Credential stacking stands in for a unified profession [E: PS-028]: GRI certification (most cited in PS-013), IFRS FSA, CFA sustainable-investing certificate, GARP SCR, and CPA for controller roles.

**Team size.** Many teams are one or two people [E, vendor view: PS-023]. This is why software and consultants substitute for junior hires.

### 3.8 In-house EHS

**Ladder.** EHS Specialist → Environmental Engineer / EHS Manager → Director/VP.

**Specialists.** Industrial hygienist (CIH), EHS auditor (CPEA, ISO lead auditor), and EHS systems/data analyst.

**Pay and credentials.** In-house earns more than consulting [E: PS-016], and credentials are pervasive [E: PS-016].

**v1 gap.** v1 has no EHS family. Its compliance content (6.1.2) is assessment-only, and its safety content (4.4) is oriented to wildland crews [E: PS-025].

### 3.9 Assurance, finance/insurance, and environmental tech

- **Assurance.** Big Four and specialist assurance practices.
- **Finance and insurance.** ESG investment analysts (a demand signal is softening: UK investors considering ESG fell from 66% to 48% between 2021 and 2024 [E: PS-024]), catastrophe and climate risk modelers, and environmental due-diligence analysts for lenders and private-equity/infrastructure investors.
- **Startups.** Product managers, in-house regulatory and domain experts (a visible destination for former regulators [J]), solutions engineers, and field sensor leads.

### 3.10 Cross-employer pattern in the craft weights

Averaging my craft-weight judgments across the 88 rows gives a pattern for IO to test with real elicitation [J]:
- **By level:** FC 0.38 / DC 0.42 / SC 0.21 at Entry, shifting to FC 0.03 / DC 0.23 / SC 0.73 at Principal/Exec.
- **By employer:** utilities, timberland and agriculture weight field craft most (FC 0.36–0.43). Corporate sustainability weights it least (FC 0.03).

**The private ladder converts field craft into statecraft as people advance.** The statecraft is of a particular kind: client, revenue and regulator-negotiation statecraft. That is consistent with v1's finding that relational and accountability work stays human-essential [E: PS-029]. It also implies a field-to-office drain inside firms that mirrors the agency one. Who keeps field craft alive past year 8? [J]

## 4. How private employers value and pay for each craft

[J, supported where marked]:

- **Field craft** is hired at entry and paid least: hourly, seasonal, contractor-employed. **Field craft earns a premium only when it is coupled with a credential or permit that confers authority.** Examples: authorized biologist, PWS delineator, ISA Utility Specialist, the CA-licensed forester, certified operator. Private employers price authority, not experience alone.
- **Data craft** is the bulk of billable junior and mid-level work: GIS, data management, modeling, calculations. It is paid at parity with the public sector [E: PS-015 for environmental scientists] and is the most exposed to AI compression (§5). A premium exists where data craft meets finance or tech (dMRV, climate risk, carbon accounting) [E, low quality: PS-013; J].
- **Statecraft** in private employment is three different things:
  - **(i) Regulator navigation.** Knowing how the agency reasons, sequencing permits, negotiating conditions. Former agency staff are hired for this.
  - **(ii) Commercial statecraft.** Client, proposals, pricing, landowner and community negotiation.
  - **(iii) Corporate-governance statecraft.** Disclosure law, board reporting, assurance.

  (ii) and (iii) are where the largest pay premiums sit [E: PS-011 for corporate; J for consulting]. v1's statecraft is built almost entirely around (i) as seen from the government side.
- **The real private premium is accountability.** It is paid for owning a stamp, a signature, a client, a profit-and-loss statement or an enterprise disclosure. This is why the BLS distribution is right-skewed [E: PS-014, PS-015; J]. The taxonomy should represent **sign-off authority** explicitly (P-PS-02), because it explains both pay and where AI cannot substitute.

**Hiring criteria, in order of how hard the screen is** [J; certification prevalence E: PS-016]:
1. Licensure or legal signatory eligibility.
2. Required safety and entry credentials.
3. Named regulatory-program experience.
4. Named tools (EQuIS, HEC-RAS, AERMOD, Esri, GHG Protocol, ISO 14064).
5. Degree field.
6. At senior levels, client book.

Competencies as such are rarely written into private postings. They show up as task lists and "experience with…" lines [J; IA can test this against postings].

## 5. AI in private environmental work (2024–2026)

**What the evidence I reached shows:**
- About 34% of sustainability professionals use AI tools regularly, and combining AI with green skills is reported to add 10–15% to pay [E, low quality: PS-013].
- ESG software vendors sell their tools as an alternative to interns and consultants, and many in-house teams have only one or two people [E, vendor view: PS-023].
- NAEM published an EHS&S-specific AI benchmark in April 2026. It is sponsored by EHS software vendors, and I could not read its findings [E: PS-018].
- The ESG/EHS software market was forecast at $4.34B by 2027 [E, secondhand Verdantix: PS-011, PS-012].
- v1's exposure instrument classified 3.6.2 T1 (collect ESG/GHG data) as Ac/P with a career-ladder-destruction signal. It also identified ESG reporting platforms as "supervision zones" [E: PS-029].

**What I could not evidence:** firm-level AI tool deployments at consulting firms, measured effects on junior hiring, changes in billing models, AI permitting software adoption, and dMRV substitution rates. These need LM's methods and sources.

**Mechanisms, in practitioner judgment** [J unless marked]:
1. **Document-production compression.** EA/EIS chapters, biological assessments, Phase I ESA reports, SWPPPs, permit applications, annual compliance reports and proposals are template-heavy and precedent-heavy, so LLM drafting compresses them. Juniors learned the regulations by writing them.
2. **Billing-model pressure** [S]. Time-and-materials and cost-plus contracts monetize hours. If drafting hours fall a lot, firms either reprice (fixed fee or value pricing), accept lower revenue per deliverable, or under-disclose efficiency. Public clients' contract forms (labor-hour task orders) lag, which creates an incentive problem that agencies and firms should name openly. *To confirm:* firm disclosures, changes in federal and state contract clauses on AI use, and fixed-fee shares in consulting revenue.
3. **Pyramid compression.** Firms are leveraged pyramids (many juniors per project manager). With drafting compressed, fewer staff scientists are needed per project, but the same number of reviewers and signers. The 2026 juniors are the 2032 reviewers. This is the private version of v1's career-ladder-destruction finding, and it is **more acute in private firms because utilization pressure removes slack for formation** [J].
4. **Field work becomes the main on-ramp.** Sampling, protocol surveys, construction monitoring, delineation and inventory stay human, though augmented by sensors, drones and AI pre-screening. The on-ramp shifts toward field technicians, who are lower paid and seasonal. Equity and retention follow from that.
5. **Compliance automation.** Permit-condition tracking, regulatory-change monitoring and incident analytics augment EHS staff. The **certifying official's legal accountability does not move**, so the role becomes review-and-certify.
6. **ESG data platforms** automate collection, emission-factor lookup and framework mapping. Human work moves to boundary and estimation judgments, controls, and liaison with the assurance provider. This is consistent with the shift toward controllers and legal oversight [E: PS-012, PS-010].
7. **dMRV.** Remote sensing and ML reduce plot counts, but registries and verifiers still require ground truth and site visits for many methods. The durable skill is uncertainty quantification and ground-truth design, not running the model.

**Where firms are likely to keep human expertise essential:**
- Anything carrying a **stamp, signature or certification**.
- **Site visits and chain-of-custody sampling.**
- **Independence judgments** in verification and assurance.
- **Agency negotiation and expert testimony.**
- **Community and landowner relationships.**

These are exactly the sign-off and relational points in §4 [J; consistent with PS-029]. Candidates PS-C19 and PS-C20 make the AI-specific private competencies explicit.

## 6. Public ↔ private translation

`public_private_translation.csv` holds 38 high-traffic translations (TR-01 to TR-38). Each gives public and private terms, the shared v1 codes (all validated against the v1 baseline), the gaps and the credential bridges. Five patterns run through the table:

1. **Mirror roles across the regulator–regulated line.** Examples: NEPA coordinator and consultant NEPA PM (TR-01); EPA inspector and EHS compliance manager (TR-02); EPA remedial project manager and the PRP's consultant PM (TR-12); NPDES permit writer and industrial compliance specialist (TR-24); USACE regulatory PM and wetland consultant or bank ecologist (TR-17); COR and contractor program manager (TR-28). **The competency is shared, but the posture is inverted.** The person moves from deciding to persuading and documenting. This is the strongest case for CA's enactment-notes hypothesis, with one addition: **posture** (decider / applicant / independent verifier) should be an explicit attribute of role variants [J].
2. **Near-identical credentials.** Water operators (TR-25), GIS (TR-09), planners/AICP (TR-16) and foresters/SAF (TR-04) translate with low friction. The private-side gap is speed-to-deliverable and client format [J].
3. **Science that needs a licensure bridge.** Hydrologists with science degrees cannot sign design work without the PE engineering pathway (TR-08). Geologists need the PG. Due diligence needs "Environmental Professional" status. These are hard frictions that EMST cannot remove, only show [J].
4. **Business practice gap on almost every row.** Scope, proposals, budget-to-complete, utilization, change orders and profit and loss. This is the single most common translation gap [J] and the basis for PS-C01 to PS-C03.
5. **New market vocabularies for old field skills.** Forest inventory becomes carbon biometrics: baseline, additionality, leakage, permanence (TR-05). Soil conservation becomes Scope 3 insetting and MRV (TR-10). Climate science becomes financial-materiality risk (TR-21). The field and data craft transfer; the integrity, finance and disclosure statecraft does not.

**Displaced federal staff (2025–26).** I could not evidence movement into consulting or contracting this session (TR-38, PS-R21 are [S]). Two cautions [J]:
- (a) Federal environmental contracting itself contracted in parts of 2025. Consulting is not a guaranteed absorber, and some firms lost federal work. *To confirm:* firm filings and contract-award data.
- (b) Median pay points down for science roles [E: PS-015].

CM and LM should test this with career-transition data before any messaging says that mobility to the private sector is an opportunity for displaced staff.

## 9. Answers to CA architecture questions

### PS-Q1. What units do private employers hire and pay on, and how should private job architectures crosswalk to EMST role profiles?

**Hiring units** [J, with certification prevalence E: PS-016]. Private employers hire on a **job (role × level) inside the firm's own job architecture**. The posting then specifies:
1. Sign-off or licensure eligibility (PE, PG, CIH, Environmental Professional, accredited verifier, certifying official).
2. Entry and safety credentials (HAZWOPER, ISA, CCA, operator certification).
3. Named regulatory-program experience (Section 404, Title V, CERCLA, FERC licensing, CEQA/NEPA).
4. Named tools and methods.
5. At senior levels, client relationships and revenue.

Competencies as abstract units are rarely the explicit screen.

**Pay units** [E for industry effects: PS-014, PS-015; J otherwise]:
- Level of responsibility within the ladder.
- Industry of the employer (oil & gas and utilities pay more than consulting).
- Sign-off authority.
- Revenue responsibility (seller-doer).
- In consulting, a **billable labor category** and rate.

Corporate sustainability pay follows corporate grade structures (manager, director, VP) and is set in finance and legal labor markets [E: PS-011, PS-012].

**Crosswalk design (proposal, [J]):**
- **Don't crosswalk firm by firm.** Firms' job architectures are proprietary and change with reorganizations. Instead, publish a **reference private job architecture** (seeded from `private_job_frameworks.csv`) at the grain *employer industry × job family × career stream (technical / management / business development) × level of responsibility*. Let employers map to it, the way they map to compensation surveys.
- **Fields on each L6 crosswalk record:** EMST role-profile ID; sector actor = private; industry (controlled vocabulary of the 14 employer types used here); ownership (investor-owned vs public, for utilities); **delivery model** (in-house | contracted | independent third-party); **posture** (decider | applicant/preparer | independent verifier); level; entry requirements (credentials); **sign-off authority**; match type (SKOS exact/close/broad/narrow) with confidence.
- **Public, machine-readable seed for consulting levels.** Federal contract labor categories (e.g., GSA's CALC data) describe private consulting roles with education and experience minimums and ceiling rates. This is the closest thing to a published private consulting job architecture [S: not accessed this session, PS-ECO-010; verify availability and licensing].
- **Use level of responsibility, not years.** Private ladders advance by scope of accountability: task → task budget → project/client → practice/revenue. That supports CA DP-4 (a generic responsibility scale with anchors). It also argues for a level descriptor that v1 lacks: **"accountable for revenue or sign-off."**

**Implication for the spine (DP-1).** Mirror roles share tasks but invert posture (§6). Translation therefore works at the **task and credential level**, with posture and sign-off as role-variant attributes. This supports CA's hybrid: a task spine (B), competency packaging (A), and sector actor as context [J]. One amendment: **industry, ownership and delivery model must be facets orthogonal to sector actor.** CA's audit lists "utility" and "consulting" as sub-types of the sector actor. Utilities can be public or private, and consultants work for every actor [J].

## Disagreements & trade-offs

- **With v1 (F14/F15 as "the private sector").** Most private employment is technical field and data work [E: PS-014]. Keeping "private" in two statecraft-heavy families would distort every mobility computation. *Trade-off:* adding private variants to every function adds authoring load. I accept that cost, because it is the only way the mobility theory holds for the private sector.
- **With v1 Structural Conditions (private premium drains agencies).** This is not true at the median for scientists [E: PS-015]. It is true for senior corporate roles and some industries. *Trade-off:* the honest message ("private moves may cost you pay unless you take on sign-off or revenue responsibility") is less attractive for adoption than "mobility creates opportunity." I recommend honesty. CM, AS and FH should decide how to frame it.
- **With CA audit §7 (partial).** I agree on retiring the five fields. I disagree with modelling "utility" and "consulting" as sub-types of the sector actor; they are industry and delivery-model facets (P-PS-01). *Trade-off:* more facets, slightly more complexity for users. Views can hide them.
- **With a strict durable-vs-tools reading (DC lane).** I support the principle, but private postings **do screen on named tools** (EQuIS, HEC-RAS, AERMOD, GHG Protocol). If L4 is not queryable from role profiles, the private translation fails at the first HR screen. *Trade-off:* the speed of the tools layer against the stability of role profiles. Link them, but version them separately.
- **The biggest trade-off: portability versus parsimony.** Adding business-practice competencies (client, proposals, billable delivery) makes EMST usable to private employers and to people moving between sectors. It also imports generic professional-services skills that O*NET and ESCO already cover, and that the federal-first audience may see as out of scope. My position: include a small, environmentally scoped cluster with public enactments (P-PS-03). Reference O*NET/ESCO for the generic remainder. CA decides.
- **Naming employers and vendors.** Illustrative employer names make the frameworks legible, but they sit uneasily with vendor neutrality. I used them only as examples and labelled them illustrative. CA may prefer anonymized archetypes in published outputs.

## Issues outside my area that affect the vision

- **Pay direction of federal-to-private moves** (CM, FH, AS). Mobility tools that ignore pay direction will mislead displaced staff.
- **Licensure is a hard gate** (LC, IO). EMST can show the PE/PG/CIH pathway but cannot confer it. Learning paths must route through licensing boards.
- **Conflict of interest and revolving door** (SC, 7.1.3). More mobility means more people preparing documents for decisions by their former colleagues, and more AI drafting of those documents. PS-C18 and PS-C20 need SC's statecraft framing.
- **Vendor-sponsored AI evidence dominates EHS and ESG** (LM). Appraise for adoption-optimism bias.
- **Postings data** (IA, CP). Private postings are the main source for private role profiles. Terms of use and licensing of job boards (PS-ECO-028) must be checked early.
- **Utilities straddle SL and PS.** Municipal and special-district utilities are local government; investor-owned utilities are private. Role variants should be co-authored by SL and PS.
- **NGO pilot** (AS). Land trusts and conservation NGOs run market programs (mitigation, easements, carbon). F15 rewrites and PS-C14 matter for the pilot kit.
- **Field-craft reproduction in private firms** (FC). Utilization pressure removes formation slack. The field-to-office drain inside firms mirrors the agency one.

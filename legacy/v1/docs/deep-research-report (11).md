# Wildland Fire Intelligence and Operations Workforce: Existing Positions and Standards

Executive summary: U.S. wildland fire already has a de facto “fire intelligence & decision support” workforce, but it is split across (a) NWCG-qualified incident roles that primarily operate inside ICS on incidents; (b) year-round Predictive Services and coordination-center intelligence functions at NICC/GACCs that blend situation reporting, resource-status intelligence, and outlook production; (c) the NWS Incident Meteorologist (IMET) program that embeds weather/environment decision support into incident operations; and (d) fast-growing state, utility, nonprofit, academic, and private-sector roles focused on technology integration (AI detection, camera networks, satellite tasking, data pipelines) that do not map cleanly onto NWCG or federal position classification. citeturn5view1turn30view0turn31view3turn18search0turn18news40

## Predictive Services and GACC intelligence staffing and competency signals

Predictive Services is explicitly defined as a coordinated national + geographic-area function. The National Interagency Predictive Services Handbook states that Predictive Services at NICC and the GACCs works “in unison” to produce products/services and that it is “comprised of Meteorologists, Wildland Fire Analysts, and Intelligence Coordinators at the NICC and GACCs.” citeturn5view1

### What positions exist and what they do operationally

The same handbook defines an Intelligence Section function inside Predictive Services units: it “collect[s], analyze[s], and report[s] on situation and resource information.” It also makes clear that GACC intelligence staffing varies, potentially including “an Intelligence Coordinator, Intelligence Officer, and/or a detailed Intelligence Support Specialist (INTS),” and then lists common duties: multi-level coordination (resource commitment/availability, threats to people, values at risk), analysis and situational assessment to support incident/resource management decisions, and archiving historical incident/resource records. citeturn12search17turn7search19

A state example (Alaska) illustrates how intelligence sections behave in practice: Alaska’s coordination-center standards describe an intelligence section responsible for gathering and disseminating wildfire/prescribed fire and resource commitment data statewide, maintaining crew status lists, producing year-end statistics, and serving as data stewards of statewide historical fire records—plus notifying NICC counterparts in sensitive/high-activity situations. citeturn7search19

### Staffing models, GS grade signals, and why “series” remains fuzzy

A key limitation: Predictive Services governance documents emphasize roles and interagency functions more than federal occupational-series standardization. However, the Predictive Services “Phase II” final report provides a concrete staffing model example at the geographic area level, including GS-grade expectations (e.g., a GS-12 Geographic Area Manager (Fire Analyst), GS-12 Meteorologist, GS-11 Intelligence Coordinator, GS-9 Intelligence Assistant). citeturn12search7

What the documents do **not** consistently provide is a universal mapping to OPM occupational series (the “0462 vs 0401 vs 0301 vs 2210 problem”), and this is itself a classification gap: the operational work spans meteorology, fire behavior/fire danger analytics, intelligence coordination, data stewardship, and briefing/tradecraft. citeturn12search17turn12search7turn27search5

**Evidence-backed partial anchors for series:**
- Meteorologist roles in Predictive Services align strongly with OPM’s Meteorology Series (GS-1340) qualification standards (education requirements and professional meteorology baseline). citeturn27search0turn5view1  
- Intelligence coordination/analysis functions often resemble the scope of OPM’s Intelligence Series (GS-0132), which covers work in collection/analysis/evaluation/interpretation and dissemination of varied information types. citeturn20search7turn20search3

### Competencies required versus practiced: the persistent divergence

**Formally defined (strong evidence):**
- Intelligence units perform coordination + analysis + historical recordkeeping and provide intelligence that supports prioritization and preparedness decisions. citeturn12search17turn7search19

## NWS IMET program as a formal decision-support profession within fire operations

The entity["organization","National Weather Service","noaa weather agency"] IMET program is one of the clearest examples of a mature, formal competency and qualification standard for wildfire decision support that is external to NWCG (though interlocked with NWCG mobilization practices).

### Program model and operational integration

NWS Instruction 10-402 defines an Incident Meteorologist (IMET) as a volunteer NWS forecaster who provides on-site weather and environmental information, typically at an Incident Command Post, to support responder safety and tactical decision-making by the IMT. citeturn30view0

The same directive specifies the dispatch pathway: an IMET request routes through the GACC, and the National Fire Weather Operations Coordinator (NFWOC) manages availability and mission tracking, including maintaining incident/mission databases. It also clarifies supervision within ICS (normally under the Plans Section Chief) while asserting the IMET as the “ultimate weather authority” on the incident. citeturn30view0

### Selection criteria and training/certification standards

NWS Instruction 10-405 provides a structured progression and explicitly nests IMET competency within broader fire-weather competency tiers: Fire Weather Forecasters, Fire Weather Program Leaders, Deployment-Ready Meteorologists, and IMETs. citeturn31view3

Key IMET eligibility and training requirements include:
- Must meet Fire Weather Forecaster requirements and be “Deployment Ready” (with specified NIMS training) before IMET certification. citeturn31view3  
- Must attain GS-12 (with limited exceptions), indicating an explicit seniority/experience threshold baked into the competency model. citeturn31view3  
- Must complete S-390; must be knowledgeable/familiar with S-490 (reviewing course reference text/slides); must attend an IMET Continuity in Excellence Exercise; and must complete at least two on-site training assignments totaling at least 20 days, including one assignment with a Type I or Type II IMT, tracked via an IMET task book. citeturn31view3  

### Recertification and competency maintenance

NWSI 10-405 also defines recertification criteria and a governance loop: annual review by Regional Fire Weather Program Managers and MICs; required refresher training; and a five-year “recent dispatch” requirement using AMRS. The NFWOC issues Red Cards and updates IROC for qualified IMETs. citeturn30view1turn31view3

### Why this matters for “Fire Intelligence & Operations”

IMET is a prototype for how a formal profession family can be structured: clear tiering, explicit prerequisites, formal field evaluation (task book), recurring proficiency requirements, and a national coordination function for readiness and mobilization—all while being deeply embedded in operational decision cycles. citeturn31view3turn30view0

A related directive, NWSI 10-2404, documents an agency-wide baseline for Integrated Decision Support Services (IDSS) delivery—useful as a non-fire analog framework for how decision support can be standardized across diverse operational contexts. citeturn29search6

## Federal classification and qualification gaps for fire intelligence and tech-integrated roles

Federal hiring and classification is a core constraint for building a coherent “Fire Intelligence & Operations” profession family across agencies. The problem is not a lack of series; it is that existing series slice the work along disciplinary lines, while wildfire intelligence roles often require a single incumbent to span multiple slices.

### OPM series commonly implicated by wildfire intelligence work

The following OPM qualification standards and flysheets represent the most frequently “legible” federal classification homes for wildfire intelligence and technology-integrated work:

- entity["organization","Office of Personnel Management","us federal hr agency"] Intelligence Series (GS-0132): defines intelligence work as collection/analysis/evaluation/interpretation and dissemination of information across many domains. citeturn20search3turn20search7  
- Meteorology Series (GS-1340): formal professional baseline for meteorologists, including the NWS IMET pipeline’s GS-level expectations. citeturn27search0turn31view3  
- Data Science Series (GS-1560): formal home for data scientist work; OPM emphasizes overlap risk and the need for careful classification analysis, a direct signal of why “hybrid fire intelligence” roles are hard to classify cleanly. citeturn27search1turn27search5  
- IT Management Series (GS-2210): common home for platform, systems, and enterprise integration roles that support fire intelligence toolchains. citeturn27search2turn27search16  
- Geography Series (GS-0150): a plausible home for professional geographers and some GIS-heavy roles where analysis/interpretation is central. citeturn27search3turn27search7

### Where classification standards fail the wildfire intelligence cross-domain reality

OPM’s own Data Science series flysheet warns that data scientist work overlaps other work and requires careful series determination. In wildfire, that overlap is the norm: a “fire intelligence” lead might need to (1) interpret remote sensing, (2) engineer data flows into operational tools, (3) brief leadership, and (4) understand fire behavior and resource ordering—all in one job. The classification system encourages splitting these into different series, which can fragment accountability and complicate career pathways. citeturn27search5turn12search17turn7search19

A second structural mismatch is the divergence between NWCG qualification (capability to perform a role in ICS) and OPM classification (discipline-based occupational series). The IMET program shows how a non-NWCG profession solves this by imposing an internal tiered competency framework linked to GS-level and field task-book evaluation—yet that approach is not widely replicated for other fire intelligence roles (e.g., coordination-center intelligence leads or tech integration officers). citeturn31view3turn12search17turn8view1

## Legislative and policy frameworks shaping wildfire intelligence workforce requirements

Recent legislation and state policy proposals do not typically define detailed competency frameworks—but they **do** encode an implied workforce model: interagency data aggregation, comprehensive assessment/prediction, and decision support spanning prevention through response and recovery.

### Proposed federal wildfire intelligence center model

S.5532 (Wildfire Intelligence Collaboration and Coordination Act of 2024) proposes establishing a “Wildfire Intelligence Center” as a joint office across the Departments of Agriculture, Commerce, and Interior. Its duties explicitly include serving as a development and operational center for comprehensive assessment and prediction of fires in the wildland and built environment interface to provide decision support services informing land/fuels management, community outreach and risk reduction, post-fire recovery/rehabilitation, and fire management/response. citeturn17view0

The proposed governance structure (a 14-member Board) also signals the intended competency breadth by hardwiring representation from NOAA, USFA, FEMA, NWS, USFS fire & aviation, DOI bureaus (BLM/BIA/NPS/FWS), USGS, USDA, DoD, and USFS R&D. citeturn17view0

**Workforce implication (inference, grounded in the bill’s functions):** This model implies a profession family that blends operational decision support with applied science, data management, and interagency coordination—closer to “national-scale wildfire intelligence fusion + decision support” than to classic incident-only intelligence. citeturn17view0

### Technology deployment/testbed as a workforce driver

H.R. 8790 (Fix Our Forests Act, 118th Congress version) includes a “deployment and testbed pilot program” for new and innovative wildfire prevention, detection, communication, and mitigation technologies, and requires reporting on technologies tested, costs to scale, outreach to technology developers, and recommendations for adoption at scale. citeturn14view1

## Adjacent profession comparisons and modern training pipelines

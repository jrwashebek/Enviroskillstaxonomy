**Five Functional Positions in Environmental Information Flow**

*A Framework for Understanding Workforce Competencies Across the
Data-to-Decision Lifecycle*

**Framework Overview**

Environmental management requires the continuous transformation of
physical reality into decisions and back again. This framework
identifies five functional positions through which information flows,
each requiring distinct competencies and each interfacing with adjacent
positions in predictable ways.

Crucially, this is not a hierarchy. The field practitioner who notices
anomalous bark beetle behavior is not &#x201C;below&#x201D; the data
scientist modeling spread patterns; they are generating the ground truth
that makes the model meaningful. Value circulates through the system,
and each position depends on the others.

**Position 1: Sensing & Generating**

*Field Interface*

This position encompasses the direct interface between practitioners and
the physical environment. Professionals operating here translate
observable reality into recordable data through standardized protocols,
field measurements, and direct observation. They are the source of
ground truth that all downstream analysis depends upon.

**Core Activities**

> • Timber cruising, ecological surveys, fuels assessments, heritage
> surveys
>
> • Operating equipment that generates data (GPS, tablets, sensors,
> drones)
>
> • Applying protocols that translate physical reality into recordable
> observations
>
> • Recognizing field conditions that deviate from expectations
>
> • Calibrating instruments and maintaining data quality at the point of
> collection

**Critical Competency:**

> *Knowing what&#x2019;s worth noticing that isn&#x2019;t in the
> protocol. The most valuable field practitioners develop intuition for
> anomalies, edge cases, and contextual factors that standardized forms
> cannot capture.*

**Interfaces With:** Structuring & Curating (upstream data flow),
Deciding & Directing (ground-truthing decisions)

**Common Blind Spot:** Underestimating the costs and complexity of data
structuring; assuming that &#x201C;good data&#x201D; is self-evident
without understanding downstream integration requirements.

**Position 2: Structuring & Curating**

*Data Infrastructure*

This position manages the systems that make field data usable,
discoverable, and interoperable. Professionals here design databases,
enforce data standards, maintain quality assurance processes, and ensure
that information collected at different times and places can be
meaningfully combined.

**Core Activities**

> • Database design, data architecture, schema development
>
> • Data standards development and enforcement
>
> • Quality assurance and validation processes
>
> • Metadata management and documentation
>
> • Versioning, provenance tracking, and data lineage
>
> • Making field data interoperable with other systems and external
> datasets

**Critical Competency:**

> *Anticipating downstream uses while preserving upstream fidelity.
> Effective data architects balance the need for standardization with
> the reality that field conditions are messy, and that future
> analytical needs cannot always be predicted.*

**Interfaces With:** Sensing & Generating (data input), Analyzing &
Interpreting (data output)

**Common Blind Spot:** Underestimating field variability and the
contextual knowledge that field practitioners carry; designing systems
that work in theory but fail under real-world data collection
conditions.

**Position 3: Analyzing & Interpreting**

*Sense-Making*

This position transforms structured data into actionable understanding.
Professionals here apply statistical methods, spatial analysis, modeling
techniques, and domain expertise to extract patterns, test hypotheses,
and characterize uncertainty. They translate between quantitative
outputs and management questions.

**Core Activities**

> • Statistical modeling and spatial analysis
>
> • Fire behavior modeling, growth-and-yield projections, species
> distribution modeling
>
> • Scenario development and sensitivity analysis
>
> • Uncertainty characterization and communication
>
> • Translating analytical results into management-relevant findings
>
> • Integrating multiple data streams into coherent assessments

**Critical Competency:**

> *Knowing what the analysis can&#x2019;t tell you. The most valuable
> analysts understand model assumptions, data limitations, and the
> boundaries of inference&#x2014;and communicate these clearly to
> decision-makers.*

**Interfaces With:** Structuring & Curating (data input), Integrating &
Presenting (output translation)

**Common Blind Spot:** Underestimating decision constraints and the
political/institutional context in which managers operate; producing
analyses that are technically correct but practically unusable.

**Position 4: Integrating & Presenting**

*Decision Support*

This position synthesizes multiple analytical streams into coherent
decision frameworks. Professionals here build tools, design interfaces,
create visualizations, and translate between technical and policy
languages. They package complexity into forms that support rather than
overwhelm decision-makers.

**Core Activities**

> • Building decision support tools, dashboards, and workflows
>
> • Synthesizing multiple analysis streams into coherent options
>
> • Designing user interfaces for non-technical audiences
>
> • Translating between technical and policy languages
>
> • Creating visualizations that reveal rather than obscure trade-offs
>
> • Facilitating structured decision processes

**Critical Competency:**

> *Designing for actual decision contexts, not idealized ones. Effective
> integrators understand how decisions actually get made in their
> organization&#x2014;the timelines, the political constraints, the
> cognitive limitations&#x2014;and design tools that work within those
> realities.*

**Interfaces With:** Analyzing & Interpreting (analytical input),
Deciding & Directing (decision output)

**Common Blind Spot:** Building tools that reflect how decisions should
be made rather than how they actually are made; creating elegant systems
that remain unused because they don&#x2019;t fit workflow realities.

**Position 5: Deciding & Directing**

*Governance Interface*

This position encompasses accountability for choices under uncertainty.
Professionals here make NEPA decisions, allocate resources, set
strategic priorities, and bear responsibility for outcomes. They
navigate between technical inputs, legal requirements, political
pressures, and stakeholder expectations.

**Core Activities**

> • NEPA decision-making and signing authority
>
> • Resource allocation and prioritization
>
> • Strategic planning and goal-setting
>
> • Stakeholder engagement and tribal consultation
>
> • Accountability for outcomes under uncertainty
>
> • Balancing competing mandates and values

**Critical Competency:**

> *Knowing when to override the tool&#x2019;s recommendation. Effective
> decision-makers understand that models and DSS tools encode
> assumptions and values, and that professional judgment&#x2014;informed
> by experience, ethics, and context&#x2014;remains essential.*

**Interfaces With:** Integrating & Presenting (decision inputs), Sensing
& Generating (outcome feedback)

**Common Blind Spot:** Underestimating uncertainty and the limits of
available information; demanding precision that data cannot provide or
making decisions as if uncertainty doesn&#x2019;t exist.

The Information Metabolism

Information flows through these positions in multiple directions. The
primary forward flow transforms observations into decisions:

**Sensing &#x2192; Structuring &#x2192; Analyzing &#x2192; Integrating
&#x2192; Deciding**

But equally important are the feedback loops:

> • Decisions produce outcomes that require new sensing
>
> • Analysis reveals data gaps that reshape collection protocols
>
> • Decision-maker needs drive what gets integrated and how
>
> • Integration failures reveal structuring inadequacies

Adaptive management lives in these feedback loops. When they
break&#x2014;when decisions never get evaluated against outcomes, when
analysts never learn why their products go unused, when field crews
never understand why data standards matter&#x2014;organizational
learning stops.

Bridging Competencies

The most valuable practitioners can move between
positions&#x2014;explaining to a field crew why data structure matters,
or helping a line officer understand model uncertainty. These bridging
competencies are often the limiting factor in project success.

Bridging competencies include:

> • **Sensing &#x2194; Structuring:** Understanding both field realities
> and data system requirements; designing collection protocols that
> balance scientific rigor with operational feasibility
>
> • **Structuring &#x2194; Analyzing:** Understanding both data
> architecture and analytical methods; knowing how data structure
> enables or constrains analysis
>
> • **Analyzing &#x2194; Integrating:** Translating analytical
> complexity into decision-relevant frameworks; communicating
> uncertainty in actionable terms
>
> • **Integrating &#x2194; Deciding:** Understanding both technical
> possibilities and governance constraints; designing decision support
> that fits actual decision processes

How Technology Shifts Bottlenecks

Technological change doesn&#x2019;t eliminate positions&#x2014;it shifts
which positions are capacity-limiting:

> • **Mobile data collection** shifted bottlenecks from Structuring to
> Sensing (more data flows in, but interpretation capacity hasn&#x2019;t
> scaled)
>
> • **Remote sensing expansion** shifted bottlenecks from Sensing to
> Analyzing (satellite data proliferates faster than analytical
> capacity)
>
> • **AI/ML tools** are shifting bottlenecks from Analyzing to
> Integrating (more model outputs than synthesis capacity)
>
> • **Decision support proliferation** may shift bottlenecks from
> Integrating to Deciding (more tools than decision-maker bandwidth to
> use them)

Workforce development strategy should anticipate these shifts. Training
investments that address yesterday&#x2019;s bottleneck may miss
tomorrow&#x2019;s constraint.

Relationship to Taxonomy Domains

The Environmental Management Skills Taxonomy organizes competencies by
subject matter domain (Ecological Science, Technology, Operations
Management, Field Methods, Communication, Policy). Functional positions
are orthogonal&#x2014;they describe where in the information flow a
competency operates, not what subject matter it addresses.

This creates a productive matrix:

> • **Domain** answers: What subject matter does this competency
> address?
>
> • **Position** answers: Where in the data-to-decision flow does this
> competency operate?

For example, &#x201C;fire behavior modeling&#x201D; is a Domain 1
(Ecological Science) competency that operates primarily at the Analyzing
position. &#x201C;Fuels assessment protocols&#x201D; is also Domain 1
but operates at the Sensing position. Both involve fire science, but
they require different functional orientations.

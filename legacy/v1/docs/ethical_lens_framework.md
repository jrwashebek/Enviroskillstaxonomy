# Ethical Lens Framework
## Environmental Management Skills Taxonomy — Task 7 Output
### Status: Complete — Stage 2, February 2026
### Dependencies: None (informs Task 12)

---

## Purpose and Scope

This framework defines the ethical lenses used to identify, tag, and develop ethical dimensions across all 215 competencies in the Environmental Management Skills Taxonomy. It is the conceptual foundation for Task 12 (ethics tagging and competency development).

The framework is a practitioner-facing analytical tool: a set of consistent, operationalizable lenses that reviewers can apply without specialized ethics training. Each lens names something that environmental professionals already encounter in their work but may not have a shared vocabulary for.

The framework is consistent with Domain 2, Cross-Cutting Competency 4 (Ethical and Equitable Technology Practice) — the most developed ethical competency in the current taxonomy — and is substantially broader than it. That competency addresses data privacy and sovereignty, algorithmic bias, equitable access to technology, and the limits of automated decision-making. It operationalizes Lens 2 and Lens 3 of this framework within the specific context of environmental technology practice.

---

## The Five Lenses

### Lens 1: Equity and Justice

**Definition:** The obligation to examine who bears the costs and receives the benefits of environmental decisions, interventions, and resource allocations — and to design and evaluate practice with that distribution in view. This lens encompasses three related but distinct dimensions:

- *Distributive justice*: Are costs, risks, and benefits distributed fairly across communities, populations, and generations? Environmental burdens (pollution, extraction, displacement) and environmental benefits (restoration, access, ecosystem services) are not neutrally distributed; they follow existing gradients of race, income, and political marginalization.
- *Procedural justice*: Do affected parties have meaningful participation in decisions that affect them? Are participation mechanisms accessible, timely, and genuinely influential — or performative?
- *Recognition justice*: Are the values, knowledge, and interests of affected communities acknowledged as legitimate? Is the full range of what communities care about brought into the analysis, or are non-market values systematically excluded?

A competency raises the Equity and Justice lens whenever it involves allocation of resources, siting of interventions, design of monitoring or engagement programs, or prioritization of management areas — particularly in contexts with documented histories of environmental injustice.

**Where it's already explicit in the taxonomy:** Domain 5 (Stakeholder Engagement) competencies on community engagement and environmental justice; Domain 6 (Policy and Governance) competencies on regulatory equity and cumulative impacts.

**Where it's implicit but important:** Domain 2 competencies on spatial analysis and site prioritization, which embed distributional assumptions in their methodologies without naming them. Conservation finance competencies (Domain 6) that structure who captures value from ecosystem service markets. Climate vulnerability assessment competencies (Domain 1) that make choices about which populations' futures get modeled.

**Why it matters for workforce development:** Environmental professionals routinely make decisions with distributive consequences without framing them as such. Workforce development that treats these as purely technical decisions produces practitioners who are technically competent and socially consequential without understanding why.

**What an explicit competency looks like:**
- Foundational: Recognize that siting and prioritization decisions have distributional consequences; identify which communities are affected by proposed interventions.
- Intermediate: Apply environmental justice screening tools to planning and analysis; identify cumulative burden patterns and incorporate them into recommendations.
- Advanced: Design engagement and decision processes that meet procedural and recognition justice standards; evaluate distributional equity of proposed interventions and document findings.
- Expert: Develop institutional equity frameworks; advance methodological standards for cumulative impact assessment.

### Lens 2: Cultural Rights and Self-Determination

**Definition:** Indigenous peoples' and local communities' collective rights to govern their lands, waters, knowledge systems, and futures — and the professional obligations that follow from those rights. This lens is grounded in the right to Free, Prior, and Informed Consent (FPIC): the principle that Indigenous peoples have the right to be informed about and to approve or reject activities that affect their territories before those activities begin. It extends to data sovereignty (who controls data collected about Indigenous lands and peoples), knowledge governance (who has authority over Traditional Ecological Knowledge), and self-determined participation (Indigenous communities as decision-makers, not just consultees).

**Distinction from Lens 1:** This lens concerns collective rights and sovereignty, not just distribution. A project could distribute benefits equitably to Indigenous community members while still violating Indigenous governance rights. FPIC is a rights standard, not an equity metric.

**Where it's already explicit in the taxonomy:** Domain 1's cross-cutting competency on Integration of Knowledge Systems (knowledge co-production, ethical protocols for TEK engagement). Domain 2's cross-cutting competency on Ethical and Equitable Technology Practice (data sovereignty, CARE Principles for Indigenous data governance).

**Where it's implicit but important:** Wildlife management competencies that describe habitat assessment and species monitoring on or adjacent to tribal lands without referencing consultation requirements. Field technology competencies describing drone deployment and acoustic monitoring without addressing data governance for monitoring in Indigenous territories. Policy competencies addressing federal consultation requirements (Section 106, Section 7) procedurally without treating those requirements as rights-based.

**Why it matters for workforce development:** Compliance framing asks "did we consult?" while rights framing asks "did the community exercise meaningful authority?" Practitioners who understand the underlying rights logic, not just the procedural checklist, perform differently in contested situations.

**What an explicit competency looks like:**
- Foundational: Understand the distinction between consultation and consent; recognize FPIC as a rights standard; be aware of CARE Principles alongside FAIR Principles.
- Intermediate: Design engagement processes that meet FPIC standards; apply CARE Principles to data governance decisions; identify when data collection triggers Indigenous data sovereignty obligations.
- Advanced: Negotiate data governance agreements with tribal governments; develop organizational protocols for culturally appropriate partnerships; navigate situations where agency mandates and Indigenous rights are in tension.
- Expert: Shape institutional and agency standards for Indigenous rights compliance; advance field-wide understanding of FPIC implementation in environmental management contexts.

**Delphi flag:** Retain as separate from Lens 1. The rights-vs-equity distinction is real and diagnostic. Flag as a Delphi question: do reviewers experience the two lenses as meaningfully distinct in practice?

### Lens 3: Transparency and Accountability

**Definition:** The obligation to make decisions, analyses, and their basis visible — and to ensure that someone bears consequence for decisions affecting others. Transparency is an epistemic obligation: results, methodologies, assumptions, uncertainties, and conflicts of interest must be documented and recoverable. Accountability is a relational obligation: decisions must be attributable to identifiable actors, affected parties must have recourse, and professional judgments must be defensible to scrutiny.

The transparency obligation extends specifically to uncertainty: honest representation of confidence intervals, data limitations, model assumptions, and the conditions under which findings might not hold.

**Where it's already explicit in the taxonomy:** Domain 2's cross-cutting competency on Data Quality and Integrity (methodological transparency, chain of custody). Domain 2's cross-cutting competency on Ethical and Equitable Technology Practice (algorithmic accountability, evaluation of automated systems for bias and fairness). Domain 6 competencies on regulatory process management.

**Where it's implicit but important:** Predictive modeling competencies that describe building and deploying models without naming the obligation to communicate limitations and uncertainty to downstream users. Conservation genetics competencies involving irreversible decisions made on probabilistic analysis. Risk assessment competencies where framing choices embedded in risk models are not always transparent to decision-makers.

**Why it matters for workforce development:** Technical authority creates accountability asymmetries. Practitioners who do not internalize transparency norms — about uncertainty, about methodological choices, about what their analysis cannot say — effectively make decisions that formally belong to others.

**What an explicit competency looks like:**
- Foundational: Document methodological choices and data limitations; communicate findings with appropriate uncertainty language; understand what information decision-makers need to evaluate recommendations.
- Intermediate: Develop transparent reporting standards for analytical products; design audit trails for data and modeling workflows; identify and disclose conflicts of interest.
- Advanced: Lead organizational transparency and documentation standards; design accountability structures for technical decision support; evaluate algorithmic systems for explainability and auditability.
- Expert: Advance field-wide standards for uncertainty communication and methodological transparency; develop frameworks for accountability in data-driven environmental governance.

### Lens 4: Intervention Ethics

**Definition:** The ethics of deliberate human manipulation of ecological systems — when intervention is justified, at what scale, with whose authorization, and with what obligations regarding reversibility and precaution. This lens asks: not *can we* intervene, and not even *how* do we intervene, but *should we*, *who decides*, and *what do we owe to the system and the future* if we do?

**Scope calibration (confirmed):** This lens triggers when a management action creates conditions without historical precedent in the system, or when the action is practically irreversible. Restoring documented historical conditions using established methods is conventional management. The gradient between those poles is where the lens is most valuable and most analytically productive.

Three sub-questions are reliably raised by this lens:
- *The reversibility question*: Can the intervention be undone if it produces unintended consequences?
- *The authorization question*: Who has the standing to decide to intervene, and on whose behalf?
- *The representation question*: Whose interests — human, non-human, future — are represented in the decision?

**Where it's already explicit in the taxonomy:** Domain 1 competencies on conservation genetics and assisted migration begin to engage this lens, particularly in risk assessment and stakeholder engagement indicators. Domain 3 competencies on adaptive management frameworks include precautionary principle applications.

**Where it's implicit but important:** Conservation genetics competencies that describe genetic rescue and gene drive development as technical challenges without naming the authorization and reversibility questions. Prescribed fire competencies that describe ignition decisions without naming the obligation to evaluate landscape-scale and long-term consequences. Watershed restoration competencies describing hydrological modification without addressing the ethics of permanently altering flow regimes. Climate adaptation competencies describing managed relocation without naming intervention ethics stakes.

**Why it matters for workforce development:** The profession is expanding its technical capacity for ecological intervention faster than it is developing its ethical vocabulary for evaluating when and whether to intervene. Practitioners who lack a framework for the authorization, reversibility, and representation questions will default to "technically feasible, therefore appropriate."

**What an explicit competency looks like:**
- Foundational: Recognize that intervention decisions require explicit justification, not just technical feasibility; understand the spectrum from passive to high-intervention approaches and the different ethical stakes at each level.
- Intermediate: Apply precautionary and reversibility criteria to intervention planning; identify authorization requirements and who has standing to decide in specific intervention contexts.
- Advanced: Develop intervention decision frameworks that incorporate reversibility, uncertainty, and multi-stakeholder authorization; communicate intervention ethics stakes to decision-makers and communities.
- Expert: Advance field-wide frameworks for intervention authorization and precautionary governance; contribute to policy development on high-intervention management approaches.

### Lens 5: Professional Integrity

**Definition:** The obligations practitioners carry as professionals when their judgments, findings, or recommendations are shaped by pressures — from employers, clients, funders, or institutional cultures — that conflict with their duties to the public and to ecological systems. This lens addresses the conditions under which professional deference to organizational authority is appropriate, and the conditions under which it is not: when findings must be reported honestly even if they contradict what an organization wants to hear; when conflicts of interest must be disclosed; when professional obligations to public and ecological welfare override employment relationships; and what practitioners owe to colleagues and institutions when they identify systemic misconduct.

**Distinction from Lens 3:** Transparency concerns the *visibility* of information; Professional Integrity concerns the *character of the practitioner* and the *institutional conditions* under which honest practice is or is not possible.

**Where it's already explicit in the taxonomy:** Domain 6 competencies on regulatory compliance include professional standards obligations. Some Domain 5 competencies on science communication address the distinction between honest uncertainty communication and strategic communication.

**Where it's implicit but important:** Environmental consulting competencies that describe client relationship management without addressing how practitioners navigate conflicts between client instructions and professional judgments. Corporate sustainability competencies that describe ESG reporting and materiality assessment without addressing the ethics of selective disclosure or greenwashing. Monitoring competency indicators that describe data collection without naming the obligation to report findings that reveal management failures.

**Why it matters for workforce development:** Workforce development that does not explicitly address the conditions under which practitioners must resist institutional pressure produces practitioners who are technically excellent and institutionally compliant — which is precisely the combination that produces systemic failures.

**What an explicit competency looks like:**
- Foundational: Understand the distinction between professional obligations to employers and obligations to the public and ecological systems; recognize situations where these come into tension.
- Intermediate: Apply professional standards frameworks (disciplinary codes of ethics, NEPA requirements, scientific integrity policies) to specific practice situations; identify and disclose conflicts of interest.
- Advanced: Build organizational cultures and quality management systems that protect professional independence; document and manage institutional pressures that compromise the integrity of findings.
- Expert: Advance professional standards for environmental practitioners; contribute to policy development on scientific integrity in regulatory and corporate contexts.

## Architectural Recommendation

**Confirmed approach:** The Task 12a → 12b sequence governs. Do not draft cross-cutting ethical competencies for Domains 3–6 before tagging is complete. Use the pattern of implicit ethics revealed by Task 12a to determine which domains need cross-cutting competencies and what they should address.

**Three integration modes for Task 12b:**
1. *Cross-cutting competencies per domain*: For ethical dimensions that apply across all skill areas within a domain. Extend the Domain 1 and 2 models to domains where the pattern of implicit ethics is dense and cross-cutting.
2. *Standalone competencies within skill areas*: Where an ethical dimension is sufficiently specific to a skill area and constitutes a genuine workforce development need. Candidates will emerge from Task 12a.
3. *Revised indicators within existing competencies*: Where the ethical dimension is integral to technical performance rather than a separate practice area. This approach is lower-visibility in Delphi but more deeply integrated into practice standards.

## Relationship to Domain 2 Cross-Cutting Competency 4

Domain 2's Ethical and Equitable Technology Practice competency sits at the intersection of Lens 2 (Cultural Rights — data sovereignty, CARE Principles) and Lens 3 (Transparency and Accountability — algorithmic accountability, bias evaluation) with secondary relevance to Lens 1 (Equity and Justice — equitable access to technology). It does not engage Lens 4 (Intervention Ethics) or Lens 5 (Professional Integrity). Cross-reference notes at Task 15 should situate each ethical competency within this broader framework so Delphi reviewers understand the architecture.

## Framework Reference Card for Task 12 Reviewers

| Lens | Core Question | Key Trigger Conditions |
|---|---|---|
| **Equity and Justice** | Who bears costs and receives benefits — distributively, procedurally, and by recognition? | Siting, prioritization, resource allocation, community engagement design, cumulative impact contexts |
| **Cultural Rights and Self-Determination** | Are Indigenous rights, consent, and data sovereignty respected? | Monitoring on tribal lands, TEK engagement, data collection about Indigenous territories, FPIC contexts |
| **Transparency and Accountability** | Are decisions visible, documented, and attributable — with recourse for affected parties? | Modeling, risk assessment, algorithmic tools, uncertainty communication, regulatory recommendations |
| **Intervention Ethics** | Is the intervention justified, reversible, and authorized by those with standing to decide? | Actions creating conditions without historical precedent; practically irreversible management actions; conservation genetics, assisted migration, hydrological modification, climate intervention |
| **Professional Integrity** | Are practitioners' findings and judgments protected from institutional pressures that conflict with public and ecological obligations? | Consulting, regulatory science, ESG reporting, organizational conflicts of interest, findings that contradict employer interests |

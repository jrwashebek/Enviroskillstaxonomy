# Workflow Stage Definitions and Boundary Criteria
## Environmental Management Skills Taxonomy — Task 6 Output
**Stage 2: Framework Definition**
**Status: Ratified — February 2026**
**Dependencies for downstream use:** Task 10 (Workflow Stage Tagging), Task 13 (Permitting Reform Sensitivity Analysis)

---

## Purpose and Status

This document provides formal definitions, boundary criteria, and tagging rules for all 6 workflow stages used to tag the taxonomy's 215 competencies. These definitions are ratified and stable. Do not modify without explicit documentation of the change and downstream review of any tagging already completed.

---

## Preliminary: Structural Decisions

Four structural questions were resolved in developing these definitions. The answers are baked into the definitions below and into all tagging rules.

**Stages 1 and 6 remain separate.** Stage 6 (Adaptive Management & Learning) is retrospective — evaluating past decisions against evidence. Stage 1 (Planning & Strategy) is prospective — designing forward under uncertainty. The competencies that enable critical evaluation of past decisions are analytically distinct from the competencies that enable forward-looking design. Collapsing them would obscure the OBAM feedback loop, which is where most environmental management systems fail.

**Stages 2 and 3 remain separate.** Environmental Review asks: *what are the consequences of this proposed action, and are they acceptable?* It is analytically oriented. Permitting asks: *which agencies have authority to authorize this, and how do we satisfy their requirements?* It is procedurally and institutionally oriented. Separating them also preserves the taxonomy's analytical value for permitting reform analysis — different reform scenarios affect different stages with precision.

**Functional interpretation governs.** Stages are defined by *what kind of work is occurring*, not when in the project calendar. Real-time monitoring during implementation is Stage 4 work (answering: are we doing this right?), not Stage 5. The same physical act means different things depending on the question it is answering.

**Multi-stage tagging cap:** 2 Primary + 2 Secondary maximum per competency. Exceptions require explicit justification. Any competency appearing to need more than 4 total stage tags should be reviewed for possible splitting.

---

## The Primary / Secondary Distinction

**Primary tag:** The competency is among the core tools practitioners *require* to do this stage's characteristic work. Removing it from their kit would leave a meaningful gap in their capacity at this stage.

**Secondary tag:** Practitioners regularly apply this competency in service of this stage's goals, but its primary application is at another stage.

**Operative test for ambiguous cases:**
> *If I removed this competency from a practitioner's kit entirely, would it meaningfully impair their ability to do this stage's characteristic work? Or would they adapt by using other competencies?*

If meaningful impairment: Primary. If they adapt without great difficulty: Secondary. If it barely registers: no tag.

**GitBook display:** Primary tags shown by default; Secondary tags expandable. Consistent with profession family Core-only default.

---

## The Over-Tagging Risk

The filter's value depends on selectivity. A competency tagged to five stages tells a user very little about when it matters. The 2P/2S cap is enforced. Competencies that span three or four stages will exist — stakeholder engagement, project management, ecological science knowledge — and that distribution is real and informative. It is not a tagging error. But the cap prevents inflation that would render the workflow filter useless for reform analysis and curriculum design.

## Stage 1: Planning & Strategy

**Definition.** Planning & Strategy encompasses all activities directed toward defining what a management system is trying to achieve, assessing current conditions relative to desired future conditions, generating and evaluating alternative courses of action, establishing priorities when resources are insufficient for all needs, and designing the organizational and operational infrastructure through which subsequent stages will be executed. The characteristic intellectual posture is forward-looking design under uncertainty.

**What distinguishes Stage 1 work.** The primary question is *what should we do, and why?* Outputs are plans, strategies, alternatives analyses, needs assessments, scoping documents, resource assessments, and priority-setting frameworks. The primary information flows are inward (synthesizing existing knowledge) and forward (translating synthesis into designed alternatives and selected approaches).

**Boundary — upstream edge.** Stage 1 has no fixed upstream boundary within a project sequence. In a functioning adaptive management cycle, it is initiated by Stage 6 output feeding back into the next planning cycle.

**Boundary — downstream edge (Stage 1 → Stage 2).** Planning ends and Environmental Review begins when a proposed action is sufficiently defined to evaluate its specific environmental consequences. The transition is from *what should we do?* to *what happens if we do this?* Marker: once an action is defined precisely enough to predict impacts on identifiable resources, review has begun.

**Boundary — relationship to Stage 6.** Stage 1 and Stage 6 are adjacent parts of a continuous cycle. Formal distinction: Stage 6 is retrospective (evaluating past decisions against objectives established in Stage 1); Stage 1 is prospective (establishing objectives, designing approaches). Tag by function: evaluation work is Stage 6; design work is Stage 1.

**Tagging rule — Primary.** Tag Primary for Stage 1 if the competency is *necessary for designing or initiating management action* — specifically: assessing landscape or ecosystem conditions as a basis for planning, synthesizing existing knowledge into management alternatives, setting measurable objectives, identifying stakeholder interests and values for incorporation into planning decisions, designing adaptive management frameworks, or allocating resources and priorities across competing needs.

**Tagging rule — Secondary.** Tag Secondary for Stage 1 if the competency is regularly applied during planning processes but its primary application is at another stage — e.g., basic ecological science knowledge used to ground planning decisions, or project management skills used to organize the planning process.

**Relationship to Five Functional Positions.** Integrating & Presenting and Deciding & Directing are most heavily engaged. Analyzing & Interpreting is prominent (modeling alternatives, synthesizing ecological baselines). Structuring & Curating supports it. Sensing & Generating is least active, though targeted baseline data collection can engage it at initiation.

## Stage 2: Environmental Review & Compliance

**Definition.** Environmental Review & Compliance encompasses the formal processes through which proposed management actions are evaluated for their potential consequences on natural, cultural, social, and economic resources, and through which compliance with applicable legal standards is documented and verified. Paradigmatic U.S. processes: NEPA analysis (EAs and EISs), ESA Section 7 biological assessments, NHPA Section 106 consultations, Clean Air Act and Clean Water Act pre-discharge reviews. The stage extends to state environmental review procedures, tribal environmental review processes, and voluntary impact assessment frameworks.

**What distinguishes Stage 2 work.** The primary question is *what are the environmental consequences of this proposed action, and are they acceptable under applicable standards?* Outputs are impact analyses, biological assessments, cultural resource evaluations, cumulative effects analyses, public comment responses, Findings of No Significant Impact, Records of Decision, and mitigation commitments. The work is analytical and deliberative: predicting consequences, evaluating against standards, engaging stakeholders, and documenting the reasoning chain.

**Boundary — upstream edge (Stage 1 → Stage 2).** Defined above: Environmental Review begins when a proposed action is specific enough to predict impacts on identifiable resources.

**Boundary — downstream edge (Stage 2 → Stage 3).** Environmental Review ends when the analytical record is complete and the question shifts from *what are the impacts?* to *does the authorizing agency approve, and under what conditions?* In NEPA terms: completing the EIS and issuing a Record of Decision closes Stage 2; subsequent agency authorizations (Section 404 permits, ESA incidental take permits, state water quality certifications) constitute Stage 3.

**Tagging rule — Primary.** Tag Primary for Stage 2 if the competency is specifically necessary for *analyzing and documenting the environmental consequences of proposed actions and ensuring legal compliance* — impact assessment methodology, NEPA documentation, biological assessment preparation, cultural resource evaluation, cumulative effects analysis, public comment process management, alternatives analysis and comparison, or mitigation design and evaluation.

**Tagging rule — Secondary.** Tag Secondary for Stage 2 if the competency regularly supports review processes without being distinctive to them — general ecological science used for impact assessment baselines, stakeholder engagement used in scoping and comment processes, data management used to compile review documentation, or project management used to coordinate multi-disciplinary review teams.

**Permitting reform sensitivity note.** Stage 2 is the primary locus of NEPA-related reform. Changes to categorical exclusion lists, tiering requirements, cumulative effects analysis scope, public comment periods, and inter-agency coordination timelines all primarily affect competencies tagged Primary for Stage 2. This is the analytical foundation for Task 13 (Permitting Reform Sensitivity Analysis).

**Relationship to Five Functional Positions.** Analyzing & Interpreting (impact prediction and evaluation), Integrating & Presenting (synthesizing multi-disciplinary analyses into coherent documents), and Deciding & Directing (agency decision-maker issuing findings and records of decision) are most prominent. Structuring & Curating is active in organizing documentation and managing comment records. Sensing & Generating is engaged where baseline data collection is required.

## Stage 3: Permitting & Authorization

**Definition.** Permitting & Authorization encompasses the processes through which management actions receive formal approval from agencies and authorities with regulatory jurisdiction over the proposed activity. This includes federal, state, and tribal permit applications; agency review and negotiation of permit conditions; cross-agency coordination for concurrent authorization processes; and government-to-government consultation requirements. The characteristic work is procedural and institutional: understanding each authorizing agency's jurisdiction and decision criteria, preparing applications that satisfy those criteria, managing parallel authorization processes, negotiating permit conditions, and tracking compliance with conditions once authorization is granted.

**What distinguishes Stage 3 work.** The primary question is *which agencies have authority to authorize this, what do they require, and how do we satisfy those requirements?* Outputs are permit applications, mitigation agreements, permit condition compliance plans, interagency coordination frameworks, and formal authorization documents. Where Stage 2 is analytically oriented, Stage 3 is procedurally and institutionally oriented — competencies center on understanding regulatory jurisdiction, preparing technically correct applications, and navigating agency approval sequences.

**Boundary — upstream edge (Stage 2 → Stage 3).** In practice, permitting processes often begin while environmental review is ongoing (pre-application meetings, agency relationship-building). The formal Stage 3 begins when the applicant submits applications to authorizing agencies.

**Boundary — downstream edge (Stage 3 → Stage 4).** Permitting ends when authorizations are in hand and permit conditions are established. Implementation begins. Some permits have ongoing compliance monitoring requirements that persist through Stages 4 and 5 — these are tagged Secondary for Stage 3 (ongoing permit compliance) and Primary for Stage 5 (effectiveness monitoring).

**Tagging rule — Primary.** Tag Primary for Stage 3 if the competency is specifically necessary for *securing regulatory authorization for management activities* — permit application preparation and submission, agency-specific procedural requirements (Section 404, ESA Sections 7 and 10, Clean Air Act permitting), government-to-government tribal consultation under legal consultation obligation (not just relationship-building), interagency coordination for concurrent authorizations (One Federal Decision frameworks, memoranda of agreement), permit condition negotiation, or cross-jurisdictional coordination in transboundary contexts.

**Tagging rule — Secondary.** Tag Secondary for Stage 3 if the competency supports authorization processes without being distinctive to them — intergovernmental relations skills for agency relationship management, communication skills in agency negotiations, project management for tracking parallel permit timelines, or ecological science knowledge to satisfy permit application technical requirements.

**Relationship to Five Functional Positions.** Integrating & Presenting (translating technical analyses into agency-specific permit formats, coordinating across agencies with different jurisdictions) and Deciding & Directing (agency decision-maker authorizing the permit; applicant lead deciding how to respond to agency conditions) are most prominent. Analyzing & Interpreting appears where technical analysis must be adapted for specific permit requirements. Structuring & Curating appears in permit tracking and compliance documentation. Sensing & Generating is minimal.

## Stage 4: Implementation & Field Operations

**Definition.** Implementation & Field Operations encompasses the execution of management actions on the ground — the physical, operational work through which plans and authorized activities become actual changes to landscapes, ecosystems, and resource conditions. This includes prescribed fire operations, restoration treatments, timber harvests, infrastructure construction and maintenance, invasive species control, wildlife management interventions, precision agriculture operations, and all associated logistics, safety management, equipment operation, crew management, and quality control functions.

**What distinguishes Stage 4 work.** The primary question is *are we executing the authorized management action correctly, safely, and adaptively within established conditions?* Outputs are completed management treatments, operational records, safety incident reports, and real-time quality control documentation. This is the only stage where the primary professional activity is doing things to the land rather than analyzing, planning, authorizing, or evaluating them.

**Functional interpretation — monitoring during implementation.** Real-time monitoring during implementation (fire behavior observation during a prescribed burn; vegetation condition monitoring during an active restoration treatment) is Stage 4 work, answering *are we doing this right?* in service of operational decision-making. When that same monitoring data is later used to evaluate whether the treatment achieved its ecological objectives, it shifts to Stage 5 function. Tag by the question being answered, not by the physical act.

**Boundary — upstream edge (Stage 3 → Stage 4).** Implementation begins when all required authorizations are in hand, resources are mobilized, and field activities commence.

**Boundary — downstream edge (Stage 4 → Stage 5).** Implementation ends when management actions are complete and the primary operational question shifts from *are we doing this right?* to *did it work?* Examples: the prescribed burn is Stage 4; post-fire vegetation monitoring at three months is Stage 5. The harvest is Stage 4; reforestation monitoring is Stage 5.

**Tagging rule — Primary.** Tag Primary for Stage 4 if the competency is specifically necessary for *executing management actions on the ground* — field crew management and operations, prescribed fire execution, restoration treatment implementation, equipment operation and maintenance, field safety management, real-time operational decision-making under field conditions, logistics and field resource management, or hands-on technical skills for specific management interventions.

**Tagging rule — Secondary.** Tag Secondary for Stage 4 if the competency regularly supports implementation without being distinctive to it — project management used to coordinate field operations, ecological science used for field-level adaptive decisions, data collection skills for real-time operational monitoring, or communication skills for coordinating crews and contractors.

**Relationship to Five Functional Positions.** Sensing & Generating dominates — actual generation of physical outcomes and real-time sensing of field conditions. Deciding & Directing is prominent: burn bosses and crew leaders make consequential operational decisions in real time. Integrating & Presenting appears in management of complex multi-crew or multi-agency operations. Analyzing & Interpreting and Structuring & Curating are least active as primary functions.

## Stage 5: Monitoring & Assessment

**Definition.** Monitoring & Assessment encompasses all systematic observation and data collection activities directed toward tracking the outcomes of management actions, evaluating whether management objectives are being met, detecting ecological responses and unintended consequences, and maintaining the information base needed to support adaptive management decisions. This includes vegetation and wildlife monitoring, water quality assessment, remote sensing change detection, long-term trend analysis, permit compliance verification, photo point monitoring, acoustic monitoring, and citizen science programs, as well as the data management, quality control, and statistical analysis that make monitoring data interpretable.

**What distinguishes Stage 5 work.** The primary question is *what is happening to this system, and is it moving in the direction our management objectives specified?* Outputs are monitoring datasets, trend analyses, annual monitoring reports, effectiveness evaluations, compliance verification records, and data management systems. What unifies Stage 5 is the orientation toward *measurement of outcomes against management objectives established in Stage 1*.

**Compliance monitoring rule.** Monitoring conducted to verify that permit conditions are being met (rather than to assess ecological effectiveness) is tagged Primary for Stage 5 (the measurement function governs the Primary tag) and Secondary for Stage 3 (the institutional mandate governs the Secondary tag).

**Boundary — upstream edge (Stage 4 → Stage 5).** The functional test distinguishing Stage 4 monitoring (operational) from Stage 5 monitoring (evaluative) is the question being answered: *are we doing this right?* versus *did it work?*

**Boundary — downstream edge (Stage 5 → Stage 6).** Monitoring produces data; Adaptive Management interprets it. The transition is from *what is happening?* to *what does it mean, and should we change course?* Formal boundary: when a practitioner stops characterizing what monitoring data shows and begins evaluating what it means for the adequacy of current management decisions, they have crossed into Stage 6.

**Tagging rule — Primary.** Tag Primary for Stage 5 if the competency is specifically necessary for *systematically measuring ecosystem or management system conditions against defined objectives* — field monitoring protocol design and execution, wildlife survey methods, water quality sampling and analysis, vegetation sampling and analysis, remote sensing for condition assessment and change detection, ecological data collection and management, statistical analysis of monitoring data, effectiveness evaluation methods, permit compliance monitoring, or long-term ecological data management and trend analysis.

**Tagging rule — Secondary.** Tag Secondary for Stage 5 if the competency supports monitoring without being its primary application — general ecological science used to interpret monitoring results in ecological context, data science used to build monitoring databases and visualization tools, communication skills for reporting monitoring results to stakeholders, or project management used to coordinate large monitoring programs.

**Relationship to Five Functional Positions.** Sensing & Generating (field data collection, sensor operation, wildlife survey execution) and Structuring & Curating (organization, quality control, and management of accumulated monitoring data) are most heavily engaged. Analyzing & Interpreting is prominent where statistical analysis and ecological interpretation are required. Integrating & Presenting appears at the boundary with Stage 6 where monitoring results are synthesized for management review. Deciding & Directing is minimally engaged — decisions following from monitoring findings belong to Stage 6.

## Stage 6: Adaptive Management & Learning

**Definition.** Adaptive Management & Learning encompasses the processes through which monitoring results are evaluated against management objectives, management approaches are revised in light of evidence, new scientific understanding is incorporated into ongoing management, lessons are documented and shared, and resulting revised understanding is fed back into the next planning cycle. This stage is where the adaptive management loop is closed — or fails to be. Characteristic products are management review decisions, trigger-point assessments (have monitoring results crossed thresholds requiring management revision?), management plan amendments, documented lessons learned, and knowledge products that capture organizational and field learning for future application.

**What distinguishes Stage 6 work.** The primary question is *what do our monitoring results mean for the adequacy of our current management approach, and what should change?* This is structurally different from Stage 1's question because it is explicitly retrospective and requires the institutional and psychological capacity to act on evaluations that may be unwelcome. The competencies most distinctive to Stage 6 are not analytical (those are primarily Stage 5) but interpretive and deliberative: threshold assessment, management review process facilitation, evidence-based revision of stated objectives, communication of inconvenient results to decision-makers, and knowledge management for organizational learning.

**Boundary — upstream edge (Stage 5 → Stage 6).** The transition from monitoring to adaptive management is functionally the transition from characterizing outcomes to evaluating their management implications.

**Boundary — downstream edge (Stage 6 → Stage 1).** Stage 6–Stage 1 boundary is crossed when the team stops evaluating past decisions and begins designing the next management cycle. Stage 6 work ends when the question shifts from *should we change our approach?* to *what specifically should the revised approach be?* The former is Stage 6; the latter is Stage 1.

**Tagging rule — Primary.** Tag Primary for Stage 6 if the competency is specifically necessary for *evaluating management effectiveness and revising management approaches based on evidence* — adaptive management framework operation (trigger-point assessment, threshold identification, decision protocol implementation), effectiveness evaluation against stated management objectives, management review process design and facilitation, knowledge synthesis for management revision, lesson documentation and knowledge management for organizational learning, or communication of evaluation results to decision-makers in forms that support genuine revision decisions rather than confirmation of existing approaches.

**Tagging rule — Secondary.** Tag Secondary for Stage 6 if the competency supports adaptive management without being distinctive to it — statistical analysis used to interpret monitoring trends, ecological science used to contextualize monitoring results, planning skills used to design revised approaches once evaluation is complete, or stakeholder engagement used to communicate evaluation findings to affected parties.

**Relationship to Five Functional Positions.** Analyzing & Interpreting (evaluating monitoring results against objectives and management theory) and Deciding & Directing (management review decisions determining whether approaches continue, are modified, or are abandoned) are most heavily engaged. Integrating & Presenting is prominent where monitoring results must be synthesized across multiple metrics for decision-makers. Structuring & Curating appears in knowledge management — structuring lesson documentation for future use. Sensing & Generating is minimal.

## Cross-Stage Edge Case Guidance

### Monitoring Competencies

Monitoring competencies will be the most frequent edge cases because monitoring activities occur at multiple stages for different functional purposes. Apply the functional test consistently:

| Monitoring function | Stage tag |
|---|---|
| Baseline data collection for planning | Stage 1 (Secondary) or Stage 2 (Secondary) |
| Real-time operational monitoring during implementation | Stage 4 (Secondary) |
| Systematic outcome monitoring against defined objectives | Stage 5 (Primary) |
| Permit compliance verification | Stage 5 (Primary), Stage 3 (Secondary) |
| Evaluation of monitoring trends for management revision | Stage 6 (Primary or Secondary) |

A monitoring protocol design competency is most often Primary for Stage 5, with Secondary for Stage 1 (where the monitoring plan is designed as part of the adaptive management framework) and possibly Secondary for Stage 6 (where the protocol may need redesign based on what worked). It does not earn tags for Stages 2, 3, or 4 unless the specific competency description explicitly addresses monitoring in those contexts.

### Stakeholder Engagement Competencies

Tag by stated purpose in the competency description, not the full range of contexts where engagement might occur. This prevents Domain 5 engagement competencies from receiving four or five stage tags, which would render the workflow filter useless.

| Stated competency purpose | Primary stage tag |
|---|---|
| Public comment process management | Stage 2 |
| Collaborative planning facilitation | Stage 1 |
| Scoping and needs identification with stakeholders | Stage 1 |
| Agency relationship management for permitting | Stage 3 (Secondary) |
| Community monitoring program engagement | Stage 5 (Secondary) |
| Communication of evaluation results to stakeholders | Stage 6 (Secondary) |

## Relationship to the Five Functional Positions: Synthesis Table

| Workflow Stage | Dominant Functional Positions | Primary Information Flow |
|---|---|---|
| 1. Planning & Strategy | Integrating, Deciding | Inward synthesis → forward design |
| 2. Environmental Review | Analyzing, Integrating, Deciding | Multi-directional evaluation and documentation |
| 3. Permitting & Authorization | Integrating, Deciding | Institutional navigation → authorization |
| 4. Implementation & Field Operations | Sensing, Deciding | Outward action → real-time feedback |
| 5. Monitoring & Assessment | Sensing, Structuring, Analyzing | Inward data collection → dataset construction |
| 6. Adaptive Management & Learning | Analyzing, Deciding | Retrospective evaluation → forward revision |

**Key structural observations.** The Sensing position is almost entirely concentrated in Stages 4 and 5 — bottlenecks in Sensing capacity most directly impair the Stage 5 information base, which cascades to impair Stage 6. The Deciding position appears prominently across four stages (1, 2, 3, 6), suggesting decision-making capacity is a cross-cycle constraint. The Stage 5–Stage 6 boundary is where Structuring ends and Analyzing begins — the point where information most frequently stalls in practice.

## Relationship to Fieldcraft / Datacraft / Statecraft

| Workflow Stage | Primary Craft | Secondary Craft | Notes |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1. Planning & Strategy | Statecraft | Datacraft | Institutional navigation and stakeholder process defining; data supports analysis |
| 2. Environmental Review | Datacraft + Statecraft (equal) | Fieldcraft | Impact assessment is analytical; regulatory compliance is institutional |
| 3. Permitting & Authorization | Statecraft | Datacraft | Regulatory navigation and agency coordination defining; technical analysis supports applications |
| 4. Implementation & Field Operations | Fieldcraft | Statecraft | Physical execution defining; operational coordination secondary |
| 5. Monitoring & Assessment | Fieldcraft + Datacraft (equal) | — | Field data collection and data management co-equal |
| 6. Adaptive Management & Learning | Datacraft + Statecraft (equal) | — | Analytical interpretation and institutional capacity to act co-equal |

Stages 2 and 6 both show equal co-primary craft distributions. Use craft tags at the competency level — where each competency will have a clearer craft identity — and treat the stage-level craft distribution as a descriptive summary, not a prescriptive filter.

## Domain 7 Architecture

Domain 7 (Cross-Cutting Professional Practice) was established in Task 14 as the taxonomy's integration layer — competencies that apply across all six subject-matter domains and all profession families, addressing the governance, ethical, and human-AI teaming capacities that no single domain captures but all practitioners require.

**Code scheme:** Standard D.SA.N format. 7.1 = Professional Ethics Architecture (4 competencies). 7.2 = Human-AI Professional Practice (3 competencies). GitBook renders domain as "Cross-Cutting Professional Practice" without a "Domain 7" label, preserving the cross-cutting framing for the publication layer.

**Domain character summary:**
- 7 competencies total; 2 skill areas
- Craft: SC Primary = 4 (7.1.1, 7.1.2, 7.1.3, 7.2.2); DC Primary = 3 (7.1.4 is SC, 7.2.1, 7.2.3 are DC — correction: 7.1.4 is SC; DC Primary = 7.2.1 and 7.2.3)
  SC Primary: 7.1.1, 7.1.2, 7.1.3, 7.1.4, 7.2.2 = 5
  DC Primary: 7.2.1, 7.2.3 = 2
- Stability: Transition = 3 (7.1.1, 7.1.2, 7.1.3); Emergent = 4 (7.1.4, 7.2.1, 7.2.2, 7.2.3)
- Automation: All tasks H/Human-Essential across all 7 competencies — no automation trajectory. Domain 7 exists as the layer that governs and maintains human judgment under automation pressure.
- CLD: Zero CLD flags across all 7 competencies. Domain 7 competencies are themselves the institutional response to CLD elsewhere in the taxonomy.
- Reform exposure: 8 total flags — digital-permitting (5), NEPA-timeline (3), consultation-reform (1), CatEx-scope (1)

**Standing Rule R10 (established Task 14A):** Domain 7.1 ethical lenses use E/A only — no I values. The constitutive test applies: E requires that removing the lens would collapse the competency's argument. This rule does not apply to Domain 7.2, which uses the standard E/I/A framework.

**Delphi flag — Domain 7.2 craft diversity:** Skill Area 7.2 contains mixed craft (DC primary for 7.2.1 and 7.2.3; SC primary for 7.2.2). This is consistent with Domain 7's cross-cutting structural function but warrants explicit Delphi panel question: do reviewers experience these three competencies as a coherent enough cluster to warrant a shared skill area, or do they belong distributed by craft to home domains (7.2.1 → Domain 2, 7.2.3 → Domain 3/6)?

## SKILL AREA 7.1: PROFESSIONAL ETHICS ARCHITECTURE

Skill area character: All four competencies are SC Primary and Human-Essential. The defining professional contribution in each case is navigating or designing institutional frameworks — governance agreements, authorization processes, organizational integrity systems, AI adoption policy — that cannot be reduced to technical analysis or field execution. All four carry high tacit density (Hi) because the judgment about when institutional pressures are legitimate versus when they violate professional obligations is tacit professional knowledge, not rule-following. No CLD flags: these competencies cannot themselves be automated without defeating their purpose, which is the preservation of human governance authority.

### 7.1.1 — Indigenous data sovereignty and governance

**Description:** Applying Indigenous data sovereignty principles to ensure that tribal nations and Indigenous communities retain governance authority over data collected about their lands, peoples, and knowledge systems.

**Job tasks:**
- T1: Apply CARE Principles alongside FAIR Principles in data governance protocols for Indigenous lands or knowledge systems
- T2: Negotiate data governance agreements with tribal governments specifying ownership, access, repatriation rights, and use restrictions
- T3: Audit organizational data practices for compliance with Indigenous data sovereignty obligations and develop institutional protocols

**Craft: SC Primary | No Secondary**
Rationale: The irreducible contribution is governance framework navigation — negotiating between data systems, institutional mandates, and tribal sovereignty authority. The practitioner's defining deliverable is a governance arrangement (agreement, protocol, compliance program) that preserves tribal authority. Technical data management work (FAIR/CARE implementation) is upstream input, not the defining contribution. SC-only at intermediate proficiency — a practitioner who understands the governance architecture but delegates data management to a DC collaborator can still fulfill the competency's core purpose.

**Workflow Stage: S1 Primary | S2 Secondary**
Primary: Framework design (S1) is the dominant function — the competency is fundamentally about designing governance architectures (CARE-compliant data governance frameworks, organizational compliance protocols, institutional data sovereignty programs). Secondary: Environmental review (S2) activates formal consultation obligations and data governance requirements in the context of federal permitting and environmental assessment.

**Stability Tier: Transition**
Reform sensitivity flag: consultation-reform (1 flag). Consultation reform — expedited review timelines, potential modifications to formal consultation requirements — directly affects the procedural landscape in which data sovereignty governance operates. The underlying sovereignty obligations are durable; the specific procedural context for activating them is in transition.

**Ethical Lenses (Standing Rule R10 — E/A only):**
- L1 Equity & Justice: E
  Constitutive. Data sovereignty is inseparable from the justice question of who controls the representation of Indigenous lands, peoples, and knowledge. Removing L1 would collapse the competency's argument — the entire framing of sovereignty as a rights issue rather than a data management protocol depends on L1.
- L2 Cultural Rights & Self-Determination: E
  Constitutive. This competency IS L2 application — tribal nations' authority to govern data about their lands and peoples is a cultural self-determination right, not merely a privacy or property right. CARE Principles (Collective Benefit, Authority to Control, Responsibility, Ethics) are the operationalization of L2. Removing L2 would leave a competency about data management protocols with no account of why tribal governance authority is the organizing principle.
- L3 Transparency & Accountability: A
  Transparency obligations are present but operate as background professional norms, not as the constitutive argument of the competency. The competency could be fully stated without naming L3.
- L4 Intervention Ethics: A
  Authorization and precaution are not the primary ethical frame of this competency. Data governance and intervention authorization are distinct professional domains.
- L5 Professional Integrity: A
  Professional integrity obligations are present as background norms but are not what defines the competency's ethical architecture.

**Profession Families:**
Core (5): F2 Wildlife & Fisheries, F5 Conservation Science, F7 Environmental Data Science, F8 Environmental Policy, F13 Environmental Health & Justice
Rationale: Field research contexts (F2, F5) where monitoring on tribal lands or TEK documentation triggers sovereignty obligations; data science (F7) where the competency's CARE/FAIR distinction is operationally central; policy contexts (F8) where tribal consultation and data governance intersect; EJ contexts (F13) where Indigenous communities are among the primary populations facing environmental decisions.
Supporting (10): F1 Forestry, F3 Ecological Restoration, F4 Water Resources, F6 Climate Science, F9 Land Use Planning, F10 Sustainable Agriculture, F11 Environmental Education, F12 Fire Management
Rationale: All other families encounter data sovereignty obligations in project-specific contexts but it is not a defining structural feature of their practice.
Absent: F14 Corporate Sustainability, F15 Conservation Finance
Rationale: Corporate sustainability and conservation finance contexts rarely involve direct tribal data governance obligations at the level that warrants Core designation, though adjacency exists where projects affect tribal lands.

**Exposure Classification:**
- T1 (Apply CARE/FAIR Principles in governance protocols): H / Hi / P / — / N
  Human-Essential: The judgment about whether a data governance protocol respects tribal sovereignty authority — distinguishing surface compliance from genuine governance recognition — requires culturally embedded knowledge and institutional relationship that cannot be automated. Career=P: applying CARE Principles with guidance is foundational-level work; it is the primary developmental task for junior practitioners entering this competency space.
- T2 (Negotiate data governance agreements): H / Hi / P / — / N
  Human-Essential: Binding government-to-government governance negotiations require human relationship, institutional trust, and culturally grounded judgment about what provisions will genuinely protect tribal authority. Career=P: junior practitioners participate in negotiations with supervision; the full contribution is present-level work that is developmentally formative.
- T3 (Audit practices and develop compliance programs): H / Hi / C / — / N
  Human-Essential: Compliance program design requires professional judgment about institutional change management, regulatory interpretation, and organizational capacity. Career=C: designing compliance programs requires intermediate-level organizational authority and domain expertise; not appropriate as P-level formation work.
- Modal: H / Hi / P / — / N
  CLD Flag: FALSE. No career ladder destruction signal. The P-modal career ladder position reflects that validation work (T1, T2) is genuinely formative at entry level — this is a governance competency that develops through participation, not through administrative task execution that AI could compress.

### 7.1.2 — Intervention authorization and ecological precaution

**Description:** Evaluating proposed ecological interventions against reversibility, historical precedent, and authorization standards to determine whether precautionary criteria are met before proceeding.

**Job tasks:**
- T1: Evaluate proposed ecological interventions against reversibility, precedent, and uncertainty criteria to determine whether precautionary triggers apply
- T2: Identify parties with authorization standing and design engagement processes satisfying authorization requirements
- T3: Develop intervention decision frameworks specifying authorization requirements, reversibility thresholds, monitoring obligations, and exit conditions

**Craft: SC Primary | DC Secondary ⚑**
Rationale: SC Primary: The irreducible contribution is the governance determination — who has standing to authorize this intervention, what criteria must be met, and whether precautionary thresholds are crossed. This is a framework navigation and governance design deliverable. Removing SC collapses the competency.
DC Secondary: Warranted — and contested. The argument for DC secondary: evaluating reversibility thresholds, uncertainty levels, and historical precedent requires analytical judgment that a SC-only practitioner without DC analytical capacity would substantially impair at intermediate proficiency. The counter-argument (adjudicated in session, confirmed): analytical work could be delegated to upstream DC collaborators, but the practitioner who cannot independently assess reversibility and uncertainty cannot make a defensible precautionary determination. The analytical dimension is structurally embedded in the governance judgment, not separable from it. DC secondary held. ⚑ Delphi: present the DC secondary argument as an explicit panel question — does the profession experience precautionary assessment as requiring integrated analytical-governance capacity, or is the analytical dimension routinely delegated?
Precedent: 6.1.1 uses SC Primary + DC Secondary on the same grounds (legal analysis requires both regulatory navigation and analytical capacity). Consistent application.

**Workflow Stage: S1 Primary | S2 Secondary**
Primary: Framework design — developing intervention authorization frameworks and precautionary criteria is S1 planning and governance design work. Secondary: Environmental review (S2) is the operational context where precautionary assessment most frequently occurs — alternatives analysis in NEPA, ESA consultation, tribal engagement for intervention proposals.

**Stability Tier: Transition**
Reform sensitivity flags: NEPA-timeline (1), CatEx-scope (1) — 2 flags total.
NEPA-timeline: Expedited review timelines compress the precautionary review window — the amount of time available for deliberate evaluation of reversibility, uncertainty, and authorization standing is directly reduced. CatEx-scope: Categorical exclusion expansion reduces the set of projects that trigger full precautionary review, shrinking the operational domain of this competency.

**Ethical Lenses (Standing Rule R10 — E/A only):**
- L1 Equity & Justice: A
  Equity dimensions are present in intervention authorization contexts but are not the constitutive argument of this competency. The competency could be fully stated without L1.
- L2 Cultural Rights & Self-Determination: A
  Tribal consultation for interventions on or near tribal lands is present in the task descriptions (T2) but L2 sovereignty obligations operate as one category of authorization standing within a broader framework, not as the competency's constitutive principle. L2=A correctly distinguished from 7.1.1 where L2 is constitutive.
- L3 Transparency & Accountability: A
  Transparency in communicating precautionary determinations is present but background.
- L4 Intervention Ethics: E
  Constitutive. Authorization and precaution ARE this competency — removing L4 leaves a competency about process management with no account of why the authorization question matters. The precautionary principle as an operational governance criterion is the direct expression of L4.
- L5 Professional Integrity: A
  Professional integrity is present as background obligation but not what defines the competency's ethical architecture.

**Profession Families:**
Core (6): F3 Ecological Restoration, F4 Water Resources, F5 Conservation Science, F6 Climate Science, F8 Environmental Policy, F12 Fire Management
Rationale: Restoration (F3) — assisted migration, species reintroduction, habitat modification decisions are paradigm intervention authorization cases. Water (F4) — hydrological modification decisions (dam removal, diversion, flow augmentation) require precautionary authorization. Conservation science (F5) — genetic rescue, assisted evolution proposals require intervention authorization frameworks. Climate (F6) — managed retreat, assisted migration, solar radiation management all require precautionary governance. Policy (F8) — policy frameworks for novel interventions are a primary product of this competency at advanced/expert levels. Fire management (F12) — burn boss go/no-go authorization decisions are paradigm high-consequence intervention authorization calls. ⚑ Delphi: F12 Core designation was contested — fire management intervention logic is operational (in-field) rather than governance-oriented (institutional framework design). Confirmed Core on grounds that prescribed burn authorization is a formal institutional authorization process, not just operational judgment, and that burn bosses make explicit go/no-go governance calls against documented criteria.
Supporting (5): F1 Forestry, F2 Wildlife & Fisheries, F9 Land Use Planning, F10 Sustainable Agriculture, F13 Environmental Health & Justice
Absent: F7 Environmental Data Science, F11 Environmental Education, F14 Corporate Sustainability, F15 Conservation Finance

**Exposure Classification:**
- T1 (Evaluate intervention against precautionary criteria): H / Hi / C / — / N
  Human-Essential: Judgment about whether precautionary triggers apply in a specific context — reading ecological uncertainty, historical precedent, and reversibility against governance criteria — requires domain expertise and institutional judgment that cannot be automated. Career=C: applying precautionary frameworks to specific cases requires intermediate knowledge of both the ecological domain and the authorization landscape; not appropriate as P-level formation work.
- T2 (Identify authorization standing, design engagement): H / Hi / C / — / N
  Human-Essential: Mapping authorization standing across regulatory, tribal, and landowner contexts and designing processes that satisfy those requirements requires institutional relationship and contextual judgment. Career=C: intermediate-level competency requiring organizational authority.
- T3 (Develop intervention decision frameworks): H / Hi / X / — / N
  Human-Essential: Designing authorization frameworks that will function across multiple intervention types and contested multi-stakeholder contexts requires advanced-level professional judgment. Career=X: framework design is advanced-to-expert work; not a primary formation task.

### 7.1.3 — Regulatory professional integrity

**Description:** Maintaining independent professional judgment and transparent reporting practices when institutional pressures conflict with obligations to regulatory processes, the public, and ecological systems.

**Job tasks:**
- T1: Identify and document situations where institutional pressures conflict with obligations to produce honest regulatory findings
- T2: Apply professional codes of ethics and scientific integrity standards to regulatory contexts (conflict disclosure, uncertainty, dissenting views)
- T3: Develop organizational QA protocols protecting the independence of regulatory findings from institutional capture

**Craft: SC Primary | No Secondary**
Rationale: The irreducible contribution is navigating the institutional relationship between practitioner obligations and organizational pressures — the governance of professional integrity within organizations. Removing SC leaves a competency about applying ethics codes with no account of the institutional dynamics that make those applications contested. No DC secondary: analytical content is present (documenting situations, applying standards) but does not rise to the threshold where a SC-only practitioner would be substantially impaired; the analytical work is an input to institutional navigation, not co-equal with it.

**Workflow Stage: S2 Primary | S5 Secondary**
Primary: Environmental review (S2) — the context where regulatory science integrity is most acutely at risk. NEPA analysis, ESA biological opinions, CWA certifications, and Section 106 determinations are the contexts where practitioners most directly face the tension between employer interests and regulatory obligations. Secondary: Monitoring and assessment (S5) — monitoring findings that contradict management interests or prior regulatory commitments create the same integrity pressure in an operational rather than review context.

**Stability Tier: Transition**
Reform sensitivity flags: NEPA-timeline (1), digital-permitting (1) — 2 flags total.
NEPA-timeline: Expedited review timelines reduce the time available for independent assessment, creating structural pressure to accept findings that serve timeline rather than accuracy. digital-permitting: AI-generated regulatory analyses create novel pressure vectors — practitioners face pressure to accept AI outputs rather than exercise independent validation, introducing a new category of integrity challenge not present in pre-AI regulatory practice.

**Ethical Lenses (Standing Rule R10 — E/A only):**
- L1 Equity & Justice: A
  Present as background — EJ communities bear the cost when regulatory integrity fails. But the competency's argument does not require L1 to be constitutive.
- L2 Cultural Rights & Self-Determination: A
  Present where tribal consultation findings are at issue, but not constitutive of the competency's architecture.
- L3 Transparency & Accountability: E
  Constitutive. The transparency obligation — honest disclosure, uncertainty communication, dissenting view documentation — is literally what the competency describes in its job tasks. Removing L3 would leave a competency about "maintaining independent judgment" with no account of what that judgment must produce: transparent, accurate regulatory findings.
- L4 Intervention Ethics: A
  Not the primary ethical frame of this competency.
- L5 Professional Integrity: E
  Constitutive. The practitioner character obligation — maintaining independent judgment under institutional pressure, not yielding to capture — is the other pillar of the competency alongside L3. L3 and L5 are jointly necessary: L3 is what the practitioner must produce (transparent findings); L5 is the professional character that makes production possible under pressure.
Note: L3 and L5 co-constitutive assignment is consistent with the competency's dual structure. L3 alone would leave a competency about transparency without the professional character that sustains it. L5 alone would leave a competency about character without the specific transparency obligations it requires.

**Profession Families:**
Core (5): F5 Conservation Science, F7 Environmental Data Science, F8 Environmental Policy, F13 Environmental Health & Justice, F14 Corporate Sustainability
Rationale: Conservation science (F5) — scientific integrity in regulatory submissions is a structural concern for this family. Data science (F7) — data integrity in regulatory contexts; practitioners frequently occupy the position of producing the analytical products that regulatory decisions rely on. Policy (F8) — regulatory practitioners are the primary professional family for whom this competency is operationally central. EJ (F13) — integrity failures in regulatory science disproportionately harm EJ communities; EJ practitioners carry this awareness as a structural professional concern. Corporate sustainability (F14) — ESG reporting integrity and the independence of sustainability disclosures from investor and management pressure is the private-sector analog.
Supporting (9): F1, F2, F3, F4, F6, F9, F10, F11, F12
Absent: F15 Conservation Finance
Rationale: Conservation finance practitioners engage regulatory systems but their primary integrity concerns are financial disclosure rather than scientific/regulatory integrity in the sense this competency addresses.

**Exposure Classification:**
- T1 (Identify and document integrity conflicts): H / Hi / C / — / N
  Human-Essential: Recognizing when institutional pressure has crossed into a professional integrity violation requires judgment about organizational culture, power dynamics, and professional obligation that cannot be automated. Career=C: documenting integrity conflicts requires intermediate-level organizational authority and professional confidence; not appropriate as P-level entry task.
- T2 (Apply ethics frameworks to regulatory contexts): H / Hi / P / — / N
  Human-Essential: Applying ethics codes to one's own professional conduct is a self-governance judgment. Career=P: applying professional codes of ethics to one's own work is correctly a P-level (foundational, participatory) task. Practitioners apply ethics standards from day one. ⚑ Decision confirmed in session: T2 is P rather than C because ethics application to one's own conduct is a primary formation task, not a gatekept intermediate capacity.
- T3 (Develop organizational QA protocols): H / Hi / X / — / N
  Human-Essential: Designing institutional systems that protect regulatory independence requires advanced organizational authority and professional knowledge. Career=X: advanced-to-expert work; organizational protocol design is peripheral as a formation task.
- Modal: H / Hi / P / — / N

### 7.1.4 — AI governance in environmental decision-support

**Description:** Making and implementing organizational decisions about AI adoption in environmental management that preserve professional judgment capacity, maintain accountability, and prevent knowledge ecology degradation.

**Job tasks:**
- T1: Evaluate proposed AI adoption against accountability, explainability, professional judgment preservation, and equitable impact criteria
- T2: Design human-in-the-loop oversight architectures defining practitioner roles, escalation triggers, and accountability pathways
- T3: Monitor and adapt AI governance frameworks in response to observed impacts on competency formation, data quality, and decision integrity

**Craft: SC Primary | No Secondary**
Rationale: The irreducible contribution is organizational governance — the institutional decisions about whether and how to adopt AI tools, and the governance architectures that determine whether adoption preserves or degrades professional judgment. Technical AI evaluation (explainability, accountability criteria) is upstream input; the defining deliverable is a governance decision and its implementing architecture. DC secondary not warranted: a practitioner with strong SC governance skills can make sound AI adoption decisions by engaging DC collaborators for technical evaluation; the governance judgment does not require DC co-equal capacity.

**Workflow Stage: S1 Primary | S6 Secondary**
Primary: Framework design (S1) — organizational AI governance policy and oversight architecture design is planning-phase work. Secondary: Adaptive management of AI adoption (S6) — monitoring whether deployed AI governance is producing intended outcomes (knowledge ecology preservation, decision integrity, equitable impact) and revising governance frameworks based on observed results is a direct S6 function. This is the first S6 involvement in Domain 7.1, consistent with the evaluative/adaptive character of governance monitoring.

**Stability Tier: Emergent**
New professional territory. No established practice canon, no recognized professional standard, no precedent body of professional literature on AI governance specifically in environmental management contexts. Reform flag: digital-permitting (1) — AI-assisted permitting creates the primary operational context where AI governance decisions become consequential.

**Ethical Lenses (Standing Rule R10 — E/A only):**
- L1 Equity & Justice: A
  Equitable impact on affected communities is named in T1 as a governance criterion, which might appear to warrant L1=E. However, equity is one criterion within the broader governance framework, not the constitutive principle of the competency. Removing L1 would not collapse the argument; the competency's core is about preserving professional judgment capacity and maintaining accountability in AI adoption decisions.
- L2 Cultural Rights & Self-Determination: A
  Not constitutive.
- L3 Transparency & Accountability: A
  Accountability is named explicitly as a governance criterion (T1, T2). The case for L3=E is real but does not meet the constitutive threshold under R10 — the competency would not collapse without L3; accountability is one dimension of the governance framework, not its defining principle.
- L4 Intervention Ethics: E
  Constitutive. AI adoption decisions are authorization decisions — they determine whether and under what conditions AI systems are permitted to operate in professional decision contexts. The authorization logic of L4 (who has standing to authorize, what precautionary criteria apply, what reversibility thresholds govern) maps directly onto AI governance. Removing L4 would leave a competency about organizational process management with no account of why AI adoption requires governance rather than just management.
- L5 Professional Integrity: E
  Constitutive. The knowledge ecology argument — that AI adoption decisions affect the conditions under which professional judgment forms and persists — is a professional integrity argument. The competency explicitly addresses whether AI adoption preserves or erodes the capacity for professional judgment. L5 is not a background obligation here; it is the substantive argument for why AI governance is a professional responsibility rather than a management decision.
Note: L4 and L5 co-constitutive assignment parallels the L3+L5 structure in 7.1.3 — two lenses that are jointly necessary to capture the full ethical architecture. L4 alone frames AI adoption as an authorization decision without explaining why professional character is implicated. L5 alone frames it as a professional stewardship concern without the governance architecture that specifies who authorizes and on what criteria.

**Profession Families:**
Core (4): F7 Environmental Data Science, F8 Environmental Policy, F13 Environmental Health & Justice, F14 Corporate Sustainability
Rationale: Data science (F7) — primary AI adopters in environmental practice; AI governance decisions fall most directly on practitioners who manage AI deployment in environmental workflows. Policy (F8) — regulatory contexts are where AI governance failures produce the most consequential outcomes; policy practitioners are primary governance decision-makers. EJ (F13) — AI adoption decisions have equity implications for overburdened communities; EJ practitioners carry governance responsibility for equitable AI deployment. Corporate sustainability (F14) — ESG and compliance AI adoption is the private-sector governance context where this competency is most directly exercised.
Supporting (11): F1, F2, F3, F4, F5, F6, F9, F10, F11, F12, F15
Rationale: All other families encounter AI governance decisions in their operational contexts but are not primary governance decision-makers at the organizational level.

**Exposure Classification:**
- T1 (Evaluate proposed AI adoption): H / Hi / C / — / N
  Human-Essential: Evaluating AI adoption against professional judgment preservation criteria requires professional domain expertise and governance judgment. Career=C: contributing-level work — evaluation requires intermediate knowledge of both AI capabilities and the professional domain in which adoption is proposed.
- T2 (Design human-in-the-loop architectures): H / Hi / C / — / N
  Human-Essential: Designing oversight architectures that define human roles, escalation triggers, and accountability pathways requires institutional design judgment that cannot itself be automated. Career=C: intermediate-level organizational authority required.
- T3 (Monitor and adapt governance frameworks): H / Hi / X / — / N
  Human-Essential: Adaptive management of AI governance frameworks — evaluating whether governance is working and redesigning when it is not — requires advanced organizational authority and domain expertise. Career=X: advanced-to-expert work.
- Modal: H / Hi / C / — / N

## SKILL AREA 7.2: HUMAN-AI PROFESSIONAL PRACTICE

Skill area character: Three competencies addressing human-AI teaming as a practitioner-level professional capacity, distinct from technical AI use (Domain 2.6) and organizational AI governance (7.1.4). The gap these competencies address is the daily professional judgment work of a practitioner operating inside an AI-augmented workflow: validating what the AI produced, structuring workflows so junior practitioners still develop the judgment the AI requires them to have, and maintaining calibrated trust in AI outputs. All three are Human-Essential; automation bias is itself the risk these competencies protect against.

Mixed craft architecture: DC Primary for 7.2.1 and 7.2.3 (the analytical evaluation of information products); SC Primary for 7.2.2 (the institutional workflow and mentoring architecture design). This mixed-craft pattern within a single skill area is unusual but consistent with Domain 7's cross-cutting structural function. ⚑ Delphi flag: present as explicit panel question — coherent skill area or distribute to home domains by craft?

Ethical lenses: Domain 7.2 uses the standard E/I/A framework (not R10). Session-confirmed values: L3=I and L5=I for 7.2.1 and 7.2.3; L5=I for 7.2.2. These are NOT currently populated in the database (stored as None). Flag for Task 15 population — these should be the first entries written in Task 15 before proceeding to Domains 1–6.

### 7.2.1 — Validating AI outputs against ecological and regulatory ground truth

**Description:** Evaluating AI-generated information products against independent ecological knowledge, field observation, and regulatory standards to ensure outputs are accurate, appropriately scoped, and professionally defensible before use in management or regulatory contexts.

**Job tasks:**
- T1: Evaluate AI-generated species identifications, habitat assessments, or ecological predictions against field observations and established ecological knowledge
- T2: Assess AI-assisted regulatory analyses against applicable legal standards and regulatory interpretive guidance
- T3: Document validation findings and communicate the basis and confidence level of validation judgments to decision-makers

**Craft: DC Primary | No Secondary**
Rationale: The irreducible contribution is analytical — evaluating an information product (AI output) against independent evidence (ecological knowledge, regulatory standards) and making a defensible accuracy determination. The practitioner's value is the analytical judgment; removing DC would leave no competency. SC secondary was considered and rejected: while validation findings have institutional consequences (regulatory submissions, management decisions), a practitioner with strong DC skills and no SC skills can perform validation at intermediate proficiency. The institutional consequences flow from the analysis but are not co-equal with it. Contrast with 7.2.3 where SC secondary is warranted because calibration is actively eroded by institutional pressure.

**Workflow Stage: S5 Primary | S2 Secondary**
Primary: Monitoring and assessment (S5) — validation occurs against monitoring outputs as the modal operational context. AI-generated species counts, habitat assessments, and ecological condition predictions are monitoring products requiring validation before use. Secondary: Environmental review (S2) — AI-assisted NEPA analysis, ESA biological assessments, and CWA certifications require validation before regulatory submission.

**Stability Tier: Emergent**
New professional territory. Validation of AI outputs as a named professional competency has no established practice canon. Reform flag: digital-permitting (1) — AI-assisted permitting creates the primary context where validation decisions are consequential for regulatory outcomes.

**Ethical Lenses (standard E/I/A — NOT R10):**
⚠️ Values below are session-confirmed but NOT yet populated in database. Flag for Task 15.
- L1 Equity & Justice: A (session judgment — equity implications are present but background)
- L2 Cultural Rights & Self-Determination: A
- L3 Transparency & Accountability: I (Implicit)
  Validation work is fundamentally about making the basis of analytical claims visible — documenting why an AI output was accepted, qualified, or rejected. This is a transparency obligation even when not named as such in the competency text. The competency text describes the practice (documentation, communication of confidence levels) without naming the ethical principle that makes documentation obligatory rather than optional.
- L4 Intervention Ethics: A
- L5 Professional Integrity: I (Implicit)
  Validating rather than rubber-stamping AI outputs is a professional integrity obligation. The competency describes the practice without naming the character obligation that sustains it — practitioners who nominally validate but substantively accept AI outputs regardless of accuracy are violating professional integrity even if no code explicitly names this failure mode.

**Profession Families:**
Core (3): F5 Conservation Science, F7 Environmental Data Science, F8 Environmental Policy
Rationale: Conservation science (F5) — ecological validity is the primary ground truth domain; conservation science practitioners are the primary holders of the ecological knowledge that makes validation substantive. Data science (F7) — validation methodology and the understanding of AI model failure modes falls centrally within data science professional practice. Policy (F8) — regulatory validity is the second ground truth domain; policy practitioners hold the regulatory knowledge that makes regulatory validation substantive.
Supporting (9): F1, F2, F3, F4, F6, F9, F10, F12, F13
Absent: F11 Environmental Education, F14 Corporate Sustainability, F15 Conservation Finance

**Exposure Classification:**
- T1 (Evaluate AI ecological outputs): H / Hi / P / — / N
  Human-Essential: Ecological validation requires the ecological ground truth that the AI is producing outputs about. The competency exists to be the human layer — it cannot itself be automated without self-defeating its purpose. Career=P: this is the primary entry-level formation task in AI-augmented ecological practice. Junior practitioners develop ecological and regulatory judgment precisely by performing validation — it is formative work, not incidental documentation. ⚑ Decision confirmed in session: P is correct. Validation against ground truth is where early-career practitioners exercise and develop independent ecological judgment; automating or gating this task would compress the formation pathway it provides.
- T2 (Assess AI regulatory analyses): H / Hi / P / — / N
  Human-Essential: Regulatory validation requires the regulatory knowledge that makes validation substantive — legal standards, agency interpretive practice, regulatory history. Career=P: same argument as T1 in the regulatory domain. Junior regulatory practitioners develop regulatory judgment through the practice of independently assessing AI-assisted analyses.
- T3 (Document validation findings): H / Hi / C / — / N
  Human-Essential: Documentation of validation reasoning in formats appropriate for regulatory use requires intermediate professional judgment about what constitutes sufficient basis for acceptance, qualification, or rejection. ### 7.2.2 — Designing workflows for tacit knowledge formation in AI-augmented practice

**Description:** Structuring human roles, mentoring sequences, and deliberate practice opportunities within AI-augmented workflows to ensure that practitioners develop foundational tacit knowledge that AI tools are compressing at entry level.

**Job tasks:**
- T1: Analyze an AI-augmented workflow to identify which entry-level formation tasks have been compressed by automation and what tacit knowledge practitioners are no longer developing
- T2: Design deliberate practice sequences, mentoring structures, and staged exposure protocols that rebuild development pathways compressed by AI adoption
- T3: Evaluate whether redesigned workflows are producing intended formation outcomes and revise in response to observed gaps

**Craft: SC Primary | No Secondary**
Rationale: The irreducible contribution is designing institutional workflows and mentoring architectures — the organizational and procedural infrastructure through which practitioners develop within AI-augmented practice. Removing SC leaves a technical task analysis with no delivery mechanism — knowing which formation pathways are compressed without designing the institutional response is not the competency. DC secondary not warranted: a practitioner with strong SC organizational design skills can design effective formation workflows by engaging DC collaborators for technical task analysis; the organizational architecture does not require DC co-equal capacity at intermediate proficiency.

**Workflow Stage: S1 Primary | S6 Secondary**
Primary: Planning and framework design (S1) — workflow architecture and mentoring protocol design is planning work. Secondary: Adaptive management and learning (S6) — evaluating whether designed workflows are producing intended formation outcomes and revising them in response to observed gaps is direct S6 work. This S6 secondary is the strongest in Domain 7: the feedback loop between formation design (S1) and formation outcome evaluation (S6) is the core operational cycle of this competency at intermediate and advanced proficiency.

**Stability Tier: Emergent**
New professional territory. No established practice canon for formation-aware AI deployment as a named professional competency. Reform flag: digital-permitting (1) — AI-assisted permitting workflows are the primary context where formation pathway compression is most acute and most consequential for regulatory capacity.

### 7.2.3 — Calibrating professional judgment in human-AI collaboration

**Description:** Developing and maintaining accurate self-assessment of when to trust, qualify, or override AI outputs — avoiding both automation bias (over-trust) and automation skepticism (under-trust) — through deliberate reflection on the outcomes of past judgment calls.

**Job tasks:**
- T1: Assess the reliability profile of an AI tool by systematically comparing its outputs to independent evidence, building a calibrated mental model of where and how the tool fails
- T2: Apply calibrated trust in operational contexts — accepting, qualifying, or overriding AI outputs based on reliability envelope position — while documenting the basis of each judgment call
- T3: Evaluate past trust decisions against observed outcomes to update calibration and revise decision heuristics

**Craft: DC Primary | SC Secondary ⚑**
Rationale: DC Primary: The irreducible contribution is analytical — evaluating AI information products against independent evidence and maintaining accurate self-assessment of reliability. Removing DC collapses the competency into a behavioral disposition with no analytical method.
SC Secondary: Warranted. Calibration failures frequently stem from institutional pressure to accept AI outputs — the same pressure that 7.1.3 addresses at the organizational level. A practitioner without SC skills for navigating that pressure would be substantially impaired at intermediate proficiency: they may accurately assess reliability in isolation but yield to institutional pressure in practice, defeating the competency's function. The SC secondary captures the dimension of calibration that requires professional standing and institutional navigation, not just analytical skill. ⚑ Delphi: present the SC secondary argument as a panel question — do practitioners experience calibration failure primarily as an analytical problem (inability to assess reliability) or as an institutional problem (inability to maintain assessed reliability against pressure)?
Note: This is the second DC + SC-sec assignment in the taxonomy after 7.1.2 (SC + DC-sec). The two are structurally inverse: 7.1.2 is governance with analytical secondary; 7.2.3 is analysis with governance secondary.

**Workflow Stage: S6 Primary | S5 Secondary**
Primary: Adaptive management and learning (S6) — calibration is inherently retrospective and adaptive. It improves through cycles of evaluating whether past trust decisions were correct against observed outcomes and revising heuristics accordingly. The learning mechanism is S6 at its core. ⚑ Decision confirmed in session: S6 Primary is correct despite being unusual (only the second S6 Primary assignment in the taxonomy alongside 5.1.5). Counter-argument considered and rejected: calibration is a continuous practice at S5, not a retrospective S6 function. Adjudicated: the continuous practice of applying calibrated trust at S5 is S5 Secondary; the process of updating calibration through outcome evaluation is S6 Primary. The S6 Primary assignment captures the developmental mechanism, not just the deployment context.
Secondary: Monitoring and assessment (S5) — operational deployment of calibrated trust. Monitoring contexts are where calibration decisions are most frequent and most consequential; miscalibrated trust in an ecological monitoring workflow produces systematically biased data.

**Stability Tier: Emergent**
New professional territory. Reform flags: digital-permitting (1), NEPA-timeline (1) — 2 flags total.
digital-permitting: AI-assisted permitting is the primary operational context where calibration decisions are consequential for regulatory outcomes. NEPA-timeline: Expedited review timelines create time pressure that is itself a calibration-eroding condition — practitioners under deadline pressure are more susceptible to automation bias regardless of their calibration in unconstrained contexts.

**Ethical Lenses (standard E/I/A — NOT R10):**

- L1 Equity & Justice: A
- L2 Cultural Rights & Self-Determination: A
- L3 Transparency & Accountability: I (Implicit)
  Documenting the basis of trust decisions and communicating reliability profiles to decision-makers is a transparency obligation. The competency text describes the practice (documentation, communication) without naming the ethical principle that makes it obligatory.
- L4 Intervention Ethics: A
- L5 Professional Integrity: I (Implicit)
  Maintaining independent professional judgment against automation bias — refusing to yield to institutional pressure to accept AI outputs more readily than reliability warrants — is a professional integrity obligation. The parallel with 7.1.3 (maintaining independent judgment under institutional pressure) is structural: 7.1.3 addresses this at the organizational level; 7.2.3 addresses it at the individual practitioner level.

**Profession Families:**
Core (4): F5 Conservation Science, F7 Environmental Data Science, F8 Environmental Policy, F13 Environmental Health & Justice
Rationale: Conservation science (F5) — practitioners are primary users of AI ecological prediction tools; calibration of trust in species distribution models, habitat assessments, and ecological predictions is a foundational professional concern. Data science (F7) — calibration methodology and reliability assessment are core to data science professional practice. Policy (F8) — regulatory practitioners must calibrate trust in AI-assisted analyses before using them in regulatory submissions; the consequence of miscalibration is regulatory error. EJ (F13) — AI miscalibration in EJ contexts can produce systematically biased regulatory outcomes for overburdened communities; EJ practitioners carry particular responsibility for calibrated review.

Career=C: consistent operational application requires the professional authority to override AI outputs when reliability warrants, which requires intermediate standing.

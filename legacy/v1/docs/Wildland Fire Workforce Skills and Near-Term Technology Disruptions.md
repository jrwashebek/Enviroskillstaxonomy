# Wildland Fire Workforce Skills and Near-Term Technology Disruption

## Executive summary

Across the U.S. wildland fire workforce, competency infrastructure is strongest where the work is most standardized for interagency mobilization and incident operations: operational line roles, incident command system positions, and dispatch/coordination functions. The core architecture is performance-based and incident-centered, built around task books, formal training courses, experience prerequisites, fitness/currency requirements, and qualification “cards” that enable national mobilization. This is well documented in entity["book","NWCG Standards for Wildland Fire Position Qualifications (PMS 310-1)","nwcg qualification standard"] and in task-book guidance that explicitly defines competencies/behaviors/tasks and requires observable evaluation in appropriate incident contexts. citeturn6view2turn20view0turn22view0

A second, complementary layer governs program leadership and key fire-and-aviation management roles: entity["book","Interagency Fire Program Management Standard (IFPM)","interagency fire qual standard"] defines minimum qualification standards for a set of key positions spanning first-line supervision, project specialists, and program managers. citeturn6view3turn7view10

In parallel, federal classification reforms (notably the new GS-0456 series) represent an attempt to better define “wildland fire management” work and career paths across prevention, suppression, and management functions—an important structural move for workforce policy, but not itself a competency model. entity["book","Position Classification Standard for Wildland Fire Management, 0456","opm job series standard 2022"] and DOI guidance on the GS-0456 “elect-in” process are primary references. citeturn5view0turn16view2

What the current competency system covers well is thus clear: incident operations, interagency interoperability, standardized qualification pathways, and (increasingly) validated dispatch competencies. entity["book","Wildland Fire Dispatch Competency Model","doi study sept 2025"] explicitly emphasizes a validated set of tasks and competencies for DOI dispatch work and intended use in selection, performance management, workforce planning, training, and development. citeturn6view1turn5view1

What it systematically misses (or treats as implicit) is equally consequential for the next 1–3 years: (a) technology-mediated work (geospatial, sensor fusion, model interpretation, data governance, human–AI teaming); (b) year-round land and fuels management (beyond “incident qualification” representations); (c) community-facing risk reduction and engagement as a core professional domain rather than an “adjacent” function; and (d) ecological and Indigenous knowledge as legitimate professional competencies, despite growing policy-level recognition that Indigenous fire stewardship and traditional ecological knowledge are integral to modern fire management. citeturn7view12turn4search14turn4search7

Near-term disruption (1–3 years) is not primarily about elimination of front-line work; it is about *re-tooling and re-weighting* competencies. Routine information-processing tasks (triage, search, summarization, routine mapping, certain forms of reporting) are being automated or accelerated by AI and modern platforms, while higher-order judgment—risk framing, model critique, uncertainty management, and accountability—becomes more central. This arc is strongly suggested by (i) “next-generation” decision support modernization in WFDSS, (ii) expanding satellite/camera-based detection plus AI interpretation, and (iii) rapid experimentation with generative AI workflows for wildfire data and narrative products. citeturn6view4turn23view4turn6view7

## Current state of wildland fire competency infrastructure

The present competency landscape in wildland fire is best understood as a layered system with uneven depth across role families.

The foundational incident-qualification layer is defined by entity["organization","National Wildfire Coordinating Group","wildland fire standards us"] and its interagency standards and training ecosystem. The PMS 310-1 front matter makes explicit that the system establishes minimum standards for training, experience, physical fitness, and currency for national mobilization and that the format moved to a web-based system (with the NWCG site as the authoritative source). citeturn6view2turn5view6 A core mechanism is the Position Task Book: in the entity["book","NWCG Task Book for Single Resource Boss (PMS 311-13)","task book aug 2024"] example, PTBs are defined as listing competencies/behaviors/tasks; trainees must be *observed* completing tasks; and incident/event coding requires evaluation in the appropriate context (e.g., “W” tasks must be evaluated on a wildfire). citeturn20view0turn19view0 This is a strong, well-specified competence-validation mechanism compared with many other environmental management domains.

A second, interagency leadership/management layer governs key fire program positions via IFPM. IFPM’s own introduction states that the standard defined 14 key fire management positions and groups them into three categories: first-line supervisors (implement fire projects), fire project specialists (plan projects), and fire program managers (manage the overall program). citeturn6view3 In addition, the entity["organization","U.S. Forest Service","usda forest agency"] describes IFPM as a collaborative effort with the Department of the Interior wildland bureaus to establish minimum qualification standards for key Fire and Aviation Management positions, and notes the existence of a Forest Service addendum/crosswalk. citeturn7view10

A third layer, often overlooked in “competency” discussions, is job classification and career structuring. entity["organization","U.S. Office of Personnel Management","us federal hr agency"] defines the Wildland Fire Management 0456 series as covering positions involving technical and specialized work protecting forest or range lands and resources from fire. citeturn5view0 DOI’s GS-0456 employee FAQs indicate the series was created in 2022 as directed by the Bipartisan Infrastructure Law and intended to define wildland firefighters’ unique duties and support clearer career advancement paths, in consultation with Interior, Agriculture, and OPM. citeturn16view2

State all-hazards incident qualification systems (particularly in California) add another layer, often explicitly anchored in NWCG while expanding beyond wildland fire. The entity["book","CICCS Qualification Guide","april 2025 guide california"] describes a history in which entity["organization","California Incident Command Certification System","incident qualification system california"] was developed using NWCG 310-1 as a model/base document and is intended to capture both NWCG’s wildland fire direction and national all-hazard requirements, while being administered locally/regionally/statewide and acknowledging differences among local, state, and federal missions. citeturn16view0turn16view1

For dispatch, the competency infrastructure is notably more “validated” than for many other fire support roles, due to DOI’s recent occupational analysis work. The DOI dispatch competency model describes a methodology that includes focus groups, observation, a job-analysis survey, and SME review, culminating in a common set of dispatch tasks and competencies for department-wide human capital uses. citeturn6view1

Finally, specialized professional certifications and officer-development frameworks fill gaps for scientific and broader fire-service leadership competencies. entity["organization","Association for Fire Ecology","professional society fire ecology"] positions its professional certification program as promoting ecologically based science and management in wildland fire and fuels and specifies applicant criteria via an applicant guidebook. entity["book","Wildland Fire Professional Certification Guidebook","afe guidebook aug 2024"] provides explicit educational and competence documentation structures (including NWCG course crosswalk concepts as evidence of continuing education). citeturn5view3turn6view8 For leadership development, entity["organization","International Association of Fire Chiefs","fire chiefs association"] provides a competency-oriented officer-development handbook emphasizing planned, progressive professional development across education, training, experience, and self-development. citeturn6view9turn5view8 The entity["organization","U.S. Fire Administration","fema fire agency"] National Professional Development Matrix is explicitly described as a planning tool that crosswalks officer competencies with training, education, and experience and is not mandated—an example of a soft but scalable competency scaffold. citeturn7view5

**Structural blind spot (inference, grounded in system design):** Because the deepest qualification mechanisms (PMS 310-1 and PTBs) are designed to support mobilization and incident-position performance, the resulting competency “center of gravity” is incident response and ICS-position execution. This is explicit in the mobilization purpose of PMS 310-1 and in task-book evaluation rules tied to incident kind. citeturn6view2turn20view0 The consequence is that year-round fuels planning, community risk reduction, and integrative ecological knowledge can be under-specified as competencies even when they are central to many wildland fire jobs and program outcomes (as shown in federal job postings emphasizing fuels program evaluation, WUI risk assessment, and cross-disciplinary integration). citeturn11view1turn11view2

## Role-by-role competency and disruption analysis

### Operational field staff

**Current competency landscape (well documented).** The operational field competency model is the most formalized and performance-validated in the wildland fire workforce. Entry and progression rely on standardized basic training and recurrent qualification maintenance, typically grounded in NWCG standards and local implementation systems (e.g., annual refreshers and task-book completion). The entity["organization","Bureau of Indian Affairs","doi bureau tribal affairs"] describes basic firefighter training as a defined set of courses (including ICS-100, L-180, S-130, S-190) and frames qualification as requiring a mix of classroom training, field exercise, and (for many roles) fitness testing, with annual renewal expectations. citeturn9view9

Operational competence is not only technical (tool use, firing operations, holding, mop-up), but also explicitly incorporates human performance and decision-making under uncertainty. entity["book","Human Factors in the Wildland Fire Service (L-180)","nwcg course student workbook"] states that firefighters have responsibilities to reduce errors, improve performance, maintain situation awareness, communicate effectively, and minimize risk; it includes an explicit decision-cycle framing that emphasizes perception, recognition, option selection, and action under time pressure. citeturn22view0turn22view1

Federal job announcements provide a “revealed-preference” signal about what employers expect from line-qualified personnel in practice. A fuels squad boss posting emphasizes directing crews in fireline construction/burnout/mop-up, applying understanding of fire behavior, topography, weather, fuels, and managing fuels projects (prescribed fire, mechanical/biological treatments, fuel-bed inventory). citeturn11view2 A general wildland firefighter posting (GW-03 to GW-05) similarly emphasizes preparation/ignition/monitoring/holding/mop-up across wildland and prescribed fire, and explicitly calls out gathering and considering information on weather, fuels, and fire behavior. citeturn11view3

**Gaps between credentialing and work (partly documented, partly inferred).** Credentialing is strongest for incident-position performance; it is thinner where the work is year-round land management, community engagement, and cross-boundary coordination. The fuels-management job posting highlights program evaluation, integration with land/resource management plans, air quality coordination, and WUI risk assessment—competencies that often sit outside “incident role” task books even though they are core for prescribed fire practitioners and fuels leadership. citeturn11view1 The gap is therefore not “no standards exist,” but rather that validated, performance-based evaluation is more mature for *incident execution* than for *landscape-scale fuels and community risk work* (inference anchored in the different documentation density and evaluation mechanisms). citeturn20view0turn11view1

**Near-term disruption analysis (1–3 years).** For field operations, the most immediate disruption is *augmentation* rather than elimination.

Remote sensing and AI-enabled detection are accelerating the speed and precision of early incident awareness. NASA’s fire systems provide near real-time active fire/hotspot data and related products to identify location/extent/intensity, and are designed explicitly to support decision-makers and the broader fire management community. citeturn23view0turn7view2 Meanwhile, AI-enabled camera networks and AI-enhanced satellite detection are increasingly operationalized; AP reporting describes large-scale camera-based AI testing for smoke detection and broader AI use for early detection and planning. citeturn23view4 The practical effect for field staff is that “initial size-up” and “situational awareness” increasingly begins with multi-source digital intelligence streams (satellite, cameras, modeled perimeters), shifting competence from *finding the fire* to *validating, interpreting, and acting on* higher-volume, higher-frequency information.

Decision-support tooling is also becoming more map- and model-centric. WFDSS guidance emphasizes short-term and near-term fire behavior products used to anticipate where the fire “wants to travel” and to inform strategy. citeturn6view6turn5view11 This tends to *elevate* field leadership competencies in interpreting modeled outputs, questioning assumptions (e.g., calibration to observed behavior), and communicating model-informed risk to crews and supervisors (inference grounded in the explicit “ask the analyst…” guidance). citeturn6view6

Digital twin and immersive simulation appear most likely as training accelerants in the near term rather than operational replacements. A 2024 review notes growing attention to digital twin systems for wildland fire management research and practice. citeturn3search7 Industry/government partnership examples like VR SAFE describe immersive 360-degree wildland fire environments intended to improve understanding of fire behavior, vegetation, and terrain for safer training. citeturn3search23

**Workforce readiness (inference, supported by training signals).** Baseline readiness for human factors, communication, and risk framing is relatively strong because these are explicitly embedded in widely used curricula (e.g., L-180). citeturn22view0turn9view9 Readiness is weaker (and variable by unit) in geospatial literacy, sensor/data fusion, and “model critique” skills—competencies increasingly required to operate safely in information-dense environments (inference based on the disparity between detailed human-factors curricula and the more uneven formalization of digital skills in entry-level qualification scaffolds). citeturn22view0turn20view0

**Ecological and traditional knowledge dimensions.** Field operations have always depended on ecological knowledge (fuels, fire behavior, terrain) and increasingly rely on prescribed fire and fuels treatment competence, as reflected in job postings. citeturn11view2turn11view3 However, Indigenous fire stewardship and traditional ecological knowledge (TEK) are only weakly formalized in the dominant credentialing system, despite system-level recognition that Indigenous practices and knowledge are important to modern fire management. DOI explicitly states that it recognizes the importance of Indigenous fire stewardship and works with tribes to incorporate traditional burning practices and ecological knowledge into modern fire management. citeturn7view12 USFS scholarship defines Indigenous fire stewardship as intergenerational fire-related knowledge systems and practices used to adapt and respond to environmental conditions and promote desired landscapes and resources. citeturn4search14 The NPS describes “cultural burning” as intentional lighting of smaller, controlled fires to provide desired cultural services and ecological outcomes. citeturn4search7

The near-term risk is not that TEK disappears from practice, but that technology modernization shifts “legibility” toward sensor outputs and model dashboards, while place-based indicators and culturally grounded objectives remain under-credentialed and therefore undervalued in workforce investment decisions (inference anchored in the mismatch between decently resourced technical systems and thinner formal recognition of TEK as a professional competency). citeturn23view0turn7view12turn6view2

**Crosswalk indicators.** Operational field competencies strongly align with workforce and organizational capacity (safe execution, teamwork), land and resource management (fuels/prescribed fire execution), and data and technology integration (increasingly via modeled products and geospatial awareness), with partial overlap into community engagement in WUI contexts. citeturn11view2turn6view6turn23view4

### Fire management officers and administrators

**Current competency landscape (well documented for “who is qualified,” thinner for “how well”).** Management roles sit at the intersection of qualification standards (IFPM), classification structures (GS-0456), and decision documentation systems.

IFPM defines key roles across supervision, project planning, and program management, framing qualification minima for those positions. citeturn6view3turn7view10 At the incident/policy interface, WFDSS is described as an interagency, web-based application that helps agency administrators and fire managers make risk-informed decisions for all types of wildland fires and integrates multiple incident-management applications into a single risk-informed system tied to planning frameworks. citeturn6view5turn5view10

Federal job postings indicate that “administration” in modern wildland fire includes analytics, modeling, governance, and cross-sector negotiation. A “Wildland Firefighting Manager (NWCG, Training and Standards, and Workforce Development)” posting explicitly lists workforce planning, budget formulation, quality improvement methods, “analytics and reporting tools,” and negotiations with industry (technology companies) and Tribal governments to develop agreements supporting NWCG training/standards and workforce development. citeturn15view0 This illustrates a marked competence expansion beyond classic incident command toward technology-enabled program governance.

**Key gaps between credentialing and practice (largely inferred, supported by labor-market signals).** Management qualification standards (IFPM) define minima for who can fill roles, but management competence increasingly requires technology governance: evaluating models, overseeing digital platforms, and ensuring interoperability while maintaining accountability. The job posting language about analytics, internal controls, and negotiating with technology firms indicates that these competencies are already entering formal duty expectations, even if they are not uniformly embedded in evaluation structures. citeturn15view0turn6view3

**Near-term disruption analysis (1–3 years).** The largest near-term shift is that administrative competence becomes more explicitly *risk-analytic and system-governance oriented*.

WFDSS modernization is a direct driver. The WFDSS NextGen FAQ frames WFDSS NextGen as an interagency, web-based system designed for collaborative, risk-informed decisions across fires of any complexity, emphasizing scalable, map-focused UX and near-real-time authoritative data to support rapid decision-making. citeturn6view4 It also indicates that FSPro is prioritized because it is only available within WFDSS due to intense data requirements, while other models (e.g., FlamMap, FARSITE, MTT) have alternatives outside the system. citeturn6view4turn5view4 This concentrates certain analytic capabilities in a single platform and makes “platform literacy + model literacy” a management-critical competency (inference based on the uniqueness of FSPro availability and WFDSS’s compliance/documentation role). citeturn6view4turn6view5

Fuels-planning decision support is also becoming more standardized and web-delivered. IFTDSS is described by USFS R&D as a web-based application designed to make fuel treatment planning/analysis more efficient and effective by providing access to data and models through one interface and being available beyond agency boundaries. citeturn7view4 This increases the importance of administrators understanding (and resourcing) data pipelines, model assumptions, and monitoring frameworks (e.g., effectiveness monitoring), and it tightens the coupling between policy objectives (“treat fuels”) and technical tools used to justify/prioritize work. citeturn7view4turn2search16

Administrative disruption also comes from dispatch/resource-management modernization: entity["book","IROC Talking Points","resource ordering modernization 2019"] describes ROSS as obsolete and being replaced by IROC, a modern web-based platform intended to support ordering, tracking, and managing resources, with integrations across multiple systems and cloud hosting expectations. citeturn16view6 This creates near-term needs for workflow redesign, training, governance, and data quality assurance in resource ordering and status tracking—functions that often sit under fire management administration.

Finally, generative AI is emerging as a management skill domain: an entity["organization","Open Geospatial Consortium","geospatial standards body"] engineering report on generative AI in wildland fire management highlights NLP/LLM utility for extracting actionable information from unstructured sources such as incident logs, weather reports, and community feedback. citeturn6view7turn5view9 If adopted, this would *transform* competencies in situational reporting, documentation, and public-facing narrative synthesis: less manual extraction, more verification, framing, and governance.

**Workforce readiness (inference, supported by tool/procurement signals).** Many administrators have long-standing experience with ICS positions and decision documentation; readiness is lower for AI governance, data ethics, and human–AI workflow design, because those have not historically been core qualification content. Yet the presence of job duties involving “analytics,” modeling, and negotiations with technology companies suggests that agencies are already building capacity in this direction. citeturn15view0turn6view4

**Ecological and traditional knowledge dimensions.** Administrators occupy a pivotal gatekeeping role for whether ecological knowledge and TEK are treated as “real” competencies (resource allocation, training requirements, acceptable decision rationales). DOI’s statement on incorporating Indigenous fire stewardship and ecological knowledge implies an expectation of active partnership and integration. citeturn7view12 The workforce risk is that modernization leads to “dashboard governance” in which what is measured (and thus defended in reviews) dominates what is valued, potentially sidelining cultural objectives and place-based indicators unless explicitly formalized as competencies and decision criteria (inference supported by the centrality of WFDSS/IFTDSS decision documentation and the relative thinness of TEK representation in qualification artifacts). citeturn6view5turn7view4turn4search14

**Crosswalk indicators.** Administrator competencies map strongly to workforce and organizational capacity (planning, budgeting, supervision), regulatory and compliance frameworks (documentation, smoke/air-quality coordination, program controls), community and stakeholder engagement (Tribal, interagency, intergovernmental negotiation), and data and technology integration (WFDSS/IFTDSS/IROC governance). citeturn15view0turn11view1turn16view6

### Researchers and scientists

**Current competency landscape (thin formalization, strong disciplinary standards).** Compared to incident operational roles, researchers and scientists operate within disciplinary education and research norms rather than a single unified competency framework. However, two partial scaffolds exist.

First, the AFE professional certification program explicitly defines criteria for ecologically based wildland fire and fuels professional practice and provides pathways/requirements through its guidebook. citeturn5view3turn6view8 This is a notable attempt at professionalization around ecological competence.

Second, science is partially embedded into the incident qualification ecosystem via technical specialist roles. The DOI Incident Position Qualification Guide indicates that a set of planning-section positions—Fire Behavior Analyst (FBAN), Fire Effects Monitor (FEMO), Infrared Interpreter (IRIN), and Long Term Fire Analyst (LTAN)—have requirements found in NWCG qualification standards. citeturn18view1turn17view0 This provides a bridge between science and operations, but it focuses primarily on incident support roles rather than the broader research workforce.

Federal job postings also show “boundary” roles that translate science into operational products. A meteorologist (fire) posting describes duties including developing new fire weather products, serving as a liaison for wildland fire agencies, and coordinating with interagency units and the National Weather Service; minimum education includes remote sensing of the atmosphere and computer science among other physical science requirements. citeturn15view1 This is a concrete example of how scientific roles in fire contexts require both domain science and data/technology literacy.

**Gaps between formal frameworks and actual work (inference, with supporting signals).** In practice, fire science roles increasingly require data engineering, reproducible workflows, and high-stakes communication to decision-makers and communities—skills not consistently formalized across institutions as “competencies” in the way NWCG does for incident roles. The reliance on ad hoc job requirements (as seen in the meteorologist posting) suggests variable competency expectations depending on unit/mission. citeturn15view1turn18view1

**Near-term disruption analysis (1–3 years).** Near-term disruption for fire researchers and scientists is largely about new analytic methods and faster translation pathways.

AI is increasingly studied and applied for wildfire detection, occurrence forecasting, spread modeling, decision-making, and human-behavior modeling. A 2025 review notes that AI offers opportunities across forecasting, spread modeling, and decision-making but also emphasizes that operational use remains limited despite sophisticated research—suggesting that near-term competency needs include “research-to-operations translation” and evaluation of model validity in real settings. citeturn3search9 Recent work on evacuation modeling illustrates the expansion of fire-related research into coupled human–environment systems with AI-enabled simulation and prediction. citeturn3search1

Remote sensing is also intensifying as a core scientific and operational data stream. NASA Earthdata describes VIIRS 375m fire detections as complementing MODIS and providing improved response over smaller fires and improved mapping of large fire perimeters, including improved nighttime performance. citeturn23view2 These improvements increase demand for competency in sensor characteristics, uncertainty, and assimilation into models and decision products.

Digital twin approaches, while not yet uniformly operationalized, are becoming a structured research and experimentation domain; a 2024 review explicitly surveys digital twin systems for wildland fire management. citeturn3search7 Over a 1–3 year horizon, the near-term competency implication is less “build a full digital twin” and more “participate in pilot design, validation, and responsible deployment,” including ethics and interpretability.

**Workforce readiness (mixed; inference grounded in evidence on operational limitations).** Scientific staff with strong quantitative backgrounds may be individually ready to adopt AI methods, but organizational readiness is constrained by operational adoption barriers and the need for validated, trustworthy, decision-grade products—consistent with the review’s observation that operational AI use remains limited. citeturn3search9 This makes competencies in evaluation, uncertainty communication, and stakeholder relevance particularly near-term-critical.

**Ecological and traditional knowledge dimensions.** Fire science increasingly recognizes Indigenous fire stewardship as a domain of legitimate expertise and evidence. USFS literature defines Indigenous fire stewardship as intergenerational knowledge and practices that adapt to local conditions and promote desired landscapes and resources. citeturn4search14 NPS public education similarly frames cultural burning as intentional, controlled burning to achieve desired cultural services and ecological outcomes. citeturn4search7

However, the formal professional competency structures that dominate operational qualification systems rarely encode TEK as a named, evaluated competence. The risk is that TEK becomes “consulted” rather than “credentialed,” limiting sustained workforce investment and professional recognition (inference anchored in the contrast between formal PTB evaluation mechanisms and the largely narrative/policy-level treatment of Indigenous knowledge). citeturn20view0turn7view12turn4search14

**Crosswalk indicators.** Research/science competencies map strongly to ecological stewardship, data and technology integration, and (for applied scientists) community and stakeholder engagement, especially where science interfaces with fire-affected communities and Tribal governments. citeturn15view1turn4search14turn7view12

### Technical and data roles

**Current competency landscape (fragmented; partially documented).** This role family spans two partially overlapping worlds:

*Incident technical specialist roles* have some explicit codification. In the DOI Incident Position Qualification Guide, a Cartographer technical specialist role is described as providing advanced geospatial analysis using sources including UAS, satellite assets, aerial imagery, LiDAR, and GPS data, producing products for situational awareness; even here, required training is primarily ICS/NIMS oriented, and “satisfactory performance as an agency cartographer” is treated as required experience rather than a detailed competency rubric. citeturn18view3

*Enterprise and operational IT/data systems roles* are often governed less by NWCG qualification paths and more by platform adoption, agency IT practices, and occupational series norms. DOI’s wildland fire IT page describes WildCAD as a computer-aided dispatch system used by wildland fire agencies across the country to streamline ordering firefighters/equipment, and it characterizes WFDSS as a tool helping managers make and record key decisions using critical data, modeling tools, and risk analysis. citeturn7view0

Dispatch job postings highlight the hands-on technical workload: a Forestry Technician (Fire Dispatch) posting lists duties including maintaining resource status/tracking systems, inputting data into multiple fire-related computer applications, monitoring fire radio communications, and maintaining conversation logs. citeturn11view0 This is an important “ground truth” signal: technical competency is not abstract; it is daily multi-system operations.

**Gaps between frameworks and practice (well supported by evidence).** For many technical/data roles (data science, systems engineering, AI/ML practice), there is no fire-specific, widely adopted competency framework analogous to PTBs. Instead, competency expectations are spread across: tool-specific training, occupational qualification standards, and unit demand. The fragmentation is visible in how (a) incident technical roles specify minimal ICS training plus experience, while (b) dispatch roles emphasize multi-application data entry and log maintenance, and (c) higher-level workforce/standards roles explicitly require negotiating with technology companies and using analytics tools. citeturn18view3turn11view0turn15view0

**Near-term disruption analysis (1–3 years).** This category is among the most exposed to near-term technology disruption because its core outputs are information products.

Remote sensing pipelines are accelerating; NASA FIRMS provides near real-time satellite-based active fire data through alerts, maps, and web services. citeturn7view2turn23view0 New satellite initiatives explicitly designed for wildfire detection (e.g., FireSat) signal a likely increase in high-frequency thermal imagery requiring AI-assisted interpretation and robust data engineering capabilities. citeturn23view5 Camera-based AI systems also expand the volume of real-time visual streams requiring triage, verification, and integration with dispatch and decision systems. citeturn23view4

Resource ordering and dispatch technology modernization (IROC, WildCAD) is shifting “competence” from memorizing procedures within legacy systems to designing and operating interoperable workflows, assuring data quality, and troubleshooting in high-tempo environments. IROC talking points emphasize cloud hosting, integrations, and complex workflows, implying new competencies in system thinking and resilience engineering. citeturn16view6turn7view0

Generative AI is likely to be most disruptive in text-heavy workflows: log triage, narrative situation reporting, after-action synthesis, and extraction of actionable items from unstructured text. The OGC engineering report explicitly frames NLP for extracting actionable information from incident logs, weather reports, and community feedback, suggesting near-term creation of “AI-enabled information officer / analyst” competencies even when job titles do not change. citeturn6view7turn5view9

**Workforce readiness (inference, grounded in current job design).** Readiness is uneven: many roles are staffed by operational personnel with strong fire context but limited time or incentives for deep software/data specialization, while “pure” data roles may lack fire-context literacy. The repeated job-duty emphasis on multi-application data entry and maintaining logs suggests that time and workload constraints are already severe; introducing new AI systems without redesigning workflows risks increasing cognitive load and error potential rather than reducing it (inference grounded in dispatch duty descriptions and the human-factors focus of standard training). citeturn11view0turn22view0

**Ecological and traditional knowledge dimensions.** Technical modernization can unintentionally encode a “thin ecology” into dashboards (fuel models, indices, remote detections) unless systems are designed to incorporate local ecological indicators and culturally grounded objectives. The disconnect between place-based TEK and formal technical specialist qualification structures is a known risk, particularly because TEK is often not represented in the data schemas that drive decision support (inference supported by the contrast between TEK policy recognition and the primarily technical data sources described in mapped systems). citeturn7view12turn18view3turn23view0

## Highest-leverage intervention points

The intervention points below are framed to matter on a 1–3 year horizon and to respect the difference between “training completed” and “competence demonstrated.”

First, modernize qualification content to explicitly include digital decision-making competence where the work is already tool-mediated. WFDSS is central to risk-informed decisions and concentrates certain models (e.g., FSPro) inside the platform; IFTDSS similarly centralizes fuels planning/analysis. citeturn6view5turn6view4turn7view4 The leverage point is not merely adding courses, but embedding *competence demonstrations* (scenario-based model critique, uncertainty communication, data provenance checks) into evaluation pathways analogous to PTBs.

Second, build a dedicated “fire information reliability” competency track spanning dispatch, resource ordering, and situation reporting. Dispatch work already requires maintaining resource status systems, inputting data into multiple applications, logging communications, and generating briefings. citeturn11view0turn6view1 Combined with modernization efforts (IROC replacing legacy systems; WildCAD streamlining ordering), this is the clearest near-term locus for productivity gains *and* safety risk if adoption is unmanaged. citeturn16view6turn7view0 A high-leverage strategy is to professionalize this track with validated competencies in workflow design, data quality assurance, and escalation protocols for AI-generated alerts.

Third, institutionalize TEK and Indigenous fire stewardship as professional competencies rather than advisory inputs. DOI’s own statements recognize Indigenous fire stewardship and traditional burning/ecological knowledge as important to modern fire management. citeturn7view12 USFS research defines Indigenous fire stewardship as intergenerational, place-based knowledge and practice shaping fire regimes and landscape outcomes. citeturn4search14 The leverage point is to co-design credentialed competencies (with Tribes) for roles that touch prescribed fire, fuels planning, and community engagement, and to ensure these competencies appear in workforce planning, position descriptions, and—where appropriate—performance-based evaluation mechanisms.

Fourth, invest in human factors for technology-rich operations, explicitly linking digital tools to error reduction and situation awareness. L-180 already frames error reduction, situation awareness, communication, and risk minimization as responsibilities. citeturn22view0 The near-term gap is how these principles apply when information is AI-mediated: managing false positives, avoiding automation bias, and maintaining shared mental models when different staff see different dashboards. The leverage point is integrating “human–AI team” modules into refresher and leadership development pathways rather than treating AI as purely technical.

Fifth, create cross-functional “standards-to-technology” governance capacity as a workforce role family. The Wildland Firefighting Manager posting indicates that agencies are already expecting leadership that can manage NWCG training/standards and workforce development while coordinating analytics/reporting tools and negotiating with technology firms and Tribal governments. citeturn15view0 This suggests a philanthropic and policy opportunity: fund and scale career pathways, fellowships, or rotational programs that produce professionals fluent in operations, standards, procurement, and data governance—reducing the lag between technology adoption and competency infrastructure updates.

Sixth, use labor-market signals as a continuous competency update mechanism. Federal job postings already embed practical competence requirements (e.g., fuels program evaluation, air quality coordination, WUI risk assessment, “maintains awareness of technological developments”). citeturn11view1turn11view2 A near-term, high-leverage move is for agencies and philanthropic partners to support routine “competency sensing” (structured review of postings, after-action lessons, tool adoption) and to feed those signals into NWCG/IFPM revisions, course updates, and evaluation artifacts—without conflating course attendance with competence.

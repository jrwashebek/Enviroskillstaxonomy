# Profession Family Definitions and Scoping
## Environmental Management Skills Taxonomy — Task 5 Output
**Stage 2: Framework Definition**
**Status: Ratified — February 2026**
**Dependencies for downstream use:** Tasks 9 (Profession Family Tagging)

---

## Purpose and Status

This document provides formal definitions and boundary criteria for all 15 profession families used to tag the taxonomy's 215 competencies. These definitions are ratified and stable. Do not modify without explicit documentation of the change and downstream review of any tagging already completed.

---

## The Core / Supporting Distinction

The tagging framework uses two tiers:

**Core:** A credentialed practitioner in this profession family would regard not having this competency as a meaningful gap in their professional preparation.

**Supporting:** Practitioners in this family regularly apply this competency in their work, but the competency is not distinctive to their professional identity.

**Boundary test for ambiguous cases:** When uncertain between Core and Supporting, ask whether you are reasoning from the *content* of the competency or from *who tends to occupy the roles* associated with it. The former is correct; the latter produces scope creep.

**GitBook display:** Core tags shown by default; Supporting tags expandable.

---

## Special Tagging Guidance: Conservation Science / Wildlife & Fisheries Boundary

A competency may be tagged Core for **both** Conservation Science & Ecology and Wildlife & Fisheries Management when it involves generating population knowledge AND informing management decisions. The knowledge-versus-action distinction is maintained but not collapsed — both tags are available when both functions are genuinely present in the competency description.

## Special Tagging Guidance: Quantitative Ecologist

The Quantitative Ecologist role is listed under both Environmental Data Science & Geospatial (Family 7) and Conservation Science & Ecology (Family 5). Assignment is resolved case-by-case using the product-vs.-tool test: is the technical/data infrastructure the professional's primary output, or is it the tool serving a scientific question?

## Family 1: Forestry & Forest Management

**Definition.** Professionals who manage forested landscapes for timber production, wildlife habitat, watershed function, carbon sequestration, and recreational values under multi-use or single-use mandates. The core professional identity is silvicultural — applying knowledge of forest ecology, stand dynamics, and tree physiology to design and implement treatments (harvests, plantings, thinnings, prescribed fire) that shape stand structure and composition over time, whether on public or private land, in urban or rural contexts.

**Boundary criteria.** Tag Core if the competency is specifically concerned with forested systems, silvicultural decision-making, timber measurement and valuation, tree physiology, or forest health assessment. Tag Supporting if it is a general ecological, field operations, data, or project management competency that foresters regularly deploy but that is not distinctive to forested contexts. Key test: does this competency require knowledge of trees specifically, or could it apply equally to grasslands, wetlands, or rangelands? If the latter, Supporting at most.

**Representative roles.**
- *Domain 1:* Forester/Research Forester, Silviculturist, Forest Ecologist, Forest Health Specialist, Urban Forester, Forest Mensurationist, Timber Cruiser, Forest Biometrician
- *Domain 4:* Forestry Technician/Forestry Aid, Chainsaw Operator/Sawyer (forest operations), UAS Operator (forest inventory)

**Domain span.** Primary: Domain 1 (forest ecology, silviculture, forest health science). Secondary: Domain 4 (field implementation, land treatment, prescribed fire), Domain 3 (timber program management), Domain 6 (forest practice regulation, NEPA for timber sales, carbon markets for forests).

**Overlap notes.** Overlaps with *Ecological Restoration* for forest restoration — distinction is whether the primary objective is restorative or productive/multi-use. Overlaps with *Fire Management* for prescribed fire in forest settings — Fire Management identity is organized around fire; Forestry uses fire as a silvicultural tool. Overlaps with *Conservation Science* for forest carbon and forest biodiversity research.

## Family 2: Wildlife & Fisheries Management

**Definition.** Professionals focused on managing wild animal populations and the habitats that support them across terrestrial, freshwater, and marine systems. The core identity is population-level thinking — applying wildlife ecology, population dynamics, and habitat science to inform harvest regulations, species recovery programs, conservation strategies, and ecosystem management decisions.

**Boundary criteria.** Tag Core if the competency requires knowledge of wildlife or fish biology, population assessment methods (mark-recapture, distance sampling, acoustic monitoring), habitat management with a primary goal of maintaining or recovering animal populations, or species-specific regulatory compliance (ESA, Migratory Bird Treaty Act, Marine Mammal Protection Act). Tag Supporting if it is a general field, data, or ecological competency applicable equally to plant-focused or physical-system management. Key test: does the competency change meaningfully when applied to animals versus other ecological targets?

**Representative roles.**
- *Domain 1:* Wildlife Biologist, Fisheries Biologist, Ornithologist/Mammalogist/Herpetologist, Population Modeler, Wildlife Ecologist, Aquatic Biologist, Wildlife Disease Specialist, Habitat Biologist
- *Domain 4:* Wildlife Monitoring Technician, Stream Survey Technician, Biological Technician (wildlife)
- *Domain 6:* ESA Section 7 compliance roles (wildlife focus)

**Domain span.** Primary: Domain 1 (wildlife ecology, population science, aquatic ecology). Secondary: Domain 4 (field surveys, trapping, telemetry), Domain 2 (habitat modeling, species distribution modeling, acoustic data processing), Domain 6 (ESA compliance, harvest regulations, co-management agreements).

**Overlap notes.** Overlaps with *Conservation Science & Ecology* — distinction is management authority: Wildlife & Fisheries professionals make management decisions about populations; Conservation Science professionals generate knowledge about those systems. See special tagging guidance above for co-Core cases. Overlaps with *Ecological Restoration* for habitat restoration targeting wildlife goals. Overlaps with *Water Resources* for aquatic species in freshwater systems.

## Family 3: Ecological Restoration

**Definition.** Professionals who plan and implement interventions to reestablish the structure, function, and self-sustaining capacity of degraded or destroyed ecosystems. The core identity is trajectory-based — using knowledge of reference conditions, disturbance dynamics, and ecological processes to design treatments that initiate and support recovery pathways across terrestrial, freshwater, and coastal systems. Ecological restoration is distinguished from ongoing management by its explicit goal of returning a system to a prior or alternative reference state.

**Boundary criteria.** Tag Core if the competency is specifically about restoring degraded systems to defined ecological targets: site assessment for restoration suitability, reference condition identification, treatment design (grading, seeding, planting, hydrology reconnection), propagation and installation, adaptive management of recovery trajectories, or restoration monitoring against reference benchmarks. Key test: does the competency require knowledge of a trajectory toward a target state, or is it a general ecological competency applicable to systems not in recovery?

**Representative roles.**
- *Domain 1:* Restoration Ecologist, Ecosystem Services Specialist (restoration context)
- *Domain 4:* Restoration Technician/Specialist, Habitat Restoration Crew Leader, Revegetation Specialist, Invasive Species Technician/Crew, Conservation Corps Member/Leader
- *Domain 3:* Restoration Project Manager

**Domain span.** Primary: Domain 1 (ecosystem science, soils, hydrology as restoration substrates), Domain 4 (field implementation, land treatment techniques). Secondary: Domain 3 (restoration project management), Domain 2 (remote sensing for tracking restoration trajectories), Domain 6 (wetland restoration permitting under Section 404).

**Overlap notes.** Overlaps with *Forestry* for forest restoration (shared silvicultural tools, distinct professional objectives). Overlaps with *Water Resources* for riparian and wetland restoration. Overlaps with *Sustainable Agriculture* for rangeland and agricultural land restoration where restoration and production goals converge. Overlaps with *Wildlife & Fisheries* where habitat restoration targets are defined by species recovery objectives.

## Family 4: Water Resources & Watershed Science

**Definition.** Professionals who assess, manage, and protect freshwater systems — including rivers, streams, wetlands, groundwater, and the watersheds that generate and regulate water supply, quality, and flow. The core identity integrates hydrology, water quality science, and watershed management to inform water allocation decisions, treatment system design, watershed protection strategies, and restoration actions.

**Boundary criteria.** Tag Core if the competency is specifically concerned with hydrological processes, water quantity and quality assessment, aquifer dynamics, wetland science and delineation, watershed-scale planning, or stormwater management. Key test: does the competency lose meaning if water is not the primary resource being managed?

**Representative roles.**
- *Domain 1:* Hydrologist/Watershed Scientist, Water Quality Specialist, Groundwater Specialist, Erosion Control Specialist, Wetland Scientist, Aquatic Ecologist, Limnologist
- *Domain 4:* Water Quality Technician, Stream Survey Technician, Irrigation Technician/Water Manager
- *Domain 6:* Natural Resource Economist (water markets), Environmental Permitting Specialist (Clean Water Act)

**Domain span.** Primary: Domain 1 (hydrology, soil science, aquatic ecology). Secondary: Domain 4 (field sampling, water quality monitoring, irrigation management), Domain 6 (water law, Section 404/401 permitting, water markets), Domain 2 (hydrological modeling, LiDAR, remote sensing for watershed condition).

**Overlap notes.** Overlaps with *Ecological Restoration* for riparian and wetland restoration. Overlaps with *Wildlife & Fisheries* for aquatic species where hydrological conditions are the management lever. Overlaps with *Environmental Policy* for Clean Water Act compliance and water rights adjudication. Overlaps with *Sustainable Agriculture* for irrigation management and agricultural water quality.

## Family 5: Conservation Science & Ecology

**Definition.** Professionals who generate and apply scientific knowledge to understand biodiversity patterns, ecological processes, and conservation threats at population, community, and landscape scales. The core identity is scientific inquiry in service of conservation outcomes — designing and conducting research, synthesizing evidence including traditional ecological knowledge, and translating scientific findings into conservation strategies, prioritization frameworks, and management recommendations.

**Boundary criteria.** Tag Core if the competency involves generating, synthesizing, or applying ecological or conservation science to identify threats, evaluate interventions, set conservation priorities, or assess ecosystem function. Key distinction from adjacent management families: Conservation Science professionals' primary output is *knowledge and analysis*; management families' primary output is *management decisions and actions*. When uncertain: is this competency more about producing evidence or acting on it? Evidence production is Conservation Science territory. See special tagging guidance for co-Core cases with Wildlife & Fisheries.

**Representative roles.**
- *Domain 1:* Ecologist/Research Ecologist, Conservation Biologist, Biodiversity Scientist, Ecosystem Services Specialist, Landscape Ecologist, Community Ecologist, Population Ecologist, Conservation Geneticist, Molecular Ecologist, eDNA Specialist, Genomics Researcher
- *Domain 2:* Quantitative Ecologist (see product-vs.-tool test), Computational Ecologist, Environmental Modeler (ecology focus)

**Domain span.** Primary: Domain 1 (heavily — ecosystem science, population biology, genetics). Secondary: Domain 2 (species distribution modeling, spatial ecology, quantitative analysis), Domain 6 (ESA regulatory science, conservation policy analysis), Domain 3 (science-to-management translation, research program management).

**Overlap notes.** Overlaps substantially with *Wildlife & Fisheries Management*, *Climate Science & Adaptation*, and *Environmental Data Science*. The distinguishing feature: professional identity organized around producing rigorous scientific knowledge, not operating technical systems or making management decisions.

## Family 6: Climate Science & Adaptation

**Definition.** Professionals who analyze climate system dynamics, assess climate-related risks and vulnerabilities, and design adaptation strategies to help ecological, social, and institutional systems maintain function under changing conditions. The core identity spans physical climate science and attribution analysis through applied vulnerability assessment and adaptation planning, with increasing emphasis on operationalizing climate information for decision-making at organizational and landscape scales.

**Boundary criteria.** Tag Core if the competency specifically addresses climate system dynamics (projections, attribution, scenarios, downscaling), climate vulnerability and risk assessment, climate refugia science, novel ecosystem management under climate change, climate-informed management planning, or organizational climate risk integration. Key test: does the competency require understanding of climate as a *driving variable*, or is climate just one of many stressors in an otherwise general competency?

**Representative roles.**
- *Domain 1:* Climate Scientist/Climatologist, Climate Adaptation Specialist, Carbon Scientist, Climate Vulnerability Analyst, Phenology Researcher, Climate Change Ecologist, Climate Refugia Scientist, Novel Ecosystem Manager, Ecological Transformation Specialist
- *Domain 3:* Chief Sustainability Officer (climate strategy), ESG Reporting Specialist (climate disclosure)
- *Domain 6:* Green Bond Analyst, Carbon Markets Analyst

**Domain span.** Primary: Domain 1 (climate ecology, phenology, novel ecosystems, carbon science). Secondary: Domain 2 (climate modeling, spatial vulnerability analysis, remote sensing for climate indicators), Domain 6 (climate policy, carbon markets, climate disclosure regulation), Domain 3 (climate risk integration into organizational strategy).

**Overlap notes.** Blurs with *Conservation Science* for climate impacts on biodiversity. Blurs with *Corporate Sustainability & ESG* for climate disclosure and net-zero planning — distinction is whether expertise is climate science informing corporate strategy (Climate Science) or corporate governance frameworks incorporating climate as one material issue (Corporate Sustainability). Blurs with *Conservation Finance* for carbon markets — Carbon Science is Core for Climate Science; carbon market mechanics are Core for Conservation Finance.

## Family 7: Environmental Data Science & Geospatial

**Definition.** Professionals who design, build, and operate technical systems for collecting, processing, analyzing, and visualizing environmental data across spatial, temporal, and biological dimensions. The core identity is technical infrastructure for environmental decision-making — practitioners who construct and maintain the data pipelines, geospatial analyses, statistical models, and decision support systems that underpin evidence-based management across all other profession families.

**Boundary criteria.** Tag Core if the competency is specifically about technical data systems: geospatial analysis and cartography, database design and management, statistical modeling and inference, remote sensing processing and interpretation, machine learning for environmental applications, data quality management, or decision support system design. Key test: is the data/technical system the *product*, or the *tool*? Environmental Data Science professionals produce technical systems as their primary work.

**Representative roles.**
- *Domain 2:* GIS Analyst/Specialist, Geospatial Scientist, Remote Sensing Specialist, Cartographer, Spatial Analyst, GIS Developer, LiDAR Analyst, UAS Operator/Analyst, Environmental Data Scientist, Database Administrator, Machine Learning Engineer (Environmental), Bioinformatician, Quantitative Ecologist (see product-vs.-tool test), Environmental Modeler
- *Domain 4:* Field Technology Specialist, Environmental Sensor Technician

**Domain span.** Primary: Domain 2 (heavily — geospatial, data science, analytics, AI/ML, database management). Secondary: Domain 1 (quantitative ecology and modeling), Domain 4 (field data collection technology, sensor networks).

**Overlap notes.** Overlaps with *Conservation Science* for quantitative ecology and species distribution modeling — boundary is whether the professional's primary identity is the scientific question (Conservation Science) or the analytical infrastructure (Environmental Data Science). Distinguished not by subject matter but by where technical infrastructure sits in the professional's value proposition.

## Family 8: Environmental Policy & Regulation

**Definition.** Professionals who develop, interpret, implement, and enforce the legal and institutional frameworks governing environmental management. The core identity is navigating and shaping the regulatory context — legal analysis, policy development, regulatory compliance oversight, enforcement, and institutional coordination across governmental, tribal, and non-governmental actors who share regulatory authority over natural resources and environmental quality.

**Boundary criteria.** Tag Core if the competency specifically addresses legal interpretation of environmental statutes, regulatory compliance program management, policy analysis and development, NEPA environmental review, enforcement actions, intergovernmental coordination across jurisdictional boundaries, or tribal consultation and co-management frameworks. Key distinction from adjacent families: Environmental Policy professionals work *on* the rules; Land Use Planners *apply* them; lawyers and scientists serving management roles *use* them.

**Representative roles.**
- *Domain 6:* Environmental Attorney/Legal Counsel, Regulatory Compliance Manager, Environmental Permitting Specialist, NEPA Coordinator, Environmental Policy Analyst, Regulatory Affairs Manager, Environmental Enforcement Officer, Policy Advisor/Specialist, Legislative Analyst, Intergovernmental Affairs Manager, Tribal Liaison/Consultation Coordinator, Transboundary Conservation Coordinator
- *Domain 5:* Public Involvement Specialist (regulatory comment processes)

**Domain span.** Primary: Domain 6 (environmental law, regulatory compliance, intergovernmental relations, policy development). Secondary: Domain 5 (public participation in regulatory processes, tribal consultation), Domain 3 (organizational compliance management).

**Overlap notes.** Blurs with *Land Use Planning* — distinction is that Environmental Policy focuses on the regulatory framework itself (statutes, regulations, enforcement), while Land Use Planning applies regulatory frameworks to spatial decisions. Blurs with *Environmental Health & Justice* where EJ is pursued through regulatory mechanisms. Blurs with *Conservation Finance* where market mechanisms are created or governed through regulatory action.

## Family 9: Land Use Planning

**Definition.** Professionals who coordinate the spatial allocation of land uses across landscapes, jurisdictions, and ownership types to achieve integrated environmental, social, and economic objectives. The core identity is the intersection of spatial analysis, regulatory process, and stakeholder negotiation — translating environmental assessments, community values, and policy constraints into land use patterns, zoning frameworks, and comprehensive management plans that govern how land is used over time.

**Boundary criteria.** Tag Core if the competency specifically involves spatial planning processes, land use allocation and zoning decisions, comprehensive or general plan development, public land management planning (Forest Plans, Resource Management Plans), environmental assessment in the planning context, or scenario planning for alternative landscape futures. Key distinguishing feature: Land Use Planning competencies require both spatial reasoning and a process for legitimating allocation decisions across multiple stakeholders and future time horizons.

**Representative roles.**
- *Domain 6:* Land Use Planner/Environmental Planner, Regional Planner, Urban Planner (Environmental Focus), Public Land Planning Specialist, Zoning Administrator, Community Development Director, Comprehensive Planning Manager, Scenario Planning Specialist
- *Domain 2:* GIS Analyst in planning contexts

**Domain span.** Primary: Domain 6 (land use planning skill area, intergovernmental coordination). Secondary: Domain 2 (GIS and spatial analysis for planning, scenario modeling), Domain 5 (public involvement in planning processes), Domain 1 (ecological assessment to inform planning).

**Overlap notes.** Blurs with *Environmental Policy & Regulation* — planning produces spatial allocation decisions; policy works on the regulatory frameworks that constrain or enable those decisions. Blurs with *Conservation Science* where ecological assessments directly inform planning alternatives. Blurs with *Environmental Data Science* where spatial analysis is the primary technical tool.

## Family 10: Sustainable Agriculture

**Definition.** Professionals who design and implement agricultural systems that maintain productivity, economic viability, and ecological function simultaneously across crop, livestock, and integrated farming and ranching systems. The core identity is agroecological design — applying knowledge of soil health, water management, plant-pest interactions, and landscape stewardship to develop farming and grazing systems that produce food and fiber while regenerating the natural capital base.

**Boundary criteria.** Tag Core if the competency is specifically about agricultural production systems: soil health assessment and management, crop production and agronomy, livestock and range management, irrigation design and management, integrated pest management, agroforestry, or precision agriculture at the farm or ranch scale. Key test: does the competency require understanding of a *managed production relationship* between land and agricultural output, or would it apply equally to non-productive ecological management?

**Representative roles.**
- *Domain 1:* Agroecologist, Soil Carbon Scientist, Regenerative Agriculture Specialist, Precision Agriculture Technician
- *Domain 4:* Farm Manager/Agricultural Technician, Regenerative Agriculture Specialist, Precision Agriculture Technician, Irrigation Technician/Water Manager, Integrated Pest Management Specialist, Soil Health Technician, Agroforestry Technician, Range Technician/Rangeland Management Specialist

**Domain span.** Primary: Domain 1 (agroecology, soil science), Domain 4 (agricultural implementation, precision agriculture technology). Secondary: Domain 3 (farm business management), Domain 6 (agricultural policy, water rights, ecosystem service markets for agriculture).

**Overlap notes.** Blurs with *Ecological Restoration* for rangeland restoration and degraded agricultural land recovery — distinction is whether the primary objective is production or ecological recovery. Blurs with *Conservation Science* for farmland biodiversity research. Blurs with *Conservation Finance* for agricultural carbon markets and water quality trading programs.

## Family 11: Environmental Education & Communication

**Definition.** Professionals who design and deliver programs, products, and experiences that build environmental literacy, foster pro-environmental attitudes and stewardship behaviors, and engage diverse audiences in understanding and caring for natural systems. The core identity is communicative and pedagogical — whether through formal education curricula, science communication, interpretive programming, or behavior change campaigns, these professionals translate complex environmental information into forms that move people toward understanding and action.

**Boundary criteria.** Tag Core if the competency specifically involves educational program design and delivery, science communication and interpretation, behavior change strategy, environmental outreach campaign design, or citizen science program coordination as the professional's *primary* work product. Key test: is this competency about producing an *audience experience*, or is communication a supporting skill for producing some other primary output?

**Representative roles.**
- *Domain 5:* Environmental Educator, Education Program Manager/Director, Curriculum Specialist, Science Communicator/Science Writer, Interpretive Ranger/Naturalist, Citizen Science Coordinator, Outreach Coordinator, Behavior Change Specialist, Social Marketing Manager
- *Domain 4:* Park Ranger (Interpretation), Visitor Center Manager

**Domain span.** Primary: Domain 5 (education and outreach, science communication, behavior change). Secondary: Domain 3 (program management for education-focused organizations), Domain 6 (policy-relevant communication in public comment processes).

**Overlap notes.** Overlaps with *Environmental Health & Justice* for community education in frontline communities. Overlaps with *Conservation Science* for science communication of research findings. The key distinction from all other families: primary measure of success is an audience outcome (knowledge, attitude, behavior), not a land, water, species, or organizational outcome.

## Family 12: Fire Management

**Definition.** Professionals who plan, implement, and manage wildland fire suppression, prescribed fire operations, and landscape-scale fuels management programs to protect communities, maintain ecosystem function, and reduce the risk of catastrophic wildfire. The core identity integrates fire behavior science, operational fire management under the Incident Command System, and landscape-level fire ecology.

**Boundary criteria.** Tag Core if the competency is specifically about fire behavior prediction and analysis, prescribed fire planning and execution, fuels inventory and treatment, fire effects assessment, fire program management, incident command, or fire prevention. Key test: does this competency require knowledge of fire-specific dynamics (behavior, effects, risk), or is it a general competency that happens to appear in fire management work?

**Representative roles.**
- *Domain 4:* Fire Management Officer, Prescribed Fire Specialist/Burn Boss, Fuels Management Technician, Wildland Firefighter/Hotshot Crew Member, Engine Boss/Captain, Helitack Crew Member, Smokejumper, Fire Prevention Technician, Fire Lookout
- *Domain 3:* Regional Fire Management Director, Fire Program Manager

**Domain span.** Primary: Domain 4 (fire management operations, field implementation). Secondary: Domain 1 (fire ecology, fuel characterization, fire effects science), Domain 3 (fire program management), Domain 6 (air quality permitting for prescribed fire, public land management planning with fire components).

**Overlap notes.** Overlaps with *Forestry* where prescribed fire is a silvicultural tool — distinction is whether fire is the organizing professional identity or a tool within a broader silvicultural practice. Overlaps with *Ecological Restoration* where fire is used as a restoration treatment. Overlaps with *Land Use Planning* for community wildfire protection planning.

## Family 13: Environmental Health & Justice

**Definition.** Professionals who assess, address, and advocate regarding the disproportionate environmental hazard burden carried by vulnerable and marginalized communities, working at the intersection of environmental science, public health, and social equity. The core identity bridges technical assessment capacity — environmental health risk analysis, cumulative impacts assessment, air and water quality characterization — with community engagement skills and systems-level advocacy for equitable environmental outcomes and regulatory reform.

**Boundary criteria.** Tag Core if the competency specifically addresses environmental health risk assessment methodology, cumulative and disproportionate impacts analysis, community-based participatory research for environmental health, environmental justice provisions in regulatory processes, health-based regulatory advocacy, or equity analysis of environmental programs and policies. The distinctive criterion: Environmental Health & Justice competencies require holding *both* technical rigor and equity analysis simultaneously — neither alone is sufficient.

**Representative roles.**
- *Domain 6:* Environmental Health Scientist, Environmental Justice Coordinator, Community Health Specialist, Environmental Health Risk Assessor, Air Quality Specialist
- *Domain 5:* Environmental Justice Coordinator (community engagement), Tribal Liaison (EJ contexts), Community Organizer
- *Domain 1:* Environmental health risk researchers

**Domain span.** Primary: Domain 6 (environmental health regulatory framework, EJ provisions, cumulative impacts policy), Domain 5 (community engagement with frontline communities, cultural competency). Secondary: Domain 1 (exposure science, toxicology, health risk assessment methodology), Domain 3 (program management for community-facing organizations).

**Overlap notes.** Overlaps with *Environmental Policy & Regulation* — distinction is that Environmental Health & Justice centers community health outcomes and equity as the primary measure of regulatory adequacy, whereas Environmental Policy centers compliance with the regulatory framework as it exists. Overlaps with *Environmental Education & Communication* for community environmental literacy. Overlaps with *Climate Science* for climate justice.

## Family 14: Corporate Sustainability & ESG

**Definition.** Professionals who develop, implement, and report on corporate environmental, social, and governance (ESG) commitments within business enterprises — translating regulatory requirements, investor expectations, supply chain standards, and voluntary reporting frameworks into operational programs, data management systems, and public disclosures. The core identity is organizational performance management through an environmental and social accountability lens: making environmental commitments credible, measurable, and actionable within corporate governance structures.

**Boundary criteria.** Tag Core if the competency specifically addresses corporate sustainability strategy development, ESG metric design and reporting, materiality assessment, supply chain environmental management, net-zero transition planning, circular economy operations, or sustainability governance within corporate structures (board reporting, external audit preparation). Key test: does this competency require knowledge of *corporate governance and investor/stakeholder accountability*, or is it a general management skill that happens to appear in corporate contexts?

**Representative roles.**
- *Domain 3:* Sustainability Manager/Director, Corporate Social Responsibility Manager, ESG Manager, Chief Sustainability Officer, Sustainability Analyst, ESG Reporting Specialist, Circular Economy Manager
- *Domain 6:* Green Bond Analyst, Natural Capital Accountant, Carbon Markets Analyst (corporate procurement)

**Domain span.** Primary: Domain 3 (Corporate Sustainability & ESG management, Circular Economy & Waste Systems), Domain 6 (environmental markets, carbon accounting, regulatory disclosure requirements). Secondary: Domain 2 (ESG data management systems), Domain 1 (natural capital assessment, biodiversity footprinting).

**Overlap notes.** Overlaps with *Climate Science & Adaptation* for climate risk disclosure and net-zero planning — distinction is whether expertise is climate science informing corporate strategy (Climate Science) or the governance and accountability frameworks within which climate commitments are embedded (Corporate Sustainability). Overlaps with *Conservation Finance & Markets* for corporate procurement of credits — Conservation Finance creates the supply side; Corporate Sustainability represents the demand side.

## Family 15: Conservation Finance & Markets

**Definition.** Professionals who structure, execute, and manage financial mechanisms designed to direct capital toward conservation outcomes — including carbon markets, biodiversity credit systems, payment for ecosystem services (PES) programs, conservation easements, green bonds, and blended finance instruments. The core identity sits at the intersection of financial structuring expertise and ecological credibility — designing and operating market-based incentives that make conservation economically viable at scale.

**Boundary criteria.** Tag Core if the competency specifically involves designing or operating financial instruments for conservation: conservation finance structure design, ecosystem service valuation and payment program management, carbon project development and verification, biodiversity credit methodology and accounting, natural capital accounting, or environmental market mechanism design and governance. Key test: does this competency require knowledge of *financial instrument design and capital flows*, not just resource valuation or policy analysis?

**Representative roles.**
- *Domain 6:* Conservation Finance Manager, Carbon Markets Analyst, Environmental Markets Specialist, Carbon Credit Developer, Biodiversity Credit Specialist, Natural Capital Accountant, PES Coordinator, Green Bond Analyst, Voluntary Carbon Market Specialist, Offset Project Manager
- *Domain 1:* Ecosystem Services Specialist (market-facing)

**Domain span.** Primary: Domain 6 (environmental markets, natural resource economics, conservation finance). Secondary: Domain 1 (ecosystem services science as technical foundation for market mechanisms), Domain 3 (financial management for conservation portfolios), Domain 2 (monitoring systems that verify ecosystem service delivery).

**Overlap notes.** Overlaps with *Environmental Policy & Regulation* (market mechanism governance) and *Corporate Sustainability* (corporate buyers of conservation credits). Operational tests: Policy professionals work on rules that govern markets; Conservation Finance professionals design and operate within those markets. Conservation Finance creates supply-side instruments; Corporate Sustainability represents demand.

## Assessment: Is 15 the Right Number?

**Conservation Finance & Markets as separate family:** Kept separate. The core professional skill — financial instrument design and capital flow management — is categorically distinct from regulatory compliance and policy advocacy. Conservation Finance will generate fewer Core tags than major families; tagging guidance should apply boundary criteria with discipline to prevent scope creep from Environmental Policy.

**Fire Management as separate family:** Kept separate. Distinct career track (NWCG qualifications), distinct operational culture (Incident Command System), and extends to non-forest systems (savanna, tallgrass prairie, shrubland) that forestry does not claim.

## Profession Families the Taxonomy Does Not Currently Address as Distinct Categories

The following professional communities exist within the environmental workforce but are not represented as distinct families in this taxonomy. The Delphi panel is invited to evaluate whether these gaps are consequential for the taxonomy's purposes.

**Ocean and Marine Resource Management.** Marine protected area management, coral restoration, marine fisheries stock assessment, and coastal zone management have a distinct coastal/marine institutional context and workforce that currently sits implicitly within Wildlife & Fisheries and Conservation Science. Whether these constitute a distinct professional identity for taxonomy purposes is a question for expert review.

**Tribal Natural Resource Management.** Tribal fish and wildlife departments, tribal forestry programs, and tribal water management operate under a distinct legal and governance framework (tribal sovereignty, treaty rights, co-management agreements) that spans most profession families but is not named as a distinct family. The taxonomy currently addresses this primarily through cultural competency and tribal consultation competencies in Domains 5 and 6.

**Urban Parks and Green Infrastructure.** Urban ecology, parks management, and green stormwater infrastructure management have a distinct professional identity that spans Land Use Planning, Ecological Restoration, and Environmental Education but is not fully captured by any one family. The workforce dimension of urban environmental work — urban foresters, green infrastructure specialists, park planners — may warrant distinct representation.

These are documented as gaps for Delphi evaluation, not as planned additions to the current taxonomy.

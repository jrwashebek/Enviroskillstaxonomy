# Structural Conditions of Environmental Management Professions

## Analytic framing and data sources

This report treats “environmental management” as an ecology of semi-professions, professions, and adjacent expert occupations whose work is oriented to land, water, biodiversity, climate risk, environmental compliance, and organizational adaptation to environmental constraints. The analytic center of gravity is entity["book","The System of Professions","abbott 1988 book"], which theorizes professional life as competition over *jurisdiction*—the link between an occupational group and the tasks it claims to control—and emphasizes how jurisdictions shift under disturbances through abstraction, audience politics, and workplace dynamics. citeturn21search0turn21search4turn21search17 In Abbott’s account, “profession” is less a status label than an unstable settlement produced by multi-arena contestation (law/administration, public discourse, and the workplace), with workplace boundary-blurring and “assimilation” often diverging from formal legal demarcations. citeturn21search3turn21search5turn21search22

Empirically, the report draws on three kinds of structural evidence, deliberately emphasizing measures that can reveal professional maturation mechanisms (credentialing, classification, entry control) rather than only “green jobs” rhetoric. First, occupational and wage baselines are taken from the federal statistical infrastructure (especially entity["organization","U.S. Bureau of Labor Statistics","us labor stats agency"]) and the O*NET/BLS employment-projections layer used by O*NET to display employment and wage estimates (e.g., “Employment (2024)”). citeturn5view1turn19search0turn19search23turn18search23 Second, federal workforce “structure” is approximated by qualification and classification systems curated by entity["organization","U.S. Office of Personnel Management","us federal hr agency"]—notably (a) position-series definitions and classification standards, which operationalize government’s concept of “the work,” and (b) “positive education” occupational-series lists that codify degree requirements for entry to many natural-resource scientist series and report “current federal employees” counts from FedScope. citeturn6view0turn15search2turn15search14turn15search25 Third, mission-capacity constraints and compensation/infrastructure dynamics are grounded in federal oversight (especially entity["organization","U.S. Government Accountability Office","us audit watchdog"], plus some departmental budget documents), where audit logic often makes tacit barriers legible (e.g., duty-station remoteness, housing affordability, hiring friction, career ladder issues). citeturn15search1turn15search5turn16search11turn15search28

Two additional evidence streams are essential for the barriers layer: (a) diversity pipeline measurement anchored by entity["organization","Green 2.0","env ngo transparency report"]’s annual environmental NGO/foundation “Transparency Report,” which explicitly documents a “green ceiling” pattern (empirically: declining representation with increasing organizational responsibility) and reports a first-ever NGO-level decline in staff of color across all levels in its 2024 tracking year; and (b) the sociological baseline on diversity in environmental institutions, especially the 2014 multi-sector study led by entity["people","Dorceta Taylor","environmental sociologist"] that documents leadership stratification and pipeline/retention mechanisms. citeturn7search4turn7search0turn7search6turn7search2

A note on measurement: many “profession families” below are not isomorphic with Standard Occupational Classification (SOC) codes. Several are cross-cutting jurisdictions assembled from multiple SOCs and (crucially) multiple credential regimes (e.g., corporate ESG spans finance, law, engineering, communications). This non-isomorphism is not a nuisance; it is itself diagnostic of jurisdictional immaturity and contested settlement—an Abbottian signal that the “profession” is not yet stabilized as a coherent jurisdiction with a unified abstraction-and-credential pipeline. citeturn21search4turn21search17turn13search3

### Abbottian assessment: how the environmental “profession families” stratify

A useful way to interpret the table is to separate *credential-closed* jurisdictions from *mission-embedded* jurisdictions and *proto-jurisdictions*.

Credential-closed jurisdictions in this domain are mostly those that borrow closure from adjacent licensed professions or from highly codified federal qualification systems. Water resources is the clearest case: when work is framed as engineering design and public safety, professional engineering licensure (via standardized exam-and-experience sequences) functions as a jurisdictional gate, and federal hydrology positions also require explicit course-credit combinations. citeturn10search11turn10search14turn6view0 This tends to raise “jurisdictional maturity” in Abbott’s sense: the occupation can defend its claim because the knowledge base is abstract enough to travel across cases (modeling, measurement, inference) and because the credentialing apparatus is legible to states and employers as an entry-control mechanism. citeturn21search17turn21search4turn10search11

Mission-embedded jurisdictions (forestry, wildlife/fisheries, fire) are structurally different. They often have robust internal qualification systems—sometimes stronger than formal licensure—but their jurisdiction is secured less by statute than by organizational embedding in land-management agencies, incident systems, and historically sedimented “audience” expectations (the public, Congress, state co-managers). citeturn20search10turn15search27turn21search4 This produces a tension Abbott explicitly anticipates: workplace competence rules (e.g., NWCG task books and currency requirements) can be more decisive than formal legal boundaries, yet those same workplace systems are vulnerable to external disturbance (budget cycling, political reorganization) that can disrupt career ladders and thus disrupt jurisdictional reproduction. citeturn20search1turn15search5turn7news42

Proto-jurisdictions (environmental data science/geospatial; conservation finance/markets; corporate sustainability/ESG; EJ as a profession family) exhibit the classic Abbottian markers of an unsettled settlement: (a) multiple adjacent professions can credibly claim the work, (b) credential stacking substitutes for a unified gatekeeping apparatus, and (c) “abstraction” is often strong in technical terms but not institutionalized as a profession-specific knowledge system with agreed diagnostics/treatments and career stages. citeturn21search22turn13search3turn12search0

### Contested families: where the vacancy is being claimed

**Environmental Data Science & Geospatial.** This family is best understood as an *assemblage* rather than a profession. Its “jurisdiction” is being built out of at least four mature knowledge systems—computer science/software engineering, statistics, GIS/geodesy/cartography, and domain sciences (ecology/hydrology)—with no single professional association controlling diagnosis/treatment norms for “environmental” applications. The existence of GIS credentialing mechanisms (e.g., GISP requiring a portfolio review, exam, and multiyear experience) indicates partial professionalization, but it primarily certifies GIS practice rather than the broader “environmental data science” jurisdiction. citeturn8search6turn19search14turn21search22 In Abbottian terms, the likely settlement is not exclusive jurisdiction but *division of labor* and *advisory* forms: domain experts retain interpretive authority while datacraft roles provide infrastructures of measurement/modeling; this settlement becomes fragile under substitution-first AI adoption because many “analytics substrate” tasks are technically performable by AI systems. citeturn12search0turn12search2turn21search22

**Conservation Finance & Markets.** This is a textbook jurisdictional vacancy. The work is real (capital allocation, valuation of ecosystem services, market design, MRV—monitoring/reporting/verification—requirements), but credentialing is essentially borrowed: financial credentials and sustainability disclosure credentials rather than “conservation finance” credentials. citeturn18search14turn9search1turn21search22 In Abbott’s framework, this produces a predictable vulnerability: “platforms” and standards can function as quasi-professional infrastructures (defining what counts as valid measurement, auditability, and disclosure) and can crowd out profession-led closure. citeturn12search0turn9search1turn21search22

**Fire Management.** Fire management has a strong *operational* qualification structure (interagency standards specifying training, experience, physical fitness, and currency, with Position Task Books as standardized performance documentation) that resembles a professional closure system even without licensure. citeturn20search10turn20search1turn20search17 Yet the system’s brittleness is not mainly jurisdictional encroachment; it is workforce reproduction under material constraints (pay, housing, burnout, remote duty stations) and under governance shocks. GAO repeatedly identifies low pay, career advancement challenges, and remote/expensive duty stations as recruitment/retention barriers, which directly threatens the intergenerational transfer of tacit operational knowledge that the qualification system presumes. citeturn15search1turn15search5turn16search11 This is an Abbottian “workplace arena” dominance: competence is defined in the field/incident system, but the capacity to keep a workforce in that arena is governed elsewhere (appropriations, classification/pay policy, housing markets). citeturn21search5turn1search18turn16search11

**Environmental Health & Justice.** The structural fact here is *necessary hybridity*. EJ work requires scientific inference about exposure pathways, public-health surveillance, legal/regulatory reasoning, and community trust/participatory practice. The literature explicitly frames EJ as requiring coordinated efforts across researchers, regulators, legislators, and the public—i.e., an ecology rather than a single profession. citeturn11search3turn11search19 Credentialing exists in adjacent domains (public health certification eligibility; environmental health specialist credentials), but there is no single professional body that can plausibly monopolize EJ in the way a mature profession would. citeturn11search0turn11search2turn21search22 Politically contingent staffing (e.g., documented layoffs of EPA EJ staff in 2025) further increases vulnerability by disrupting institutional memory and weakening the organizational footholds through which EJ competencies are reproduced. citeturn17news40turn7news41turn21search22

**Corporate Sustainability & ESG.** Here the contest is between established professions (finance/accounting, law/compliance, engineering/risk, communications) and rapidly evolving disclosure/standard-setting infrastructures. Investor demand for ESG disclosure is well documented, and “materiality” logic pulls the jurisdiction toward finance and securities disclosure rather than environmental science per se. citeturn18search14turn9search1 The credential ecosystem reflects that pull: finance-oriented sustainability credentials and disclosure-specific credentials rather than “environmental management” credentials. citeturn9search4turn9search1 From an Abbottian viewpoint, the likely jurisdictional settlement is unstable because the core tasks are partly codifiable (reporting workflows, metric compilation) and thus technically exposed to automation, while high-end judgment (strategy, risk tradeoffs, stakeholder negotiation) remains difficult to mechanize. citeturn12search0turn21search22turn9search1

### Taxonomy Implications

The taxonomy’s profession-family layer will be most robust if it treats “profession families” not only as content domains but as **jurisdictional settlement types**. Abbott’s framework implies that stability tiers should not be assigned only by “topic durability” but by the *strength of jurisdictional closure mechanisms* (licensure, qualification standards, accreditation pipelines, and organizational embedding). citeturn21search4turn21search17turn21search22

For stability-tier assignments, the evidence above suggests that Environmental Data Science & Geospatial, Conservation Finance & Markets, and (parts of) Corporate Sustainability & ESG are *structurally emergent* despite being anchored in durable underlying techniques, because their professional identity and credentialing closure remain unsettled and contested. citeturn12search0turn9search1turn8search6 Conversely, Fire Management’s competencies may be “durable” in task terms but “transition-sensitive” in workforce reproduction terms because pay/housing and governance shocks can collapse the ladder that transmits tacit competence. citeturn15search5turn1search18turn16search11

For Delphi review flags, the taxonomy should explicitly flag families with (a) non-isomorphic SOC mapping and (b) credential stacking as the primary entry signal. These conditions predict jurisdictional volatility and help explain why competency boundaries will be contested in expert panels (experts will import their home-profession’s abstraction system). citeturn21search22turn13search3turn21search17

For exposure classification, Abbott’s emphasis on abstraction as a mechanism of jurisdictional shift suggests the taxonomy should distinguish competencies that are (i) formally codified and portable (high abstraction), (ii) semi-codified but context-dependent (mid abstraction), and (iii) place-based tacit craft knowledge. That distinction becomes decisive under AI substitution/augmentation scenarios. citeturn21search4turn12search0turn12search2

## Barriers and structural constraints

### Compensation and pay structure

The compensation structure of environmental management is segmented along three axes: (1) federal pay architecture (GS locality + classification), (2) state/local and nonprofit pay regimes, and (3) private-sector “market-premium” niches (consulting, ESG/finance, advanced data roles). Federal pay is bounded by statutory and administrative pay systems that are designed to achieve comparability through locality-based adjustments but are structurally prone to “pay compression” and slow responsiveness relative to fast-moving labor markets for specialized skills. citeturn16search2turn16search25turn15search14 The GS pay tables set the base constraints for many environmental roles in federal agencies, and the locality pay system uses BLS survey infrastructure to compare federal and nonfederal pay for comparable levels of work. citeturn1search8turn16search2

At the macro level, federal compensation competitiveness is empirically contested, but high-quality synthesis indicates that comparisons depend strongly on education level and on whether benefits are included. The Congressional Budget Office’s 2024 comparison of federal and private compensation for 2022 emphasizes that wage/benefit comparisons vary across worker groups and that benefits are a large component of total compensation. citeturn16search6 For environmental management specifically, the more relevant structural point is not the average federal premium/penalty but the *shape of the ladder*: if entry and mid-level pay is constrained while private consulting and market-oriented roles offer steep premiums, the sector experiences an “apprenticeship collapse” where practitioners exit before accumulating the tacit, situated knowledge that makes them most mission-effective. This mechanism is directly visible in GAO’s diagnosis of wildland fire: low pay and career advancement challenges are among the core recruitment and retention barriers, implying that pay structure interacts with ladder formation and knowledge transmission. citeturn15search1turn15search5turn21search22

The wildland fire compensation crisis has unusually clear evidence because it is repeatedly audited and politically salient. GAO identifies low pay and remote/expensive duty stations as barriers; Forest Service budget materials and USDA explanatory notes explicitly connect housing unaffordability to higher declination and attrition. citeturn15search1turn16search11turn15search28 The compensation mechanism is not only low base pay but reliance on overtime/hazard-linked earnings, creating volatility and making “living near duty stations” structurally difficult in high-cost western communities. citeturn15search1turn16search26turn1search13 The 2025 policy landscape includes a permanent pay reform pathway: the Department of Labor’s FECA bulletins summarize statutory changes under Public Law 119-4 (Full-Year Continuing Appropriations and Extensions Act, 2025), including Section 1807, described as establishing permanent pay reform for federal wildland firefighters. citeturn1search18 DOI guidance similarly describes updated pay-setting changes for federal wildland firefighters under that public law. citeturn1search14

In nonprofits, the empirical record on wage differentials is contested across the broader literature, and BLS explicitly notes the absence of consensus on how nonprofit wages and benefits compare to for-profit and public sectors. citeturn16search0 This matters because environmental management relies heavily on large NGOs and foundations for conservation science, restoration, policy advocacy, and environmental education—especially in roles where professional closure is weak and compensation cannot be defended by licensure-based scarcity. In Abbottian terms, weak closure makes the field more vulnerable to wage competition because the labor supply is not restricted by formal entry controls; mission motives can substitute for pay until attrition breaks the knowledge ladder. citeturn21search4turn16search0turn15search5

Finally, market-premium niches (ESG, sustainability disclosure, conservation finance, specialized data roles) show extreme within-domain dispersion. The credential ecosystem for ESG (finance and disclosure credentials) signals that these roles are pulled into finance labor markets, where pay norms are not constrained by public-sector ladders. citeturn9search4turn9search1turn18search14 This divergence produces a structural compensation gradient that can drain experienced practitioners from agencies and NGOs into consulting/finance-adjacent roles—especially when tacit knowledge is undervalued in formal HR classification systems that reward codified task lists over place-based competence. citeturn15search14turn12search0turn21search22

### Geographic constraint and remote posting

Environmental management work is spatially concentrated in rural and remote landscapes (fire-prone forests, watersheds, coasts, rangelands) and in small western communities where housing markets have become increasingly unaffordable relative to public-sector wages. This is not simply a “lifestyle” issue; it is a structural recruitment constraint because geographic immobility interacts with class and family-network constraints, filtering who can accept and sustain these roles. GAO explicitly identifies remote or expensive duty stations as a barrier for federal wildland firefighters and notes lack of services and remoteness as part of the mechanism. citeturn15search1turn15search5 USDA budget explanatory notes for the Forest Service directly state that many duty stations are in areas where housing costs and availability are beyond many employees’ economic means, linking those conditions to declination and attrition. citeturn16search11

The housing constraint has prompted nontrivial policy responses, including Forest Service efforts to ease housing costs for some employees, reflecting that housing is being treated as a workforce infrastructure issue rather than an individual problem. citeturn16search26turn15search28 But the structural contradiction remains: the work is geographically “sticky” (it must be done where the landscape is), while many forms of professional community, dual-career household stability, and access to services are geographically “non-sticky” and cluster in metros. In Abbottian terms, this produces a conflict between workplace jurisdiction (where competence is constituted) and the external environment that determines who can remain in that workplace long enough to become competent. citeturn21search5turn15search1turn21search22

Geographic constraint also has a demographic implication: remote posting requirements systematically exclude practitioners without financial cushion, without family-proximity support networks, or without the ability to absorb housing volatility (including seasonal housing in fire and recreation economies). The evidence base is stronger for wildland fire (GAO + agency budget documents) than for other families, which is itself a gap: similar dynamics likely operate in forestry, wildlife management, and watershed science, but are less consistently audited. citeturn15search1turn16search11turn5view1

### Racial and ethnic diversity pipeline

The diversity evidence base in environmental management has two structural anchors: long-run documentation of underrepresentation and leadership stratification, and ongoing sector transparency tracking. Taylor’s 2014 multi-institution study documents severe underrepresentation of people of color in environmental organizations and finds that the more senior the leadership position, the lower the percentage of ethnic minorities—an empirical pattern consistent with what later reporting calls a “green ceiling.” citeturn7search6turn7search2 Green 2.0’s 2024 Transparency Report explicitly states that it documents the first decline in staff of color at all NGO levels in the history of the report (while foundations showed marginal improvements), suggesting either a structural reversal or a measurement/participation artifact that demands careful interpretation. citeturn7search4turn7search0

The question of whether the 2024 decline represents structural change or artifact cannot be “resolved” from the report language alone; the defensible analytic move is to treat it as a **sentinel indicator**: if a long-run monotonic trend reverses, the sector should assume a structural disturbance until proven otherwise. In Abbottian terms, diversity pipeline strength is not merely an equity outcome; it is a condition of jurisdictional reproduction. If entry cohorts and mid-career retention decline, tacit knowledge transmission becomes fragile—especially in mission-embedded families where competence is learned through apprenticeship-like workplace assimilation and field exposure. citeturn21search3turn7search4turn15search5

Evidence on specific pipeline mechanisms is uneven. For experiential gatekeeping, peer-reviewed work emphasizes that access to field experiences is a key determinant of participation in the environmental and natural resources workforce and notes that minimal data exist on what undergraduates prioritize when applying for field experiences—a sign that the sector still lacks robust causal identification of pipeline barriers. citeturn17search11 For early-career economic filters, the empirical case is stronger in adjacent ocean sciences: unpaid internships are explicitly identified as a barrier to diverse and equitable recruitment in marine science, with socioeconomic exclusion and early-exit mechanisms noted. citeturn17search3turn17search15 The generalization to environmental management is plausible but should be treated as an inference: many entry pathways rely on low-paid seasonal work, unpaid internships, or geographic relocation, which disproportionately burdens those without intergenerational wealth or flexible family support. citeturn17search31turn17search11turn17search3

A distinct issue is the Indigenous environmental practitioner workforce. Tribal natural resource professionals operate in legal frameworks shaped by self-determination authorities and trust-resource governance, which are not captured by standard NGO diversity metrics and often not visible in federal workforce series data. citeturn14search9turn14search34turn14search5 This is not a minor measurement limitation: it implies that “diversity” in environmental management cannot be modeled as a single pipeline and that the taxonomy should treat tribal governance contexts as producing distinct competencies and distinct career ladders.

Finally, the intersection between diversity barriers and the recent federal workforce crisis is partially evidenced but not fully quantified in public sources. Reuters documents layoffs in EPA’s Office of Environmental Justice and External Civil Rights in 2025, which plausibly affects a workforce segment that is comparatively more diverse than legacy technical series, but definitive cohort-level demographic impact requires internal HR microdata not in the public record. citeturn17news40turn7news41 This is an explicit gap: the sector lacks transparent, disaggregated impact assessments of workforce reductions on diversification gains.

### Credentialing pathways and entry barriers

Credentialing in environmental management is a patchwork that reveals the domain’s structural heterogeneity. Some families have robust, multi-layer apparatuses: forestry has programmatic accreditation and professional certification, and wildlife management has a long-running certification program with explicit education and experience requirements. citeturn20search5turn20search34turn20search3turn20search20 Fire management has an operational qualification system that tightly specifies training, experience, and measurable performance through Position Task Books; it is “professionalization without licensure,” and it is arguably stronger than licensure in its ability to constrain who can claim specific incident roles. citeturn20search10turn20search1turn20search0

At the same time, large portions of environmental management exhibit either weak closure (no controlling credential) or misaligned closure (degree requirements not clearly justified by the tacit knowledge content of the work). OPM’s “positive education” series lists illustrate how the federal system hard-codes degree/credit requirements for many natural resource and environmental science series (e.g., forestry 0460; fish biology 0482; wildlife biology 0486; hydrology 1315; meteorology 1340; oceanography 1360) and reports thousands of incumbents in those series. citeturn6view0turn10search14turn14search3 This is a structural entry barrier: even if the *work* could be performed by experienced practitioners with nontraditional backgrounds, the qualification system formalizes credential requirements as a gate in the name of professional/scientific standards. citeturn15search25turn15search14turn6view0

The key analytic question is not “Are degrees good?” but “Is degree inflation occurring—i.e., are graduate degrees required for roles where graduate-level abstraction is not necessary?” The evidence is mixed and varies by occupation. O*NET classifies hydrologists as Job Zone 5 (“extensive preparation needed”), noting that most of these occupations require graduate school, which suggests that at least some water resources roles genuinely rely on advanced abstraction and not only on tacit field skill. citeturn19search9turn19search1 But in other families, professional job performance may be dominated by fieldcraft and relational competencies (community trust, incident leadership, local ecological knowledge), while hiring systems still use degrees as a proxy for competence because those competencies are hard to measure and hard to credential. This creates an *economic filter*: unpaid/low-paid early roles and degree requirements reduce diversity and may misallocate training investment away from the tacit domains that actually drive performance. citeturn17search11turn17search31turn21search22

The absence of equivalent credentialing systems in Conservation Finance & Markets, Environmental Data Science, and Environmental Health & Justice is not merely “immaturity”; it is a structural signal that these families are in proto-jurisdictional conflict. Where there is no gate, employers often substitute credential stacking (CFA/IFRS credentials for ESG; GIS certifications for geospatial; public health/environmental health credentials for EJ), which in turn imports the values and abstraction systems of adjacent professions. citeturn9search1turn9search4turn8search6turn11search0

### Organizational culture and institutional constraints

Organizational culture operates as a “hidden” barrier because it shapes retention, identity, and whether practitioners can actually exercise their competencies inside institutional constraints. In land-management agencies, multiple mission cultures coexist—fire, timber, recreation, wildlife, watershed—and can produce internal jurisdictional tensions. GAO’s forest service decision-making report explicitly describes expected conflicts among recreation demand, sustaining wildlife and fish, and timber production, which is a governance-level manifestation of competing jurisdictions inside a single agency. citeturn15search27turn21search22 Empirically, this means that practitioners’ career progression can be constrained not only by their technical competence but by whether their work aligns with the dominant unit culture and budget authority—an internal analogue of Abbott’s system-level jurisdictional competition. citeturn21search4turn15search27turn15search16

Culture also affects safety and learning infrastructures. Research on Forest Service safety culture describes how top-down safety initiatives are questioned and shaped by employees, implying that cultural negotiation is part of how competencies are enacted and institutionalized. citeturn15search4turn15search0 In fire management, culture and institutional constraints are intertwined with compensation and staffing: GAO documents barriers including mental health challenges and work-life balance, which are simultaneously “culture” and “structure” because they shape who remains long enough to transmit tacit competence. citeturn15search5turn15search1turn20search1

Federal civil-service classification systems impose additional constraints by shaping the official definition of “the work” and thus what practitioners can be hired to do, evaluated on, and promoted for. OPM classification standards define occupational series (for example, Environmental Protection Specialist GS-0028 as administrative/program work related to environmental protection programs, requiring specialized knowledge of administering environmental protection programs). citeturn15search2turn17search5 The classification standards program is rooted in Title 5 classification law and explicitly functions as a basic building block for personnel management and equal-pay-for-equal-work principles. citeturn15search14turn15search6 From a skills-taxonomy viewpoint, this introduces a persistent mismatch risk: the taxonomy can describe a competency domain that is real in practice, but if that competency does not appear in a position description aligned with a GS series, the employee may be structurally unable to practice or develop it (or to be recognized/rewarded for it). citeturn15search25turn15search10turn15search2

This mismatch is particularly salient in cross-disciplinary families (EJ, data science, corporate sustainability) where competencies span multiple series. The DOI’s development of a GS-0028 competency model is a rare example of an attempt to consolidate and standardize competencies for an environmental protection workforce across a department, suggesting institutional recognition that classification fragmentation undermines workforce planning. citeturn17search17turn15search2

### Taxonomy Implications

For stability tiers, the barriers evidence implies that “stability” must incorporate **workforce reproduction stability**, not only task stability. Fire management competencies might look stable in task terms, but GAO’s persistent recruitment/retention barriers and the housing-pay interaction documented in USDA materials imply instability in the ladder that reproduces those competencies. citeturn15search5turn16search11turn1search18

For exposure classification, compensation and geographic constraints should be encoded as *structural risk multipliers* that raise the probability of knowledge loss even in augmentation-first AI regimes. If entry cohorts cannot afford to enter or remain, AI augmentation cannot compensate for missing tacit learning substrate. citeturn15search1turn12search2turn21search22

For Delphi review, diversity evidence suggests that the taxonomy should include an explicit “pipeline fragility” flag for competencies that rely on field experiences and apprenticeship-like assimilation (fieldcraft-heavy domains). The Jensen et al. evidence on field-experience access, coupled with Green 2.0’s documented leadership stratification and 2024 decline signal, implies a credible risk that “who knows the work” is shrinking even if formal job counts remain steady. citeturn17search11turn7search4turn7search6

For the 215-competency structure, credentialing fragmentation implies that competencies associated with proto-jurisdictions (data science, conservation finance, EJ) should be treated as **cross-family bundles** rather than as single-family possessions. The likely empirical failure mode is misclassification: practitioners will disagree on “ownership” because they occupy different home-profession abstraction systems. citeturn21search22turn13search3turn21search17

## Workforce futures under alternative scenarios

### Scenario matrix

The scenario logic uses two axes: federal investment level (sustained/expanded vs contracted/withdrawn) and AI adoption mode (augmentation-first vs substitution-first). “AI adoption mode” is treated as an organizational strategy choice rather than a technological inevitability, consistent with the empirical literature showing that generative AI can complement workers (raise productivity; facilitate learning for less experienced workers) but can also plausibly substitute for codified tasks depending on implementation incentives. citeturn12search2turn12search5turn12search0 The Iceberg Index framework is used as a conceptual scaffold for exposure: it measures the wage value of skills that AI systems can technically perform within occupations, which is analytically useful because it separates *technical performability* from *organizational adoption*. citeturn12search0

| | AI augmentation-first | AI substitution-first |
|---|---|---|
| Sustained/expanded federal investment | **Scenario A**: capacity expansion + hybridization | **Scenario B**: throughput expansion + ladder collapse risk |
| Contracted/withdrawn federal investment | **Scenario C**: “thin state” + augmentation triage | **Scenario D**: austerity + automation + irreversible knowledge loss risk |

### Scenario A: sustained investment and augmentation-first

**Workforce shape (2030–2035).** Under sustained BIL/IRA-scale public investment and augmentation-first AI adoption, the environmental workforce “thickens” in project delivery and monitoring capacity, with AI used to accelerate analysis, scenario modeling, and documentation while preserving human judgment, stakeholder negotiation, and field verification. The closest empirical analogue for augmentation-first effects is the generative AI workplace evidence showing productivity gains with heterogeneous impacts and evidence consistent with learning benefits for less experienced workers (AI disseminating best practices and helping workers move down the experience curve). citeturn12search2turn12search5 In this scenario, AI is used as *scaffolding* for entry-level work rather than as replacement.

**Expanded families.** Climate science/adaptation and water resources expand due to infrastructure investment and hazard adaptation demands; ecological restoration grows through implementation pipelines; environmental data science grows as an augmentation layer across all families. The Council on Environmental Quality’s IRA appropriation oversight work illustrates that large-scale public investment can directly increase environmental governance and oversight workloads, reinforcing demand for statecraft + datacraft competencies. citeturn18search10turn12search0turn19search1

**Contracted families.** Minimal net contraction occurs, but relative share may decline for purely extractive compliance roles if AI reduces documentation time. The risk is not job loss but role reconfiguration: fewer “document processors,” more “reviewers/validators” and field-oriented verifiers.

**Most transformed families.** Environmental policy & regulation and land-use planning transform through “AI-assisted permitting” and documentation workflows; geospatial roles become more integrated into all families as remote sensing and automated change detection expand. The vulnerability is mitigated because augmentation-first preserves human accountability and retains entry-level learning substrate. citeturn12search0turn21search22turn19search14

**Competencies most valued vs at risk (Fieldcraft/Datacraft/Statecraft).** Datacraft rises in value (modeling, monitoring, evaluation) because investment increases the volume of work and because AI amplifies analytic throughput; statecraft remains essential for governance, permitting, and interagency coordination; fieldcraft becomes a bottleneck because physical verification, restoration implementation, and incident operations cannot be scaled purely digitally. The structural implication is a renewed premium on field supervisors who can translate AI outputs into operational decisions.

**Highest-leverage workforce development intervention.** Protect the entry-level ladder by designing AI-mediated apprenticeships: formalize “AI-augmented analyst-to-field-practitioner” pathways where early-career workers use AI tools while still being required to perform field validation and stakeholder engagement tasks that transmit tacit knowledge.

**Decision points.** The trajectory hinges on (1) whether agencies and major NGOs institutionalize AI as a training scaffold (explicitly measuring learning outcomes, not only throughput), (2) whether procurement and HR systems reward “field verification” competencies rather than only documentation outputs, and (3) whether housing and duty-station infrastructure is treated as workforce capacity (especially in fire and land agencies). These decisions must be made early (by ~2028) because once a generation misses apprenticeship opportunities, tacit knowledge loss becomes path-dependent. citeturn12search2turn15search1turn16search11

### Scenario B: sustained investment and substitution-first

**Workforce shape (2030–2035).** Here public investment continues, but organizations deploy AI primarily for cost reduction and throughput, substituting AI for entry-level analytical and documentation tasks. The Iceberg Index logic implies that many documentation-heavy, codifiable tasks overlap substantially with AI technical capability, making substitution feasible—especially in statecraft/datacraft hybrid work such as compliance reporting, standardized monitoring, and templated environmental review drafting. citeturn12search0turn18search25

**Expanded families.** Investment still expands climate and water infrastructure work in raw dollars, but human headcount does not expand proportionally; instead, “thin staffing + high throughput” models dominate. Environmental data science grows, but as a smaller group of high-status validators and tool governors rather than broad practitioner cohorts.

**Contracted families.** Environmental policy/regulation, land-use planning documentation roles, and portions of corporate sustainability reporting contract in headcount even as output volume rises. The likely failure mode is “hollowing out”: fewer juniors doing the work, more seniors overseeing AI outputs.

**Most structurally transformed families.** Environmental policy/regulation becomes a review-and-signature profession rather than a learning-through-production profession. The NEPA domain provides an instructive parallel: scholarship and policy commentary document that environmental impact documents are often written by consulting firms and that third-party or project-sponsor preparation raises conflict-of-interest and credibility concerns—i.e., shifting who “does the work” can destabilize trust and jurisdictional legitimacy. citeturn18search25turn18search0turn18search1

**Precedent logic from other professions.** Comparative professional research on AI and digitalization in established professions (law, accounting) emphasizes boundary work, jurisdictional control, and the risk that technologies repackage tasks and redistribute them across occupational strata rather than eliminating them outright. citeturn13search3turn21search22turn21search4 The critical risk for environmental management is *ladder destruction*: if AI performs the entry-level substrate (drafting, initial analysis), there is a credible pathway to a cohort gap where fewer practitioners develop the judgment required for later-stage work.

**Competencies most valued vs at risk.** Datacraft becomes bifurcated: high-level model governance and tool oversight valued; routine analysis at risk. Statecraft becomes bifurcated similarly: high-level regulatory judgment and negotiation valued; routine compliance documentation at risk. Fieldcraft may paradoxically become more valuable because it remains resistant to substitution, but the fieldcraft workforce may be under-supplied if ladders are broken.

**Highest-leverage workforce development intervention.** Create “protected learning substrate” mandates: require that certain categories of environmental decision products (e.g., permitting rationales, NEPA alternatives analysis) include a human-produced component built by supervised early-career staff, analogous to training requirements in other professions where apprenticeship is necessary for competence reproduction.

**Decision points.** The highest leverage points are (1) procurement and performance metrics (do they reward throughput only, or learning + quality?), (2) accountability regimes (who is liable for errors—humans, agencies, contractors?), and (3) professional associations’ ability to define practice standards that require human judgment and training pathways. These must be set while investment is high; otherwise organizations will “lock in” substitution because it appears cost-effective in the short term. citeturn12search0turn18search1turn21search22

### Scenario C: contracted investment and augmentation-first

**Workforce shape (2030–2035).** This scenario extends the post-2025 contraction trend in federal staffing and program capacity while practitioners adopt AI augmentation to maintain mission output with fewer people. Recent reporting documents substantial workforce reductions in major environmental agencies (e.g., Forest Service layoffs; EPA reductions), indicating the plausibility of a continued contraction baseline. citeturn7news42turn7news41turn7search7 Under augmentation-first, remaining staff rely on AI to triage workloads, automate repetitive components, and preserve some analytic capacity.

**Surviving families vs existential threats.** Families with strong private-sector demand retain capacity in recognizable form: environmental data science/geospatial, corporate sustainability/ESG, conservation finance/markets. Families concentrated in public agency employment (forestry, wildlife/fisheries, fire, environmental policy/regulation, parts of environmental health/EJ) face capacity threats because the institutional home that anchors jurisdiction is thinned. citeturn6view0turn15search5turn17news40turn21search22

**Knowledge transmission mechanics.** Augmentation-first can preserve tacit knowledge only if enough practitioners remain to do field-based work and to supervise novices. But under contraction, novice hiring is often the first to be cut, producing a fragile “senior-heavy” workforce. Abbott’s workplace assimilation concept implies that without novices in the workplace arena, boundary knowledge and tacit practice cannot be reproduced even if documentation outputs continue. citeturn21search3turn12search2turn15search5

**Sentinel network implications.** In a contracted workforce, the capacity to participate in ongoing taxonomy validation becomes uneven. Public-agency practitioners (core fieldcraft and statecraft holders) may have reduced slack to engage in Delphi processes, while private-sector datacraft practitioners may become overrepresented in validation—biasing the taxonomy toward codifiable competencies and under-documenting tacit, place-based judgment. This is a predictable measurement distortion under austerity.

**Highest-leverage workforce development intervention.** Build refugia through inter-institutional ladders: formal partnerships between state agencies, tribes, universities, and NGOs to host rotational apprenticeships that preserve fieldcraft and statecraft competencies even when federal hiring shrinks.

**Decision points.** The leverage points are (1) whether federal agencies protect a minimum entry cohort each year (even small), (2) whether state/tribal programs are funded to absorb displaced expertise and maintain continuity, and (3) whether AI tools are deployed explicitly to *free time for mentorship* rather than solely to increase throughput. citeturn14search9turn15search5turn12search2

### Scenario D: contracted investment and substitution-first

**Workforce shape (2030–2035).** This is the most hostile scenario: investment contracts and AI is used primarily to replace workers, not augment them. The result is not merely smaller headcount but structural degradation of professional capacity—especially in domains where tacit ecological knowledge, place-based judgment, and community relationship competencies cannot be reconstructed once cohorts disperse.

**Irreversible knowledge loss domains.** The highest-risk domains are those where competence is constituted through repeated situated practice in specific landscapes and institutions: fire leadership and operations, local forest/watershed management judgment, tribal-government relationship practice, and EJ community trust work. GAO’s documentation of persistent recruitment/retention barriers in wildland fire, combined with the strong dependence on workplace qualification ladders, implies that major cohort breaks would cause long-run capacity loss. citeturn15search5turn20search1turn16search11 In EJ, politically contingent staffing and office dismantling risks eroding hard-won institutional knowledge and relationships, which are not substitutable by documentation AI. citeturn17news40turn11search3

**Refugia institutions.** The most plausible refugia for capacity under this scenario are (a) tribal programs exercising self-determination authorities, because their jurisdiction is anchored in sovereignty and local legitimacy; (b) state agencies that maintain statutory responsibilities for water and wildlife; (c) university extension and applied research centers; and (d) private conservation organizations with stable endowments or market-linked finance streams. The DOI and CRS material on self-determination explicitly notes that tribal nations can assume responsibility for federal programs/functions, including natural resource management—i.e., a structural pathway for capacity refugia. citeturn14search9turn14search34turn14search5

**Highest-leverage workforce development intervention.** “Knowledge salvage and continuity”: intensive documentation and apprenticeship capture programs focused on tacit domains, paired with institutional memory preservation (rotational assignments; community-of-practice infrastructures) housed in refugia institutions.

**Decision points.** The decisive points are (1) whether agencies adopt substitution-first automation for entry-level work (closing ladders), (2) whether Congress/state legislatures fund refugia institutions and protect field-based staffing, and (3) whether professional associations standardize minimum human oversight and training requirements to resist full substitution in safety-critical and trust-critical domains. The window is short: once experienced cohorts exit and the ladder is broken, rebuilding requires decades. citeturn21search22turn15search5turn12search0

### Taxonomy Implications

Across scenarios, the taxonomy’s exposure instrument should treat “AI exposure” as **technical overlap × institutional adoption mode × ladder fragility**. The Iceberg Index provides a model for technical overlap, but scenario outcomes depend on whether organizations pursue augmentation or substitution and whether career ladders survive long enough to transmit tacit competence. citeturn12search0turn12search2turn21search22

Scenario analysis also implies that Fieldcraft/Datacraft/Statecraft should be treated not only as competency categories but as **distinct reproduction regimes**: fieldcraft requires place-based practice and mentorship; datacraft is more codifiable and thus more exposed; statecraft is partially codifiable but tied to legitimacy, discretion, and accountability. This suggests that stability tiers should be scenario-sensitive: a competency can be “durable” in the abstract but highly fragile under substitution-first adoption. citeturn12search0turn21search17turn21search22

Finally, the scenario logic indicates likely taxonomy gaps in *AI governance and validation competencies*: “tool oversight,” “model risk management,” “human accountability design,” and “field verification protocols for AI-produced analyses” become core cross-family skills in all scenarios except the most austere collapse, where they become luxury functions. This gap is especially salient because professional jurisdictions increasingly defend themselves by redefining what counts as legitimate human judgment versus automatable routine—an Abbottian jurisdictional shift mechanism. citeturn21search22turn13search3turn12search0

## Cross-layer synthesis and evidence gaps

The central structural contradiction across layers is that environmental management relies heavily on **tacit, situated knowledge** (fieldcraft and relational statecraft) while many of its institutions govern careers through **codified credential signals** (degrees, classification series, templated job descriptions) and increasingly through **codifiable outputs** (documents, reports, dashboards) that are exposed to automation. Abbott’s framework predicts that when disturbances occur—budget shocks, political reorganization, or technology waves—jurisdictional settlements shift toward forms that can be defended through abstraction and audience legitimacy. citeturn21search4turn21search17turn12search0 In environmental management, that predicts a drift toward datacraft and compliance formalism unless explicit counter-institutions protect fieldcraft reproduction.

Three evidence gaps are especially consequential for a forecasting taxonomy. First, **workforce size measurement** is systematically weak for hybrid/proto-jurisdictions (EJ, conservation finance, adaptation), and even where large O*NET categories exist (e.g., Sustainability Specialists), the categories may be too broad to serve as clean environmental management workforce proxies. citeturn19search3turn12search0 Second, **cohort and ladder data** are scarce: outside of fire (where GAO audits explicitly discuss barriers), there is little public evidence that quantifies when and why practitioners exit before reaching mastery stages, even though this is mission-critical for forecasting tacit knowledge supply. citeturn15search5turn15search1 Third, **distributional impacts of workforce shocks** are under-measured: the public record documents agency reductions and EJ layoffs, but credible demographic impact evaluation requires microdata that is not publicly accessible, making it difficult to test hypotheses like “cuts disproportionately hit recently diversified entry cohorts.” citeturn7news42turn17news40turn7news41

A final cross-layer tension is that professionalization can be both protective and exclusionary. ### Taxonomy Implications

The strongest cross-layer implication is that taxonomy maintenance should incorporate **jurisdictional monitoring**: track where credentialing consolidates, where classification systems reclassify work (e.g., competency models), where pay/housing shocks destabilize ladders, and where AI adoption shifts the boundary of what counts as “core professional judgment.” citeturn17search17turn15search14turn12search0turn21search22
